# L1 overview

- **Signal vias changing return planes as the real cavity-noise source** — When a signal via transitions from one layer to another, its return current must jump between two planes; the cavity impedance converts that current into a plane-to-plane voltage wave that couples into every other via in the cavity. This is primarily a signal integrity and EMC issue, not a power integrity one, and the noise is long range: a single 200 ps edge on a 50 Ω line injects about 6 mA and roughly 5 mV per victim via, so ten simultaneous aggressors deliver on the order of 15% of the signal swing.
- **Lower impedance and higher damping from thin dielectric** — Cavity noise is impedance times current, so the design levers are lower characteristic impedance and more attenuation. Dielectric thickness is the single strongest parameter because both cavity impedance and conductor loss scale inversely with h; Dk helps only as a square root and also shifts modal resonances downward, so never trade h for Dk.
- **The 1/6-wavelength shorting-via rule** — Uniformly distributed shorting vias lower loop inductance and push the first parallel resonance up; a 3D-solver fit gives a minimum resonant wavelength of six times the via-to-via spacing. Practically, spacing in inches must be less than 1/f in GHz (three times the rise time in nsec), i.e. at least two shorting vias across the rising edge. This only works when both planes are the same voltage.
- **DC blocking capacitors are a poor substitute** — With dissimilar planes, capacitors must block DC, so their capacitance is nearly irrelevant and their mounting loop inductance governs. Typical 2 nH mounting inductance demands 1/42 wavelength spacing, about seven times tighter than vias and roughly fifty times more parts (thousands per board); even 1 nH needs about 1/20 wavelength. Capacitor count is set by the larger of two criteria: pushing the first resonance above the signal bandwidth, or holding dI/dt plane bounce under 10%.
- **Damping, ESR, and capacitor-value selection** — With few capacitors, the parallel peak is driven by mounted plus spreading inductance against cavity capacitance; when spreading inductance is small, adding capacitors barely changes peak height because the higher resonant frequency and lower parallel ESR cancel out. Controlled-ESR capacitors (0.2-1 Ω) are the effective damping tool, while mixing 1 µF, 0.1 µF, and 0.01 µF values gives essentially identical peaks — low ESL and more parts are what matter.
- **Transmission-line cavity model and design checklist** — A cavity viewed from its center can be approximated by four radial transmission lines fed through spreading inductance, fast enough for any SPICE simulator and accurate for the self-resonant frequency and first modes; a mesh of 64 or more unit cells recovers higher-order modes. The chapter closes with ten rules, the first being: use the same voltage for every return plane so shorting vias become possible.

- `#L11874-L11998` — 7.3: higher-order modes are damped more than lower ones, and thin dielectric simultaneously lowers spreading inductance, lowers cavity impedance, and raises conductor-loss damping; Dk lowers impedance and raises attenuation only as √Dk but shifts resonances down, so never sacrifice h for Dk.
- `#L11999-L12362` — 7.4: shorting vias between same-voltage planes; the first peak is the parallel resonance of n via inductances with cavity capacitance, and the 3D fit gives minimum resonant wavelength = 6 × via spacing, yielding spacing (inches) < 1/f (GHz) or < 3 × rise time (nsec), two vias per rising edge; add one return via minimum per signal via (seven for a coax-like launch), and note differential pairs cancel cavity return current except for skew-driven common mode.
- `#L12363-L12461` — 7.5: DC blocking capacitors act as surrogates for shorting vias, so mounting inductance is the metric and capacitance above roughly 100 nF is irrelevant; at 2 nH they turn inductive above about 11 MHz and behave like vias, but 25 capacitors need mounting inductance below about 50 pH to match via performance.
- `#L12462-L12671` — 7.6: counting rule for blocking capacitors, n_caps = L_mount/L_via + 1, giving spacing that shrinks as the square root; with 2 nH mounting inductance the spacing is 1/42 wavelength (≈150 mils below 1 GHz, ~5,000 parts on a 10 in board), and even 1 nH needs about 1/20 wavelength, so capacitor density scales as roughly 1/(rise time) with examples of 1 per in² at 1 nsec and 10 per in² at 0.3 nsec.

- `#L12808-L12917` — 7.8: with too few capacitors the cavity shows a new low-frequency parallel peak (about 89 MHz in the example) formed by mounted plus spreading inductance against cavity capacitance; below the modal frequencies the whole cavity bounces uniformly, and a clock at that frequency builds the noise to roughly 90 mV peak-to-peak.
- `#L12918-L12991` — 7.9: parallel resonant frequency from effective capacitor inductance and cavity capacitance; the levers are low ESL per capacitor, many capacitors in parallel, and small low-capacitance cavities, with thinner dielectric favored despite higher capacitance because spreading inductance and damping improve, and return-current planes kept continuous.
- `#L12992-L13130` — 7.10: damping via ESR, with ESR ≈ 0.15/C(nF) Ω for an 0402 MLCC (28 mΩ at 100 nF, 3.5 mΩ at 10 µF) and Q = √(L/C)/ESR; when spreading inductance is small the peak height is set by √(L/C)/ESR and is nearly independent of capacitor count, since higher resonance frequency and lower parallel ESR cancel — hence small-value parts with higher ESR damp better.
- `#L13131-L13209` — 7.11: cavity conductor loss adds about one square of sheet resistance per surface (≈2.5 mΩ at 100 MHz for ½ oz copper, ≈5 mΩ total), comparable to a 10 µF capacitor's ESR, so including cavity loss drops that peak from 50 Ω to 12 Ω while leaving high-ESR small capacitors unchanged; interactions become too complex for hand analysis.
- `#L13210-L13326` — 7.12: with a fixed capacitor count, value selection barely moves the parallel peak; 12 × 0.01 µF lowers the 200 MHz peak but creates a large 3 MHz VRM resonance, and any mix of 1/0.1/0.01 µF gives about the same 200 MHz height while merely shifting other peaks, so more low-inductance capacitors is the design principle.
- `#L13327-L13428` — 7.13: controlled-ESR capacitors (1-10 µF with 0.2-1 Ω ESR) cut the parallel peak by 20× or more because Z_peak = √(L/C)/ESR, without affecting modal peaks; their parallel ESR also sets a low-frequency floor, giving a second capacitor-count criterion, n_sig/n_caps = 0.1 × Z0/ESR (5 signals per 1 Ω capacitor at 50 Ω and 10% noise).
- `#L13429-L13497` — 7.14: consolidated design rules — same-voltage return planes shorted with vias spaced under 1/6 wavelength (3 × rise time in inches), otherwise thin dielectric plus DC blocking capacitors with the lowest practical mounting inductance, at least one return via per signal via, capacitor density from the larger of the two criteria, and controlled-ESR parts where affordable.
- `#L13498-L13601` — 7.15: SPICE-compatible cavity model of four radial transmission lines (each half the cavity length, impedance set to match a quarter of the cavity capacitance, delay from Dk and length) fed by about ¾ square of spreading inductance split four ways; it matches the 3D solver for self-resonance and first modes, and a 64-cell mesh is needed for higher-order modes.
- `#L13602-L13640` — 7.16: the bottom line in ten points — cavity noise is an SI/EMC problem, thin dielectric is the best peak-height reduction, same-voltage planes plus shorting vias push resonances up, DC blocking capacitors are poor vias with mounting inductance and then ESR as their key parameters, and suboptimal capacitor counts demand full 3D simulation.
