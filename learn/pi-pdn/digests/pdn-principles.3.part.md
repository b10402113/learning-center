# L1 overview

- **3.1 Why measure low impedance** — PDN design is designing to a target impedance profile from DC to the highest signal bandwidth; targets range from >1 Ω down to <1 mΩ. Measurement verifies both the manufactured part and the simulation flow, and speeds debug. The challenge is resolving milliohm structures above 1 GHz.
- **3.2-3.3 Two equivalent definitions of impedance** — The V/I ratio (drive a sine, measure amplitude and phase of current) is the classical definition but caps out near 100 MHz. The reflection definition uses the reflection coefficient ρ = (Z2−Z1)/(Z2+Z1) at an impedance discontinuity. With a known 50 Ω source, Z2 = Z1(1+ρ)/(1−ρ), giving the total integrated input impedance looking into the DUT including fixture and multiple bounces.
- **3.4 The VNA and S-parameters** — A VNA separates incident from reflected waves in the same coax, unlike a scope that only sees total voltage. Internals: source → 50 Ω series resistor → precision 50 Ω line, with Vref measuring the incident wave and Vtotal measuring incident plus reflected. S11 equals the reflection coefficient and converts to impedance by complex algebra; S21 is the insertion loss. Rule: S-parameters are the de facto standard for high-frequency interconnect behavior.
- **3.5-3.6 Worked one-port examples** — A ceramic DIP pair of leads measured from 10 MHz to 1 GHz looks capacitive (fits a 4.8 pF capacitor) then turns inductive near 800 MHz; log-log plots make ±1 slopes (capacitor/inductor) obvious. A 1-inch copper loop looks inductive below 400 MHz (1/10-wavelength criterion) and its R and L are extracted as R = Re{Z}, L = Im{Z}/ω. Coining "measurement-based modeling"/"hacking the interconnect."
- **3.7 Limits of one-port VNA at low impedance** — Three artifacts dominate below ~0.1 Ω: (1) S/N of the reflected signal (−0.996 vs −1 is −0.035 dB), (2) fixture transmission-line phase delay that inflates extracted L (a fraction of an inch swamps low-inductance DUTs unless de-embedded), and (3) probe contact resistance (0.05 Ω gold-to-gold up to 2 Ω oxidized) plus residual tip loop inductance (~20 pH/mil, so 5 mils of scrub = 100 pH). Microprobe floor is roughly 0.05 Ω and 0.1 nH.
- **3.8-3.9 Kelvin four-wire and the two-port technique** — Contact resistance comes from current constriction at tiny real contact areas. Lord Kelvin's fix: separate current-carrying leads from voltage-sensing leads so the high-impedance voltmeter ignores contact resistance. Extended to RF as the two-port VNA method: port 1 drives the current loop, port 2 senses the voltage, giving Z = 25 Ω × S21 for low impedances (S21[dB] → Z = 25 × 10^(S21/20)), with the exact form Z = 25(S21)/(1−S21) valid at any impedance. With high power, averaging, and a narrow IF filter, a −90 dB S21 floor reaches sub-milliohm impedances past 1 GHz.

# L2 detail

- `#L2663-L2688` — Why measure low impedance: target impedance profile spans DC to signal bandwidth and 1 Ω to 1 mΩ; measurement validates parts, simulation accuracy, and the whole PDN ecology, and drives debug; the hard part is 1 mΩ up past 1 GHz.
- `#L2689-L2729` — V/I definition: drive a sine across the DUT, measure current amplitude and phase, Z(f) = V(f)/I(f); the basis of low-end impedance analyzers, limited to roughly 100 MHz because direct current measurement gets impractical above that.
- `#L2730-L2780` — Reflection basis: signals are simultaneously a propagating voltage wave and a current loop with direction of propagation and circulation; any change in instantaneous impedance creates a reflected wave and distorts the transmitted wave.
- `#L2781-L2893` — Reflection coefficient ρ from boundary conditions, Z2 = Z1(1+ρ)/(1−ρ), and with Z1 = 50 Ω the input impedance is extracted from ρ; for extended DUTs the measured ρ is the sum of all reflections and bounces, i.e. the total integrated input impedance (a VNA performs this).
- `#L2894-L3036` — VNA architecture: "vector" means phase is measured; incident and reflected waves are separated, ports are calibrated 50 Ω to the coax end, unlike a scope that only sees total voltage; S-parameter definition Sij, S11 = return loss, |S21| = insertion loss, and ZDUT = 50(1+S11)/(1−S11) with no model assumptions.
- `#L3037-L3134` — Ceramic DIP example: two adjacent leads soldered to an SMA, S11 swept 10 MHz–1 GHz, converted frequency-by-frequency to ZDUT; |S11| ≈ 0 dB means all information is in phase, and S11 phase ≠ impedance phase; log-log plot shows capacitive then inductive behavior, fitted by a 4.8 pF capacitor — "measurement-based modeling" or "hacking the interconnect."
- `#L3135-L3219` — Wire-loop example: 1-inch copper loop measured one-port; Bogatin's Rule #9 (anticipate the result first); 1/10-wavelength sets ~400 MHz for a 3-inch circumference; Z = R + jωL with R = Re{Z} and L = Im{Z}/ω; above 400 MHz extracted L rises from distributed effects, raising questions about fixture inductance, skin depth, and noise below 0.1 Ω.
- `#L3220-L3338` — One-port limits: S11 is −0.996 at 0.1 Ω (−0.035 dB) and collapses below that; fixture transmission-line phase adds artifacts that inflate extracted L with length (0 to 1 inch in 0.25-inch steps on a 0.01 Ω/1 nH DUT); probe contact resistance spans 0.05 Ω to 2 Ω and tip scrub adds ~20 pH/mil of residual loop inductance; practical floor ≈0.05 Ω and 0.1 nH, limiting C to <0.01 µF and L to >0.1 nH — insufficient for on-die capacitance and via inductance.
- `#L3339-L3420` — Kelvin four-wire: contact resistance is constriction/spreading resistance at the tiny true contact area (≈20 mΩ gold-to-gold at 100 g, >100 mΩ with oxide, up to 2 Ω on oxidized solder/copper); two-wire measurement includes it; Lord Kelvin (William Thomson) separated current and voltage leads so the high-impedance voltmeter ignores contact resistance — the basis for both DC and RF low-impedance measurement.
- `#L3421-L3651` — Two-port technique: port 1 drives the current loop, port 2 senses the voltage across the DUT on independent contacts; with a near-−1 reflection the net DUT current is twice the incident current, so S21 ≈ ZDUT/25 Ω and ZDUT = 25 Ω × S21 (dB form Z = 25 × 10^(S21/20), e.g. −40 dB → 0.25 Ω, −60 dB → 25 mΩ); impedance in dB behaves as an amplitude so use 20log (dBΩ, 0 dB = 1 Ω, 28 dB = 20log 25); the exact form Z = 25·S21/(1−S21) holds at any impedance while the approximation needs S21 < ~−20 dB; S21 loses accuracy for very large impedances; with high power, averaging, and a narrow filter a −90 dB S21 floor yields sub-milliohm resolution past 1 GHz.
