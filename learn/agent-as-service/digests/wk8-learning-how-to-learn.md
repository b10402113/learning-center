# L1 overview

- Learning how to learn matters most in AI because the space churns constantly — new tools every week, new models every month — driven by "AI building on AI": the labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) use their own tools at the frontier, which accelerates the tools themselves. The counterweight is separating what's evergreen from what's not.
- Evergreen: the service-delivery business model itself, standardized since the Mad Men-era ad agencies — discovery call → proposal/offer → deliver → expand via referrals, testimonials, case studies. It worked for consultants 30 years ago and will keep working; trust compounds regardless of tools, and clients will always have problems and pay for solutions. What's not evergreen: specific tools and models (GPT-4 went from impressive to outdated in months) — expect continual churn, and when building products, design for the model that will exist six months from now.
- Model choice barely matters: Gemini, Claude, and GPT are within ±5% on niche topics; what matters is mastery of your chosen tool for your specific application. Invest in AI-proof, stacking skills — discovery, delivery, building relationships — and stay current on tools without obsessing.
- Jay's own learning system, reproduced with prompts: an OpenClaw assistant morning briefing that scours the web, X/Twitter, and YouTube for AI news (including auto-extracted transcripts from select channels like Nate Jones and Matt, which only Gemini can do natively among chat LLMs); a daily-learning skill that recommends 3 books and digests one per day (example: "Working in Public" on open-source maintenance, applied to his OpenClaw content plans); and a Chrome extension adding a Gemini button to any YouTube video that copies a summary prompt to the clipboard. Core rules: be intentional about your attention diet, and always build more than you consume — the best practitioners build the most, not read the most.

# L2 detail

- `#L1-L120` — Module scope: learning how to learn, severely important in the AI space because AI moves fast but not everything changes; the plan is to identify what is evergreen (persists even when AI changes), what is not, and how to stay current without drowning in information overload. The churn is structural: new tools every week, new models every month, because tool-makers like OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google are themselves the frontier users of these tools — literally AI building on AI — so you can get swept away feeling left behind.
- `#L122-L248` — What's evergreen: the whole course's business model of offering services to clients is evergreen because service delivery is a solved business problem, standardized since Mad Men-era ad agencies; the delivery cycle doesn't change whether you offer AI agents, AI automations via n8n ("NIN" in transcript), agentic workflows via Claude Code and Antigravity, or soon autonomous agents from OpenClaw-type setups — there is always a discovery call, a proposal (your offer), delivery, and expansion through referrals, testimonials, and case studies. It worked for consultants 30 years ago, works today, and will work in 10 years as long as organizations have problems needing experts.
- `#L250-L332` — Trust compounds and the fundamental exchange: it doesn't matter whether you use Claude Code, n8n, or anything else — clients care about delivered results and positive impact on their team or business, which builds trust and reputation. Clients will always have problems, AI is one of the best ways to answer them, and the exchange of client demand for your supplied expertise will never change.
- `#L334-L476` — What is not evergreen: specific tools and models — GPT-4 was impressive a few months ago and is now outdated; expect continual churn. Building advice: when designing solutions, especially products or SaaS, sometimes it's better to think of the model available six months from now — if Claude's Opus 4.6 ("Atropic" in transcript) gets your use case right only 2 out of 10 times today, the newer version will likely one-shot it in six months, because the models are fixing and improving themselves. Plan around that cycle.
- `#L478-L588` — Investment strategy: always invest in evergreen, in skills that stack — get good at discovery, delivery, and building relationships; these are AI-proof because people will always build and solve things for other people. Stay current on tools but don't obsess: know what's possible, don't master every new thing. Gemini vs. Claude vs. GPT differ by roughly ±5% on niche topics, so the model you pick matters less than your mastery of it for your specific application. Therefore implement an intentional learning system instead of mindlessly scrolling YouTube or TikTok.
- `#L590-L740` — Jay's personal learning system: (1) a morning briefing with his OpenClaw ("open-class" in transcript) assistant that scours the web, X/Twitter, and YouTube for the latest AI news so he can triage what's worth diving into; he can ask for a deep-dive on a topic (example: Netflix AI film tech) and get the article grabbed, summarized, plus input on how it applies to his own business. Chat LLMs like ChatGPT and Claude now have built-in memory so the prompt below works with any of them, but OpenClaw is more flexible: he scheduled it to extract transcripts from select YouTube channels he follows (Nate Jones; Matt) and summarize instead of watching 20-minute videos — natively only Gemini can do this because Google owns YouTube transcripts. (2) A daily learning skill: during downtime he asks for 3 books it thinks he'll like, picks one for a digest, and has the rest logged as read — enabling "a book a day"; example digest of "Working in Public" (making and maintenance of open-source software, relevant to his OpenClaw use) returned implications like "you're about to build a content empire around an open source project… the maintainers' attention is finite… don't just teach a tool, teach your community how to be good contributors to the ecosystem" — an angle passive content consumption wouldn't surface.
- `#L742-L813` — Third tool and closing principles: a Chrome extension (linked below) adding a Gemini button to any YouTube video that copies a summary prompt to the clipboard, saving him from sitting through 20-minute videos when he only needs a snippet. Key takeaways: be intentional about your attention diet and what you let in; always produce more than you consume, because learning happens in the work — watching videos and reading news all day isn't learning, since relearning happens from doing; the best practitioners in any space are the ones who build the most, not read the most. Final advice: master the evergreen, stay curious about tools. Next module: what's next — your path forward after the course.
