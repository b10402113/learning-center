# L1 overview

- Jay argues why AI agents specifically (not basic chat AI) are the real business opportunity: chat is commoditized table stakes, while agents take action, work autonomously, and integrate into real business workflows — where the value is.
- Historical parallel: the MSP (managed service provider) wave of the 80s/90s, now a $380B market (Accenture, TCS, Capgemini); businesses still need someone to set up, maintain, update, and fix agents — every business with knowledge work is becoming an AI business.
- Proof points: ~half of Y Combinator's latest batch are AI agent companies, Andrew Wilkinson (Tiny) is publicly hiring agent builders, AI-as-a-Service projected to grow from $20B to $91B by 2030. Four things that won't commoditize: integration, domain expertise, reliability, taste.
- Action for the week: craft a one-liner — "I'm working on RoboLabs, an AI solutions practice that helps brands and content businesses use AI in meaningful ways" — built from name + practice name + what you are + who you help, sayable in five seconds.

# L2 detail

- `#L1-L152` — Framing and why agents: you already use AI daily; the question is turning that into income by offering it as a service. Basic AI (ChatGPT-style chat, image generation, essays) is easily commoditized table stakes. Agents are different: they take action, work autonomously, and integrate into real business workflows — that's where value lives. The clear gap: businesses struggle to implement AI agents on their own, and the service sector stays valuable even as tooling improves.
- `#L153-L281` — The MSP parallel: history doesn't repeat but it rhymes. In the 80s/90s managed service providers emerged because companies lacked expertise to manage computers and hired external providers to set up, run, and fix systems. That market is now worth $380 billion (Accenture, TCS, Capgemini and other big players). Even now that computers are easy to set up, those firms moved up the stack to cloud storage, data cataloging, data governance. The same applies to AI agents today: businesses need someone to set up agents, maintain them over time, update them when models change ("every month we get a new model"), and someone to call when things break.
- `#L282-L426` — Scope across industries: this is not just for tech companies — every business where knowledge/intelligence work happens is becoming an AI business, echoing the big-data wave. SMBs through enterprises will undergo significant transformation. Examples: law firms use agents for document review and client intake; real estate gets agents to qualify leads and schedule viewings; creative production pipelines are disrupted because agents can build videos end to end. Takeaway: every industry needs help from people like you.
- `#L427-L489` — Proof points: nearly half of Y Combinator's latest startup batches are AI agent companies; billionaire Andrew Wilkinson (founder of Tiny) is publicly hiring experts to build AI agents instead of learning it himself; AI-as-a-Service market projected to scale from $20B to $91B by 2030. Then raises the learner's objection: if AI keeps improving, won't this work be commoditized anyway?
- `#L490-L604` — Answer part 1 (integration): focus on what only humans stay valuable at for the foreseeable future. Integration — guiding agents to connect to legacy systems; models are trained on broad data but will miss niche use cases and custom systems, businesses get stuck there, and someone must direct the agents and communicate to the client what is being integrated. Answer part 2 (domain expertise): much of what models know is text/image/video; edge cases and practical experience in your specific domain can't be captured by the models yet.
- `#L605-L766` — Answer part 3 (reliability): being the person responsible when something breaks at 2 a.m.; businesses pay for peace of mind — someone on the hook, someone to call. Accountability must be a human being, not an AI agent or a piece of technology. Answer part 4 (taste): AI can make things work end to end but can't tell you what separates good from bad when it isn't codifiable (what makes a great ad vs a mediocre one); we create for humans, so judging what resonates requires your taste and foresight. Caveat: recommendation algorithms already learn preferences, so AI may understand taste eventually — but for now it's a clear advantage.
- `#L767-L950` — The action: craft a one-liner. It seems trite but it's the starting point of your identity and alignment; you need it at events, on calls, in DMs, with friends. Jay's example: "I'm working on RoboLabs, which is an AI solutions practice that helps brands and content businesses use AI in meaningful ways." Breakdown: your name, the practice name, who you are (AI solutions practice), who you help. Specific, clean, five seconds to say. The word choices are deliberate and center everything the practice does. Questions: how do you talk about what you do, what is your practice; naming comes in a later module — focus on positioning now.
- `#L951-L1193` — Positioning recommendations: if unsure, start with "AI solutions practice" — broad, highly professional, and captures what businesses pay for (solutions to problems); it also covers automation and operations work beyond creative. Specializations: "AI automation agency" = in the business of less (less effort, less time, faster results); "AI creative agency" = in the business of more (more content, assets, ads, leads, customers). You can change it anytime. Caution on the word "consulting": region-dependent negative connotation — implies strategy, opinions, PowerPoint without actually solving. Outcome of this week: a clear one-liner plus a sense of identity to attach to yourself and the practice once you announce it. Next: make it tangible — pick a name, secure a domain/website, create a logo.
