# L1 overview

- Jay's Wk4 module 2: how to price AI-agent services and three closing techniques (anchoring, guarantees, scarcity); the common failure modes are undercharging out of insecurity or overthinking and never quoting.
- Core principle: price for the value you deliver, not the time it takes — an automation you can spin up in an hour but that saves the client 20 hours/week is priced on the 20 hours; templates and expertise make your build time irrelevant.
- Don't guess rates: use an AI research prompt (feed it company, industry, the work, and estimated revenue/spending power) in ChatGPT/Gemini/Claude to benchmark rates; adjust for client spending power — a $100,000 bill is a rounding error to Unilever or P&G (approvable by low-level managers) but real money to a local SMB.
- Closing techniques: anchor high first (show $10,000 crossed out, then the $5,000 you actually want — frames a "special rate" and gives future raise leverage); guarantees (outcome-based refund promises or commission-based pay-per-result) put skin in the game and win your first credentials; scarcity (capacity-based, e.g. "I only take three clients at a time") is honest urgency; when a client balks at price, don't just discount — shrink scope into a small paid pilot (e.g. a two-week workflow setup) and expand after it proves value; frame any price against the alternative (a full-time hire at $80,000/year vs a one-time $5,000 build plus $500/month tuning retainer) to make the math obvious.

# L2 detail

- `#L1-L120` — Module framing and the pricing pain point: covers pricing frameworks, rate research, and closing techniques (anchoring, guarantees, scarcity); the two common mistakes — undercharging because you don't feel qualified on your first pitch, or overthinking and never quoting at all.
- `#L121-L300` — Price for value, not time: if you've built an automation before you can spin it up for a new client in an hour, but that doesn't mean charging for an hour — value is what matters; an automation saving 20 hours per week is worth a lot to a real business regardless of build time; building a practice means templates and prior expertise "exponentialize" the value, so focus on what the result is worth to the client's business.
- `#L301-L530` — Rate research with AI: never guess — use the template prompt in ChatGPT/Gemini/Claude, describing the company, industry, the work you're doing, and ideally an estimate of annual revenue or spending power; the demo shows ChatGPT researching and returning suggested hourly rates and tiering for the specific market and client; rates vary by your location and the client's, which is why AI research beats guessing; always adjust for the client's spending power — a startup and an enterprise may get the same deliverable but have very different budgets; this is also why scaling up means chasing bigger clients: a $100,000 bill is minuscule for multinationals like Unilever or P&G — literally a rounding error, approvable by even low-level managers (he works with such companies) — whereas local SMBs, where you likely start, have much smaller marketing/consulting budgets.
- `#L531-L680` — Closing technique 1, anchoring: present a high price first — if AI research says $5,000, don't open with it; show a $10,000 price crossed out, then the $5,000 final; two reasons it works: (1) you can frame it as a special rate ("maybe because you like them, maybe because it's your first time working with them") so they feel they're getting a deal — which with you, they are; (2) future leverage — on later projects you can raise prices because "technically you were always supposed to charge this higher amount anyway"; the first engagement is positioned as a discounted trial; illustrated with a real proposal: the final slide shows the agent being built and its value prop for anyone on the client team, then per-BIT-phase pricing where the original quote of $4,000 for build+integrate was an anchor play and the real special rate was $1,300 for this small engagement (one agent of a larger set); the breakdown shows deliverables per phase and makes the rate feel tailored; also explains the milestone-1 handoff assurance — if the client cuts the engagement after phase 1 they keep everything built (demo, tools, templates), with integration remaining their own work.
- `#L681-L840` — Closing technique 2, guarantees: taking the risk off the client — very powerful when you're starting out without credentials; type 1, outcome-based guarantee: "if this doesn't save you X hours per week within 30 days, then I'll refund you"; it favors you because you get the project and the credential — professionals have taken free work just to add a big-name brand to their credentials; adjust based on your confidence; type 2, commission-based pricing: your payment is a commission per sale/lead you generate, so the client only pays when they get results — a "no-brainer" yes; both work because you're "putting your money where your mouth is," which builds trust fast, and the won projects feed your funnel and website for future engagements.
- `#L841-L1010` — Closing technique 3, scarcity: the offer feels available only for a limited time or a limited number of people; the beginner-friendly version is capacity-based scarcity — a small team (even just you, balancing family or a 9-5) genuinely can only take, say, three clients at a time, so communicate that limit; it's "not untruthful… a real fact" positioned in your favor; frame it as ensuring each client gets proper attention — no need to mention family or your day job; it works because it's not fake urgency, everyone's capacity is genuinely limited.
- `#L1011-L1193` — Starting small and price framing: if a client balks at the price in the tailor phase, don't just lower it — lower the output too and package a small paid pilot (e.g. a two-week engagement setting up one workflow for less money), then expand if it works; lower commitment makes yes easier, and once the pilot proves value, expanding is a no-brainer; final framing move: position against the most expensive alternative — "you could hire a full-time person for $80,000 a year, or this agent and automation can handle it for a one-time $5,000 fee plus a $500-a-month tuning retainer"; this shifts the conversation from absolutes ($5,000 feels big to a beginner too) to savings; other comparison anchors: software subscriptions they already pay, the manual time cost, and the cost of not future-proofing against AI-driven change — real risks businesses pay to counter; recap: price for value, use AI to research rates, anchor high, offer a guarantee, use real scarcity; next module: contracts, invoices, and the business side.
