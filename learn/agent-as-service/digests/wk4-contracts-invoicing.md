# L1 overview

- Jay's Wk4 module 3: the business essentials — contracts, invoices, business structure — with the goal "protect yourself and look professional"; rule one: every client engagement needs a written contract covering scope, payment amount, and deadlines.
- Why contracts matter in an emerging field: clients don't know what to expect from AI agents, so undefined scope invites scope creep and infinite revisions; contracts set boundaries on what is and isn't included, protecting both sides and keeping extra projects separate.
- Business structure: a sole proprietor can talk to clients and close deals without incorporating in most jurisdictions (income reported on personal taxes); later, incorporate for liability protection and professionalism (LLC in the US, PTY Limited in Australia); "don't let it block you — formalize later."
- The simple contract covers six things (scope, timeline, payment terms, revisions, IP ownership, termination); invoices list both parties, the work description, amount, due date, and payment method (Wave is free; Stripe/PayPal have built-in invoicing); the 50% upfront / 50% on completion standard ties naturally to BIT phases; a written email agreement ("a meeting of the minds") beats waiting for the perfect contract — done beats perfect when starting.

# L2 detail

- `#L1-L120` — Module framing and why written contracts are non-negotiable: goal is protecting yourself and looking professional; the first rule of offering AI agents as a service is a written contract outlining scope of work, payment, and deadlines; the sector is emerging so clients don't know what to expect — without a written scope they may change the scope mid-engagement (scope creep), forcing infinite revisions with no expectations on revision counts or what triggers the next payment; the worst case is delivering the service and having the client refuse to pay because they changed their mind about what AI output "should" be.
- `#L121-L200` — Two more contract reasons and business structure: revisions spiral if you don't set boundaries — countless engagements devolved this way for lack of a written contract; contracts set boundaries on what is and isn't included, so future extra projects are clearly separate and scoped separately; on legality, a sole practitioner can start talking to clients and even close deals without incorporating or forming an LLC in most jurisdictions — work, get paid, report on personal taxes; the resources prompt helps research your locality; later you "level up" — LLC (US), PTY Limited Company (Australia, where he is) — for liability protection (liability limited to the business's amount if things go haywire) and a more professional look; the key message: don't let legal structure block you, formalize later, just start.
- `#L201-L440` — The contract itself: every engagement needs a contract every time, and contrary to popular belief it can be simple — no legal jargon, no logo header required, just clear and complete; the six things to include: (1) scope — what exactly you're delivering; (2) timeline — when you'll deliver it; (3) payment terms — how much, by when, and how; (4) revisions — how many rounds of changes are included; (5) IP ownership — who owns the work when it's done; (6) termination — how either party can end the agreement; a template below can be fed to your LLM for refinement; simplicity wins, and any contract beats starting work with none; sealing the deal means getting it signed with DocuSign or PandaDoc so everything is documented before work starts.
- `#L441-L560` — Invoicing: after a BIT phase (e.g. phase 1 build), the client pays for that phase before you move to integrate — send an invoice, "here's what I did, here's what you owe, and here's how to pay"; keep it simple: your business name and contact info, client's name and contact info, description of the work, amount due, payment due date, method of payment; software: Wave is free and works great, Stripe and PayPal have built-in invoicing, and honestly a PDF works fine; payment terms industry standard is 50% upfront / 50% on completion, which protects you from doing work and not getting paid; a PayPal-template sample invoice shows both parties' names, due dates, amount due, and the delivered item; platforms like PayPal or Wave auto-generate these on the fly.
- `#L561-L805` — AI-agents-as-a-service specifics and the minimum viable close: with the BIT framework you charge and set expectations per phase — the real proposal outlines a 50% down payment for phase 1, work begins, and at milestone 1 the rest of the build payment is sent before phase 2, which follows the same structure; the initial proposal only discusses phase 1 because that's the only concern at this stage; Tune appears as a footer since it's the retainer, scoped separately depending on build complexity — you can't assess tuning difficulty until you've built the agent, and legacy systems mean harder upkeep so you bid higher; technical clients managing their own systems may skip phase 3 entirely (build, operate, transfer); real example: a Ralph Lauren dashboard project where integration was much harder because of legacy databases (old Oracle instead of modern Postgres SQL), so phase the work and set expectations on pricing as complexity becomes clear; final caveat: the minimum viable approach — for a first client without a perfect template, write the basics in an email and get their agreement, formalize later; a contract is legally "a meeting of the minds," so an email thread stating the scope plus their yes is enough to start; done beats perfect, especially when starting.
