# L1 overview

- **Ch8 intro + §8.1 — the PDN ecology and the frequency-domain view** — The PDN is every interconnect from VRM to on-die power rails; six elements each dominate one frequency band, and the elements that matter most are the *peaks* at the boundaries between bands, not the minima inside them. The six: on-die capacitance (ODC); package PDN including on-package decoupling (OPD); board power/ground planes (cavity); board MLCCs; bulk caps tied to the VRM; the VRM plus its immediate bulk caps. Figure 8.1 shows the ecology and the die-pad impedance profile. Central rule: optimizing elements one at a time is the least effective strategy — the ODC–package interaction makes the largest peak, the VRM–bulk interaction sets supply stability, and plane + MLCC interactions with package and ODC can break an otherwise robust PDN. Hence MLCC-selection rules quoted without the rest of the system are meaningless.

- **Ch8 §8.2 — on-die decoupling capacitance (ODC), the GHz-only lever** — ODC comes from five sources (Figure 8.2): power/ground metallization grid, distributed gate capacitance of p/n CMOS, on-die signal load capacitance driven by the on transistor, diffusion junctions, and intentionally added decoupling. Above the low-GHz point ODC plus its ESR is the *only* thing holding die-rail impedance down; nothing at board level can compensate for too little ODC. Sizing rule from charge conservation with q = CV and a 10% droop: Vdd1/Vdd0 = CODC/(CODC + Cload) = 0.9, so CODC ≈ 9·Cload; the "switching factor" Cload/CODC sets the edge droop; energy in Cload is ½CV², an RC charge/discharge dissipates equal energy in the resistor, so at clock frequency f the average power is 2 × ½CloadVdd²f, giving Cload = ⟨power⟩/(Vdd²·Fclock) and CODC ≈ 10 × Cload for 10% droop. Worked number: 1 W, 1 V, 1 GHz → CODC ≈ 10 nF; scaling gives ~100 nF for 10 W and up to 1000 nF on high-power dies. Rule of thumb: ~10 nF minimum per watt of rail power. Typical 90 nm CMOS decoupling density ≈ 1 nF/mm² (MOS or MIM), so accurate extraction buys die area. Measurement (Ch3/§3.14): probe package balls with a VNA, use S21 → Z21, convert by Z = 1/jωC; ODC is strongly bias-dependent — Figure 8.4 shows ~100 nF unbiased (metallization + diffusion only) rising to ~450 nF when the chip is powered (gates and interconnect now connected through gate outputs). Density trend: ~1.2× per node in the 2000s, ~1.7× per node in the 2010s, expected to continue at the 10 nm FinFET node, with most capacitance now coming from wiring channels (Figure 8.5). ESR scales inversely with C, and the R·C product has held at ≈250 ps for processor cores (IO blocks differ) and is trending down; consequently ESR ≈ 250 ps/CODC — 50 nF → 5 mΩ, 200 nF → 1.2 mΩ. Measure ESR on small die areas and scale up by the RC constant; on large die areas horizontal power-bus resistance, not vertical bump-to-cap ESR, limits the effective ODC radius. Horizontal RC sets relaxation time: several hundred ps for a few-mm² square die, >1 ns for long narrow buses — above the clock period, the die is no longer a lumped node and needs 3D modeling (Figure 3.42, §3.14). Vertical ESL of the on-die bus is ~0.1 pH, so R dominates jωL to tens of GHz. Reference profile (Figure 8.6): 5 W, 1 GHz, CODC = 50 nF, ESR = 5 mΩ, ESL = 0.1 pH — ESR visible above 400 MHz, ESL only above 10 GHz.

- **Ch8 §8.3 — the package PDN: a lumped inductor below ~600 MHz** — Package PDN = die-to-substrate wire bonds/solder balls, die pads to internal package planes, on-package decoupling (OPD), package-to-board balls/leads, and the board vias from top pads into the plane cavity (package fixes via *location*, board stackup fixes via *length*). Figure 8.7 contrasts a leaded package and a multilayer BGA. Without OPD the package looks purely inductive until either the first modal cavity resonance or the point where total path length exceeds λ/10. Square cavity first resonance (half-wave between ends): fres ≈ 0.59/(Len·√Dk) GHz with Len in inches and Dk ≈ 4; the λ/10 condition gives the upper bound on lumped behavior. For a 1-inch BGA that puts the lumped-inductor approximation valid below ~600 MHz. Equivalent loop inductance spans an enormous range — a few pH for a large multilayer BGA with hundreds of power/ground balls and multiple plane pairs, up to ~10 nH for a QFP with 0.5-inch leads and only two power/ground pairs: a 10,000× spread, so generalization is unsafe and the vendor's package model is essential. Estimation: Lloop = Lper-len·Len/npairs with Lper-len ≈ 20 pH/mil; assumed valid when plane loop inductance is small. Examples: BGA, 50 mil path (PCB via + ball + trace + C4), 100 pairs → ~10 pH, and with 1 mil planes (sheet L = 32 pH/mil × 1 mil = 32 pH, ~1 square spreading ≈ 32 pH) the plane term may or may not matter; CSP, 250 mil, 20 pairs → ~250 pH. For better accuracy measure or 3D-simulate, bus all power/ground ports in parallel, short one end, and extract L from S11(f). Reverse-engineered path-count estimate: npairs_min ≈ ⟨power⟩/(Vdd·Imax_pair), with IPC ~2 A for a 10 mil via and ~1 A (conservative 0.25 A) for a 1 mil gold wire bond; substituting into the loop formula makes Lloop scale with Lper-len and Len, rise with Imax_pair, and fall with ⟨power⟩. Worked: small leaded, 250 mil, 20 pH/mil, 1 V, 0.25 A, 5 W → ~500 pH; large BGA, 100 mil, 25 W → ~20 pH. Series resistance: via + ball ≈ 10 mΩ each, ½ oz copper = 1 mΩ/square, ~10 squares of trace ≈ 10 mΩ, so one pair ≈ 20 mΩ per leg / 40 mΩ total; 20 pairs in parallel ≈ 2 mΩ, small next to the 5 mΩ on-die ESR of 50 nF. Figure 8.8: package-lead profile with board shorted, Lloop = 250 pH, ESR = 5 mΩ, CODC = 50 nF (Keysight ADS).

- **Ch8 §8.4 — the Bandini Mountain: ODC ∥ package L is the dominant peak** — In the best case (board = dead short) the die pads see ODC in parallel with package loop inductance — a parallel resonance and the single most important feature of the PDN ecology. Reference case (Figure 8.9, ADS): CODC = 50 nF, ESR = 5 mΩ, Lloop = 250 pH → peak at ~45 MHz. Peak height is set by the q-factor, i.e. the ESR of both elements; package-lead ESR plus on-die ESR normally leaves q high, with peaks approaching 1 Ω. f = 1/(2π√(LC)): 50 nF and 250 pH → 45 MHz. Named by Steve Weir after the 100-foot Bandini Fertilizer manure pile built for the 1984 LA Olympics commercials (references [5]–[7] are his DesignCon foundations). It exists in *every* device; OPD can lower it by cutting loop inductance and adding damping but never removes it. Figure 8.10: measured test chip showing the peak at 33 MHz; Figure 8.11: 600 mV peak-to-peak die-rail noise when transient current's first harmonic lands on that 33 MHz peak — far beyond any noise budget (details in Ch10). "Power virus" microcode toggling many gates at the Bandini frequency is why vendors withhold ODC and package-L data. The two dominant knobs — raise ODC, lower package L — are both controlled by the semiconductor vendor; Figure 8.12 sweeps four ODC/L combinations with ESR scaling inversely with ODC. Crucially, this peak is *not* the plane-cavity peak of Ch6/Ch7: cavity peaks are measured probing the planes, the Bandini peak by probing the on-die capacitance itself. Above the Bandini frequency ODC owns the die-pad impedance, and package + board inductance together with ODC filter out any board cavity resonance — the frequency at which SI and PI problems separate.

- **Ch8 §8.5 — estimating the Bandini frequency without vendor data** — Exact form when data exists: f = 1/(2π√(Lloop·CODC)); 50 nF and 0.25 nH → 45 MHz. Otherwise combine the two reverse-engineered estimates — CODC ≈ 10·⟨power⟩/(Vdd²·Fclock) nF and Lloop ≈ Lper-len·Len·Vdd·Imax_pair/⟨power⟩ (Lper-len ≈ 20 nH/inch) — into f_Bandini; the average power cancels, because more power means both more ODC and more parallel leads (less L), and f depends on the C·L product. Bounds: QFP at 250 MHz, 1 V, Imax_pair = 0.5 A, 20 nH/in, 0.5 in → ~10 MHz; high-performance package, 50 mil path, 3 GHz, 0.5 A → ~100 MHz; the practical range is ~10–100 MHz, up to ~200 MHz for high-end communications/memory parts. OPD practice: engineer the lowest possible die-pad-to-OPD loop inductance, size OPD ≥ 5× ODC, and set OPD ESR near the target impedance for damping; the OPD then cuts (not eliminates) the peak and relieves board high-frequency capacitor count. Aligning OPD series resonance exactly on the Bandini peak is a stated philosophy but rarely done because of tolerances and available values. Planning default when data is missing: assume 10–50 MHz. Why vendors withhold: PI engineers know the fix (more ODC, less package L) is "outrageously expensive," so publishing the parameters invites demands that would price the product out of the market.

- **Ch8 §8.6 — intrinsic damping: Z0, q, and peak impedance of the peak** — Four metrics describe the parallel resonance: resonant frequency, characteristic impedance, peak impedance, q-factor. Characteristic impedance Z0 = √(L/C) (the impedance where ωL and 1/ωC cross) — with L = 0.25 nH, C = 50 nF → √(0.25e-9/50e-9) = 71 mΩ; it is the lowest impedance achievable under best (critically damped) conditions and, per Ch9, also sets the step-current voltage drop. Warning: transmission-line Z0 and parallel-LC Z0 are not the same physical property, only both "characterizing" impedances. With damping only from on-die ESR (board assumed lossless, so this is worst case), the 250 ps RC rule gives ESR = 250 ps/CODC, q = Z0/ESR, and q ≈ 2πf·250 ps; at 45 MHz q ≈ 71 mΩ/5 mΩ ≈ 14, i.e. fmax_limit = 200 MHz is where q falls below ~3 and board-level damping stops mattering. Peak impedance Zpeak = q·Z0 = 2πf·L = (2π·250 ps)·L — independent of CODC, set only by package loop inductance. So 250 pH → 0.4 Ω-scale peaks, 0.025 nH → 0.1 Ω, and Zpeak < 10 mΩ requires Lloop ≈ 2.5 pH. This matches Figure 8.12: two cases with the same ESL had identical peaks despite a 10× difference in ODC. Three design knobs: lower package L, raise ODC, add ESR in the package loop or on-die cap. Higher Bandini frequencies are inherently better (lower q), so lower package inductance is doubly valuable; below 200 MHz the board's resistive features become real levers.

- **Ch8 §8.7 — cavity with multiple via-pair contacts: only 3D full-wave is exact** — Package balls land on board vias that reach buried planes; the cavity impedance seen from a via pair requires a 3D full-wave solver accounting for plane shape, features, and observation point. Solver output is an S-parameter file whose ports are the observation points — effectively a behavioral equivalent circuit. One-port extraction: Z11 = 50·(1 + S11)/(1 − S11) with both complex and frequency-dependent; Z11 is the "self" impedance. Impedance is preferred over S-parameters for interpretation because the useful information in S is largely in phase, while log-scale Z makes features readable. Example (Figure 8.13): 5 in × 5 in, 10 mil FR4 cavity probed at center — capacitive at low frequency, a self-resonance dip, then modal peaks; Mentor HyperLynx PI. With multiple package via contacts the model is multi-port; bus all ports in parallel in a circuit simulator to get the equivalent impedance a BGA sees. At low frequency 1 or 10 ports see the same cavity capacitance; once inductance matters, contact geometry sets spreading inductance. One via pair is dominated by via loop inductance; many parallel pairs dilute via inductance down to the cavity's spreading inductance, shifting the self-resonant dip *up* (same C, less L, series-LC behavior from Ch6). Modal resonant frequencies do **not** move with added vias: modes are multiples of a half wavelength set by cavity size and wave velocity, and a via barely perturbs time of flight. Figure 8.14: one center port vs four parallel ports plus the bussing circuit — same modal frequencies, lower spreading/BGA-via inductance. Adding a VRM modeled as series R = 1 mΩ, L = 10 nH across the cavity (Figure 8.15; cavity 5 in × 5 in, 10 mil, Dk = 4.3) gives the composite cavity-plus-VRM profile.

- **Ch8 §8.8 — looking from the die through the package into the cavity: the cavity is transparent** — Adding die and package to the VRM-plus-cavity model shows board cavity modal resonances above 1 GHz are huge for SI/EMI but invisible at the die pads and irrelevant to on-die power quality. The die sees the board through the package loop inductance, which rises with frequency, in series with ODC, which rises at the Bandini frequency — so higher-frequency cavity peaks are filtered and cannot drive on-die noise. Three cases (Figure 8.16): (1) ODC + package leads with the board side shorted to Vss — low impedance at low frequency (shorted lead L) with a 45 MHz parallel peak; (2) cavity + simple VRM seen at the PCB vias — VRM inductance at low frequency, a ~30 MHz parallel peak from VRM L and cavity C, a ~200 MHz series-LC cavity dip, and modal resonances above 1 GHz; (3) die pads looking through the package into the VRM-loaded cavity — the package loop now includes the ~10 nH VRM path, so the Bandini peak drops from 45 MHz to 7 MHz and is very high. The same ODC/package-L pair that makes the Bandini Mountain is also the low-pass filter that hides board modes from the die; ODC impedance is far below the cavity's. One new feature appears at ~180 MHz: a small peak-and-dip from the parallel combination of package-lead L and cavity spreading L with cavity C, slightly below the cavity's own series resonance because the package loop adds inductance. Above it the die sees only ODC; below it, ODC ∥ cavity C gives a slightly lower impedance. Collapsing the cavity to a single ideal capacitor (C ≈ 7.2 nF here, package L = 0.25 nH) reproduces the same features, matching the 3D result well up to ~100 MHz (Figure 8.17), and the die-pad profiles for the 3D S-parameter cavity and the lumped-RLC cavity are nearly identical (Figure 8.18). The 180 MHz disturbance is insensitive to VRM inductance and very sensitive to cavity capacitance. Practical conclusion: modeling the cavity as one capacitor in parallel with the PDN is usually sufficient; cavity modes do not matter for PI.

- **Ch8 §8.9 — role of the cavity: spreading inductance, not capacitance; power puddles** — From the die pads the cavity is a lumped capacitor; its only real PDN contribution is spreading inductance between the MLCCs and the BGA pads, plus plane capacitance that is swamped by ODC. Same for package planes. Demonstration: 10 mil cavity, nine 100 µF caps (1 nH mounting, 25 mΩ ESR) on a 1 in × 1 in vs a 10 in × 10 in cavity — 100× capacitance difference (C = ε0·Dk·A/h; 1 in² at 10 mil, Dk ≈ 4.3 ≈ 0.94 nF vs ~94 nF for 10 in²). Four impedance features: cap-dominated low frequency (identical for both); cap self-resonance fres = 1/(2π√(Lmount·C)) (same for both, shifted slightly down by spreading L); parallel resonance of cap mounting L with cavity C, f = 1/(2π√(Lmount·Ccavity)) (different per cavity); and the first cavity mode, f ≈ (c/(2·Len·√Dk)), with n = 2 excited because drive and observation are both at the center. Estimated 150 MHz / 1500 MHz for the cap–cavity parallel resonances and 5.7 GHz / 0.57 GHz for first modes; 3D simulation (Figure 8.20) gives 105 MHz / 1300 MHz (low because the estimate omits spreading L) and 6 GHz / 0.6 GHz. Adding the S-parameter cavity models (on-die 50 nF, package 250 pH, 10 mil, Dk = 4.3, nine 100 nF/1 nH/25 mΩ caps) to the ODC-plus-package circuit (Figure 8.21) gives *identical* die-pad profiles for the 1-inch and 10-inch cavities (Figure 8.22), reproduced by a lumped capacitor model. Adding ~1 square of sheet inductance (spreading) between package and caps makes the lumped model match the S-parameter model (Figure 8.23). Three consequences: (1) the cavity's job is low spreading inductance from package pads to caps — cavity capacitance and cavity size outside the cap footprint are irrelevant; thinner dielectric is better, and the common claim that the cavity supplies high-frequency capacitance to the chip is wrong; (2) cavity peaks at and above the Bandini frequency are not seen at the die pads; (3) since cavity size does not matter to the PDN, a cavity only needs to connect the caps to the package it decouples — enabling "power puddles," small islands of power/ground plane around the package that do not extend to the VRM, and plane segments so many rails share one layer (valid only if those planes are not signal return paths). Capacitors for PDN impedance and capacitors for damping plane resonances are two separate requirements sharing only the low-mounting-inductance goal; the SI/EMI caps should be small body size (low mounting L) with the largest C in that body, so their parallel resonances sit below the Bandini band.

- **Ch8 §8.10 — the VRM as a series RL, sized from slew time** — A VRM holds Vout by feeding back a sense line against a reference to control a pulse generator driving dI/dt through an inductor (switching) or a series FET (linear). Full feedback modeling is possible, but under normal use a series R + L approximates it well — measured vs simulated output impedance of a DC-DC POL (Figure 8.24) matched with R = 4 mΩ, L = 500 nH, plus a 740 µF / 4 mΩ / 5.7 nH bulk cap. The 500 nH is not a physical loop but the feedback loop's time constant: a faster loop looks like lower inductance, and depending on regulator type the equivalent L ranges 1 nH to 1000 nH. Estimate from the large-signal slew question "how long to go from ~0 to ~max current?" using V = L·dI/dt with V = the PDN tolerance (≈5% of Vdd) and *without* bulk-cap current: 1 V rail, 0.5 A transient, 1 µs ramp → L = (0.05 V × 1 µs)/0.5 A ≈ 100 nH. Because that L is large, the bare VRM impedance exceeds any reasonable target impedance even at low frequency, which is exactly why bulk capacitors are required. Faster feedback → lower equivalent L → higher parallel resonance with the cavity; above that resonance the cavity impedance is independent of the VRM. Below the cavity's series-LC resonance the cavity impedance is location-independent; above it, spreading inductance to the observation point matters. The VRM's series L usually dwarfs the interconnect L from VRM to observation point, which only slightly increases the effective loop.

- **Ch8 §8.11 (part) — bulk capacitance: frequency-domain and time-domain sizing** — Frequency domain: bulk caps must hold impedance below Ztarget over the band where the VRM cannot, i.e. below fVRM-max, the frequency where the VRM output impedance rises past Ztarget. With Ztarget = ωL_VRM, fVRM-max = Ztarget/(2π·L_VRM) (typically 10 kHz–1 MHz): 0.01 Ω target with 10 nH → ~160 kHz; 10 mΩ with 20 nH → ~80 kHz. Minimum bulk C is set so the cap impedance crosses Ztarget at that same frequency, Cbulk = 1/(2π·fVRM-max·Ztarget) = L_VRM/Ztarget², i.e. the same condition as Z0 = √(L/C) ≤ Ztarget — the "characteristic impedance" criterion, a starting floor for minimum capacitance; more C is often needed to damp the peak down to an acceptable level. Worked: Ztarget = 10 mΩ, L_VRM = 20 nH → f ≈ 80 kHz, Cbulk ≈ 1/(2π·80 kHz·0.01) ≈ 20 µF. Time domain: the bulk cap supplies charge until the VRM responds, and its droop ΔV = I·Δt/C must stay within the typical 5% tolerance.

- `#L1647-L1734` — §8.11 part 1: bulk capacitance is the most-asked, not the most important, question. Frequency-domain role: hold impedance below Ztarget where the VRM cannot; fVRM-max is where VRM impedance crosses Ztarget, set by Ztarget = ωL_VRM (10 kHz–1 MHz range; 0.01 Ω with 10 nH ≈ 160 kHz). Minimum C from Ztarget = 1/(2π·fVRM-max·C) → Cbulk = L_VRM/Ztarget², equivalent to the parallel-LC characteristic-impedance criterion Z0 = √(L/C) ≤ Ztarget — a floor only, since extra C is often needed for damping. Numbers: 10 mΩ target, 20 nH → ~80 kHz and ~20 µF. Time-domain view begins: bulk cap supplies charge until the VRM responds, and ΔV = I·Δt/C must stay under the 5% tolerance (the rest of §8.11 continues past this chunk).
