# L1 overview

- **8.11 Bulk capacitors: how much is enough?** Estimates the minimum board capacitance from the frequency where the VRM's output impedance crosses the target, giving Cbulk ≈ LVRM/Ztarget², and shows a time-domain charge-storage derivation lands on the same number. Typical answers land in the 100–1,000 µF range, with larger values needed for low target impedances and slow VRM loops.
- **8.12 Optimizing the bulk capacitor and VRM.** Adding bulk capacitance to a VRM creates a parallel resonance peak that can exceed the target by 3× and even destabilize the supply, so the real design variables are ESR and damping rather than capacitance alone. Low-ESR ceramics underdamp, while electrolytic or tantalum ESR near the target impedance removes the peak.
- **8.13 Building the PDN ecosystem.** Below roughly 1 MHz the VRM and bulk capacitors look like a single ~10 nH inductance that adds to cavity spreading inductance and package lead inductance, shifting the Bandini Mountain lower and making it much taller. The worked case turns a 1 Ω/45 MHz shorted-board peak into a 10 MHz, 9.6 Ω peak.
- **8.14 Fundamental limits to peak impedance.** Because a dead-short board still leaves the package lead inductance resonating with on-die capacitance, the optimum board is not a short but a resistor equal to the target impedance. The peak can never fall below the characteristic impedance of the package inductance and on-die capacitance, making lower package inductance and more on-die capacitance the only real levers.
- **8.15 One-value MLCC capacitors: general features.** Establishes the case-study baseline and the three reference impedance profiles, then shows a single MLCC creates two new parallel peaks. Any added capacitor lowers the Bandini peak because its ESL parallels the VRM and bulk inductance, but some values do far more than others.
- **8.16 Optimizing the single MLCC value.** Argues that arbitrary values such as "add 10 µF" have no basis, and that the best single value puts the MLCC self-resonant frequency near the Bandini peak (about 160 nF in the example). It also proves the golden rule: lowering mounting inductance beats everything else, and more identical capacitors can be worse because parallel ESR removes damping.
- **8.17 Using three different MLCC values.** The common 10 µF/1 µF/0.1 µF per-pin recommendation is shown to be arbitrary: with equal ESL the three values behave almost identically, and what matters is peak impedance, not the impedance dips. Hundreds of arbitrarily chosen capacitors only make the board look like a dead short, which is not a robust solution.
- **8.18 Optimizing the values of three capacitors.** If the full ecology is known, three values (1000 nF, 60 nF, 20 nF) can cut the peak from 10 Ω to 0.25 Ω, a 40× improvement over no capacitors and better than a dead short, by adding board-level damping. The catch is that the optimum depends on on-die capacitance, package inductance and every ESL, which motivates the FDTIM.
- **8.19 The Frequency Domain Target Impedance Method (FDTIM).** FDTIM starts from the Bandini Mountain's characteristic impedance and q-factor, then picks a minimum capacitor count so board inductance falls below package inductance. For high-q peaks below 200 MHz it aims for a flat impedance at the target to damp the resonance; for low-q peaks above 200 MHz the capacitor values barely matter.
- **8.20 Selecting capacitor values with the FDTIM.** Real catalogs offer three values per decade (1/22/47), so the smallest value is chosen for an SRF above the Bandini frequency (about 633 pF at 200 MHz and 1 nH), giving a pool of up to 14 values from 22 µF to 1 nF. Sizing each value by ESR/Ztarget yields a flat profile with 16 capacitors that beats 16 equal 10 µF parts by 5×.
- **8.21 When on-die capacitance is large and package inductance is small.** A 400 nF/2.5 pH die with a 5 mΩ target shows the same method at the extreme: 200 identical capacitors achieve a 100× peak reduction but no damping, while 135 FDTIM-selected capacitors meet the target. Bulk mounting inductance and capacitor count then clean up the residual 1 MHz resonance.
- **8.22 An alternative strategy using controlled-ESR capacitors.** Controlled-ESR parts trade capacitance for a flat, value-independent impedance centered on their SRF, adding a third sizing condition: parallel resistance near the target. Thirteen such parts reach ~130 mΩ in the 100 mΩ case and 100 parts meet 5 mΩ, at roughly 5× unit cost but similar assembly cost.
- **8.23 On-package decoupling (OPD) capacitors.** Package substrates allow interdigitated or reverse-aspect-ratio parts with ≤0.2 nH mounting inductance, letting OPD capacitors split the Bandini Mountain into two lower peaks. Ten controlled-ESR OPD capacitors can replace dozens of board MLCCs, a risk-reduction step common on large FPGAs and ASICs despite higher component cost.
- **8.24 Multiple chips sharing the same rail.** Other chips on a shared rail act as extra decoupling capacitors with lower ESL but much lower ESR than MLCCs, and their board-side SRF typically lands near the Bandini frequency. If the board is undamped they slightly lower the peak, but if the board was FDTIM-damped they short out that damping and make the peak worse.
- **8.25 The bottom line.** The PDN is the whole path from die pads to VRM pads, and its peak impedances—above all the on-die-capacitance/package-inductance Bandini Mountain—are the real figure of merit. Cavity capacitance and size hardly matter, equal versus three capacitor values barely matters, and the robust prescription is a resistive board at the target impedance plus FDTIM value selection.
