# L1 overview

- **9.1 Why the transient current matters** — The chapter opens by arguing that a constant-current die would need only a DC resistance spec (IR drop), and that real noise comes from the transient current waveform interacting with the PDN impedance. It warns that a PDN impedance spec is meaningless without knowing the worst-case transient current, and it sets up the three canonical stimuli (impulse, step, resonance) plus the flat-region/peak view of an impedance profile.
- **9.2 Flat impedance and target impedance** — With a resistive (flat) PDN, voltage noise is simply current times impedance, so a voltage tolerance and a worst-case current define a target impedance, `Ztarget = Vdd × tolerance / Itransient`. The worked example is 1 V, 1 A, 5% (50 mV) giving 50 mΩ, and the section defines transient current as maximum minus minimum current on Figure 9.1.
- **9.3 Estimating the transient current** — Getting the transient current is the hard part, since vendors rarely supply it; usually only the rail's maximum current is known. The section bounds the problem with the idle-to-max ratio (about 50% for typical FPGAs, near 100% for mobile chips, 90% idle two decades ago) and recommends 50% of maximum as a default, acknowledging that buying a lower target impedance is "insurance."
- **9.4 Actual PDN current through a die** — A constant current source is only a first-order model; the real current is a train of charge impulses at clock edges. The section walks through a CMOS inverter driving a lumped capacitive load, defines the clock-edge current and its charge `Qclk_edge = C × Vdd`, and shows that dynamic current is that charge times clock frequency, so doubling the clock doubles average current but not the per-edge pulse.
- **9.5 Capacitance referenced to both Vss and Vdd** — Real interconnect capacitance is split between both rails, which doubles the effective load capacitance and the RC charging time. The displacement current splits, so only half the charging current flows around the PDN loop on the rising edge, but a loop current also flows on the falling edge. The net result is PDN current on both clock edges — twice per cycle for the clock tree, once per cycle for combinational logic.
- **9.6 Measurement of clock-edge current** — An embedded controller (Arduino-class, 5 V, 16 MHz) is instrumented with quiet HIGH and LOW pins feeding 450 Ω plus 50 Ω coax into a 12-bit scope. The measured die rail collapses about 20 mV on Vdd and bounces 35 mV on Vss, appearing at twice the clock frequency. This is the experimental proof that PDN current flows only at clock edges.
- **9.7 The real origin of PDN noise** — The chapter reframes noise generation: gates consume charge from the on-die capacitance, its voltage droops, that droop appears across the package inductance, and `v/L` drives the `di/dt` that replenishes the charge. The forcing function is the ODC voltage drop, not `L di/dt` as usually taught. Simulations halving inductance (50.7 pH to 25.3 pH) show a faster current ramp, a 25% higher resonant frequency and lower Q, while the initial 31 mV droop is unchanged.
- **9.8 Equations of an impedance peak** — The same RLC is series when viewed from the board (VNA) and parallel when viewed from the die, with the same resonant frequency and the same `Z0 = √(L/C)` crossover. Peak height is approximately `Q × Z0`, series minimum is `R`, and `Q = Z0/R`. A worked design starts from 50 nF and 100 mΩ at 100 MHz backward to L = 50.7 pH, Z0 = 32 mΩ, Q = 3.15 and R = 10.1 mΩ, confirmed by an ADS simulation.
- **9.9 The three characterizing current waveforms** — Impulse (one clock edge, plus anti-impulse for a missing edge), step (abrupt dynamic-current turn-on from clock start, frequency change or cache miss), and resonance (steps repeated at the PDN resonant frequency). Each interacts with a different impedance feature and lets you either predict noise from RLC values or reverse-engineer RLC values from measured response. Table 9.2 sets up the 1.55 A / 32 mΩ / 100 MHz case study.
- **9.10 Impulse response** — At 1 GHz and 1.55 A the average charge per cycle is 1.55 nC, and a single impulse produces an initial droop of `ΔV = ΔQ/C`, predicted at 31 mV. The simulation shows 29 mV (some charge arrived from the board during the 1 nsec pulse), rings for over 40 nsec at a 10 nsec period (100 MHz) and damps in about three cycles (Q ≈ 3). Only on-die capacitance can reduce this clock-edge droop.
- **9.11 Step response** — A step of `I0` into a parallel RLC gives a damped sine with an initial droop of `I0 × Z0`. When the step equals the worst-case transient current, the droop equals the voltage tolerance only if the characteristic impedance meets the target impedance. Simulation gives 50 mV (5% of 1 V). The fix is to raise ODC or lower mounted package inductance (or add low-inductance on-package capacitors), since `Z0 = √(L/C)`.
- **9.12 Resonance response** — A square wave at the resonant frequency has a first harmonic `4/π` larger than its peak-to-peak value, so the peak-to-peak noise is `(4/π) × Itransient × Zpeak`. The simulation reaches 201 mV against a 198 mV estimate with only 1.55 A of load current — loop current swings from +4 A to −2.5 A as energy accumulates, showing a one-sided current producing a two-sided voltage. It takes about Q cycles to build up, and the section derives the 1.6× target-impedance allowance for resonance peaks.
- **9.13 Target impedance vs transient and AC steady state** — The target-impedance definition pairs a single-sided peak-to-peak current with a double-sided (±5%) tolerance, which matches transient behavior where a VRM loop or an LC recenters the voltage and a current release produces an opposite spike. The 32 mΩ resistive and reactive PDNs droop identically, but the reactive one recenters to 12.5 mV of DC IR drop and then overshoots 39 mV on release. In AC steady state the definition is conservative by 2× for a sine wave and 1.6× for a square wave.
- **9.14 Reactive elements, Q-factor and peak impedances** — Four PDNs are engineered to the same 50 mΩ peak at 1 MHz with Q from 0 to 8, which requires higher Q to mean lower L, higher C and lower R (constant `Z0 × Q`). For a 1 A step the droop falls with higher Q (50 mV for the resistor, 24 mV for Q = 2) because Z0 is lower, even though ringing lasts longer. Under sine or square excitation every peak eventually reaches 50 mV peak-to-peak, independent of sharpness, and the low-Q case drifts off center because of DC IR drop.
- **9.15 Rogue waves** — With several comparable, high-Q peaks, carefully sequenced square-wave bursts at 1, 10 and 100 MHz can stack energy from one resonance onto the next. Each peak alone stays within tolerance (969, 967, 962 mV), but the sequenced train reaches 930 mV, 7% below nominal, despite every peak meeting the 50 mΩ target. The effect scales with Q and is extremely unlikely from real microcode, but it is the reason to keep the profile flat and Q low.
- **9.16 Robust design strategy** — The three ingredients of a rogue wave are comparable peaks near the target, high Q, and a precisely sequenced current. You cannot control customer code, so you attack the first two: one dominant peak, low Q, low DC resistance, and regulator feedback near the load. The section contrasts this with adaptive voltage positioning / load lines, and formalizes fully robust (all peaks below target) versus marginally robust (characteristic impedance below target) PDNs.
- **9.17 Clock-edge impulses from switched capacitor loads** — A constant current source keeps drawing its programmed current regardless of rail voltage and therefore provides no damping, unlike CMOS whose current tracks voltage. A switched capacitor load (SCL) consumes charge per edge and reproduces the Poisson-like decay of real logic current. The half-clock topology is preferred over a Vss-only full-clock load, and an RC time constant of about a quarter clock period shapes the pulse so it ends before the next edge.
- **9.18 Waveforms composed of clock impulse trains** — The SCL reproduces all three signatures: a single impulse (29 mV versus 31 mV predicted), a step (56 mV including superimposed clock-edge noise), and a resonance (196 mV peak-to-peak versus 201 mV for the current source, because the load adds damping). Branch currents show the ODC supplying the spikes while the BGA current is smoothed by the LC filter, and a missing clock edge (anti-impulse) produces a voltage spike of the opposite polarity. Doubling the clock to 2 GHz leaves impulses and anti-impulses unchanged but doubles the step droop to 106 mV.
- **9.19 Clock gating, clock swallowing and power gating** — Gate-on produces the step signature, and a PDN whose Z0 is twice the target droops twice the tolerance. Clock swallowing creates anti-impulses; the 12-bit patterns (1110, 110, 10, 100, 1000, 100000, 100000000000) show a 1/12 subharmonic near 83 MHz that sits close to the 100 MHz resonance, so power falls 12× while noise remains substantial. Because the PDN has memory, exact pulse timing matters, especially when the clock period approaches the PDN time constant.
- **9.20 Power gating** — Collapsing a domain means closing a power switch between an always-on upstream domain and a downstream one, causing inrush and charge sharing. With equal upstream and downstream capacitance the shared node lands at half the supply (500 mV droop with a 1 mΩ switch), while a 1 Ω switch limits the droop to 16 mV at the cost of a slow downstream rise. The design rule is to make the switch RC time constant at least 20× the reactive PDN time constant, or to sequence several smaller switches.
- **9.21 The bottom line** — Ten closing rules: target impedance is the primary metric; 50% of maximum current is a reasonable default; PDN current is clock-edge impulses smoothed by ODC and package inductance; noise is driven by ODC droop, not `L di/dt`; impulse/step/resonance cover the fundamentals; characteristic impedance below target is the marginal-robustness condition; all peaks below target is full robustness; the SCL beats a current source; rogue waves are rare but real; and power-saving clock/power gating raises transient current and therefore requires lower impedance.

# L2 detail

- `#L1-L30` — Chapter 9 opening: the Vdd–Vss transient current plus PDN impedance sets voltage noise; a truly constant die current would reduce PDN design to IR drop, and an external sense line at the die pads would make even that trivial. The Tip states that no robust PDN spec is possible without the worst-case transient current.
- `#L31-L60` — Current depends on the microcode and application, so simplified special cases are used to build a parameterized model of the transient current. The analysis mixes a time-domain current stimulus with a frequency-domain impedance, which requires assuming the impedance-forming circuits are linear and time invariant; the VRM and die dynamic load violate this and their damping changes with time.
- `#L61-L90` — Three stimuli are introduced — the single clock pulse (impulse response), the step current response, and the resonant response — each sensitive to a different frequency-domain feature. Knowing any two of current waveform, impedance profile and voltage response yields the third, which is how PDN knowledge carries from one product generation to the next.
- `#L91-L110` — An impedance profile is decomposed into flat regions (frequency-independent) and peaks (resonant frequency, characteristic impedance, q-factor), and each current waveform interacts with them differently. The plan is to analyze flat regions first with broadband current, then peaks.
- `#L111-L136` — For a flat, resistor-like PDN the noise is `ΔV = I × Z`, and requiring noise below a tolerance yields the target impedance `Ztarget = Vdd × tolerance / Itransient`. Example: 1 V rail, 1 A worst-case transient, 5% (50 mV) gives 50 mΩ; the Tip calls target impedance a starting place for flat regions.
- `#L137-L163` — The starting-place current model is a change between minimum and maximum draw, illustrated in Figure 9.1. Transient current is the maximum change, typically an idle-to-heavy-logic transition, i.e. the difference between the two current limits; the minimum includes static, leakage and clock-generation current.
- `#L164-L190` — The hard part is obtaining the transient current, which vendors rarely provide because it depends on microcode. Only maximum rail current is usually available (it sizes the VRM), so the designer must decide how much of it changes; typical FPGAs and large chips differ about 50% between minimum steady-state and maximum, two decades ago some processors idled at 90% power, and mobile chips can swing 1% to 100% in microseconds.
- `#L191-L214` — Without better data, 50% of maximum current is a reasonable transient-current estimate and feeds the target impedance. The Tip warns that vendor answers are rarely realistic, so uncertainty is inherent; one can buy insurance by assuming a lower target impedance and paying more for the PDN.
- `#L215-L260` — The simple model treats the die as a modulated constant current source, good enough for first-order guidelines but not how current actually behaves. A transistor-level simulation of gates, extracted interconnect and specific microcode is possible but yields only a point solution with no transferable insight; the practical path is to approximate the per-gate current and build typical and worst-case signatures.
- `#L261-L300` — A CMOS inverter is a p-channel/n-channel push-pull pair between Vdd and Vss (Figure 9.2), driving a load that is the output net metallization plus the next gates' input capacitance, modeled as one lumped capacitor. With the load referenced only to Vss, the p-channel charges it on a rising transition and the n-channel discharges it on a falling transition (Figure 9.3).
- `#L301-L340` — With a Vss-only reference, PDN current flows only during the rising transition, as a pulse whose rise follows the gate turn-on and the RC time constant formed by channel on-resistance and load capacitance; 10 Ω and 1 pF give a 10 psec charge time. The result is one PDN current pulse per clock cycle (Figure 9.4).
- `#L341-L389` — The charge per edge is `Qclk_edge = C × Vdd`, the area under the clock-edge current pulse, and it is independent of clock frequency. Average (dynamic) current is `Q/T = C × Vdd × f`, so doubling the clock leaves each pulse unchanged but doubles dynamic current (Figure 9.5). On-die capacitance smooths the spikes, so the package and board see something close to the averaged dynamic current.
- `#L390-L435` — Interconnect capacitance is normally referenced to both Vdd and Vss (Figure 9.6), roughly symmetrically, which doubles the load capacitance and the RC charging time and doubles the integrated charge. The displacement current driven by C·dV/dt splits, half to Vss and half back up to Vdd, and only the Vss half flows around the PDN loop; the Vdd-side capacitor is effectively discharged through the p-channel.
- `#L436-L480` — On a falling transition the reversed dV/dt drives displacement current from the Vdd capacitance through the n-channel to Vss (Figure 9.8), so a PDN loop current now exists on both edges. The p- and n-channel currents are twice the loop current because energy stored in the capacitors is dissipated in the FETs; the clock tree therefore draws at twice the clock frequency.
- `#L481-L512` — Combinational logic differs: latches release data on the rising edge, gates settle, and the logic does not re-fire until the next rising edge, so logic draws once per cycle while the clock tree draws twice (Tip). Vdd/Vss coupling may differ edge to edge but averages out across many wires; the rail-to-rail loop current is what creates noise across the PDN impedance.
- `#L513-L560` — Measurement setup: an embedded controller with a single 5 V core/I-O rail, one pin pegged HIGH (Vdd sense) and one pegged LOW (Vss sense), each through 450 Ω and 50 Ω coax into a 50 Ω scope input, plus a fast-toggling third pin. The 450 Ω resistors limit I/O current to 5 V / 500 Ω = 10 mA (Figure 9.9).
- `#L561-L600` — A Teledyne LeCroy HDO 12-bit scope captures the rails at a 16 MHz clock (60 nsec period) with 3 nsec driver edges, while microcode holds the switching pin low for three cycles and high for one (Figure 9.10, 50 nsec/div). The measured Vdd rail collapses about 20 mV and the Vss rail rises about 35 mV on each clock edge, i.e. at twice the clock frequency.
- `#L601-L617` — The twice-per-cycle compression is explained by capacitance referenced to both rails plus clock-tree activity on both edges while the chip is near idle; more gate activity (I/O latches on the rising edge) produces a larger collapse. Regardless of reference topology, the current is a train of pulses coincident with the clock, whose average is the dynamic current.
- `#L618-L660` — The noise mechanism: switching gates drain charge from the ODC, its voltage falls, that voltage appears across the package inductance and drives a di/dt that pulls current in from outside. Capacitor and inductor relations (Figure 9.11) make `i/C` the forcing function for capacitor voltage and `v/L` the forcing function for inductor current; which is cause and which is effect is situational.
- `#L661-L700` — Capacitor current may step instantaneously while capacitor voltage moves only gradually (`i = C dv/dt`); inductor voltage may step while inductor current moves gradually (`v = L di/dt`). In a CMOS PDN the ODC charge depletion is the cause of the droop, so the root cause of PDN noise is charge consumed by charging load capacitance; increasing ODC or reducing switched charge is the most direct mitigation.
- `#L701-L745` — The ODC also shields core circuits from external Vdd noise: external noise must pass the package inductance and then divides against the ODC's low impedance. PDN noise is therefore mostly self-aggression. The die-side voltage drop across the package lead is the forcing function that produces di/dt through the lead — the reverse of the usual inductance intuition.
- `#L746-L790` — In integral form, the time integral of inductor voltage changes its current, so the ODC voltage must fall and create a drop across the inductor before external current arrives. `v/L` sets how fast charge is replenished; a larger droop brings current faster but hurts Fmax, while a smaller L brings current faster without the droop. Figure 9.12 is the illustrative circuit with a switched-capacitor "circuits" block.
- `#L791-L830` — Simulating a single 1 nsec (narrowed to 100 psec) clock-edge impulse with 50.7 pH versus 25.3 pH (Figure 9.13): the ODC voltage drops 31 mV per `ΔV = ΔQ/C` plus an extra drop from the 2 mΩ ODC series resistance. Halving L doubles di/dt, raises the resonant frequency 25% (f ∝ 1/√L) and lowers Q, but leaves the initial sag unchanged because no inductor delivers current within the impulse.
- `#L831-L877` — For I/O circuits the causality reverses: the di/dt of large off-die switching currents and loop mutual inductance drive noise on quiet loops, and "ground bounce" is inductive crosstalk through shared return inductance. For core logic, the large-signal `dI` is what matters and its rise time `dT` selects the charge source — ODC for fast edges, on-package capacitors over several cycles, board capacitors over tens, VRM over hundreds.
- `#L878-L920` — The case study RLC (Figure 9.14) is series when viewed from the board (a VNA sees a resonant dip) and parallel when viewed from the die with a low-impedance board (a resonant peak), using identical component values. Both share the same `f = 1/(2π√(LC))`, and the L and C reactances cross at that frequency with value `Z0 = √(L/C)` (Figure 9.15).
- `#L921-L956` — The series minimum is R and `Q = Z0/R`; the parallel peak sits as far above Z0 as the series dip sits below it, giving `Zpeak ≈ Q × Z0`, accurate for high Q but not near Q = 1. Three uses of these equations: compute peak properties from RLC, extract RLC from measured frequency/Q/initial dip, or engineer RLC for a desired peak (e.g. 50 nF giving 100 mΩ at 100 MHz).
- `#L957-L991` — Table 9.1 works the example backward: 50 nF and 100 MHz require L = 50.7 pH, giving Z0 = 32 mΩ; a 100 mΩ peak requires Q = 3.15 and therefore a 10.1 mΩ loop resistance. Figure 9.16 shows the split of that resistance, and the ADS simulation in Figure 9.17 confirms a nearly 100 mΩ peak at 100 MHz with Z0 deliberately set equal to the target impedance.
- `#L992-L1030` — The three characterizing waveforms are defined: impulse (single clock-edge charge, with anti-impulse as the missing-edge counterpart), step (abrupt minimum-to-maximum dynamic current from clock start, frequency change, or pipeline restart after a cache miss, with rise time as fast as a clock edge), and resonance (repeated steps at the PDN resonant frequency, low probability but devastating). They both predict noise and allow reverse engineering of RLC values.
- `#L1031-L1075` — Table 9.2 converts the frequency-domain case study into time-domain inputs and closed-form results. A steady 1.55 A dynamic current is set (observable as a 1.55 A increase in bench supply current), giving a 32 mΩ target impedance for ±5% at 1 V; that current was chosen so the target-impedance line crosses exactly at the Z0 crossover. The stimuli use a SPICE constant current source.
- `#L1076-L1120` — At 1 GHz and 1.55 A the average charge per cycle is 1.55 nC; in the lab a single edge is isolated by slowing the clock to about 10 MHz so the impulse is over in 1 nsec and the response is visible in the quiet period. Since the impulse is short relative to the PDN response, all charge comes from the ODC and the initial droop is `ΔV = ΔQ/C`, predicted as 31 mV.
- `#L1121-L1166` — The ADS impulse response (Figure 9.18) shows a 29 mV initial dip rather than 31 mV because some charge entered from the board during the 1 nsec pulse, which is not negligible against the 10 nsec resonant period. The ringing lasts over 40 nsec with a 10 nsec period (100 MHz, as designed) and about three visible cycles (Q ≈ 3 versus 3.15 calculated); the droop depth gives C, the ring period gives f, the decay gives Q. Only ODC reduces the initial droop; loop inductance barely helps.
- `#L1167-L1210` — A step of I0 into the parallel RLC gives a damped sine offset by the DC IR drop; the initial droop is `I0 × Z0`. If the step equals the worst-case transient current, the droop equals the tolerance only when `Z0 ≤ Ztarget`; if the Bandini Mountain's characteristic impedance is higher, the droop exceeds the noise limit (Figure 9.19).
- `#L1211-L1260` — Simulation gives a 50 mV droop, i.e. 5% of 1 V, matching the tolerance used to derive the target impedance because Z0 was engineered equal to it. Reducing the step droop requires lowering Z0 by increasing ODC or reducing mounted package loop inductance, or by adding on-package decoupling with very low mounting inductance; meeting Z0 alone is not sufficient because resonant peaks can still exceed tolerance.
- `#L1261-L1310` — For a square wave at resonance, the Fourier first harmonic is `4/π` times the square wave's peak-to-peak amplitude, and that harmonic flows through the peak impedance `Zpeak = Q × Z0`. Hence `Vpp = (4/π) × Itransient × Zpeak`; the case study's 100 mΩ peak with 1.55 A peak-to-peak predicts about 198 mV. The simulation in Figure 9.20 uses the same 1.55 A square wave.
- `#L1311-L1360` — Each load step lands in phase with the existing loop current, so the loop current grows to between +4 A out of the board and −2.5 A back into it, far above the 1.55 A load. This is the one-sided-current / two-sided-voltage rationale for the target-impedance definition, and Q ≈ 3 means about three cycles to reach maximum excursion. Q affects buildup but not the step response's initial droop.
- `#L1361-L1410` — Simulation reaches 201 mV peak-to-peak versus 198 mV predicted because the simulated peak is slightly above 100 mΩ. Noise scales with Q, Z0 and transient current; the conservative assumption that peak-to-peak is twice the allowed single-ended noise yields a condition `Zpeak < 1.6 × Ztarget`, and the section lists the three robustness requirements (flat regions, characteristic impedances, and peak impedances). Full stimulation of a resonance by real code is rare but can be forced by clock gating at the resonant frequency; aircraft-grade designs demand peaks below target, which costs more.
- `#L1411-L1460` — Target impedance is re-examined: `Ztarget = Vdd × tolerance / Itransient`. The transient current is peak-to-peak and single-sided while the ±5% tolerance is double-sided, which is consistent for resistive and flat PDNs and for load-line/adaptive voltage positioning schemes where the VRM does not restore nominal under load.
- `#L1461-L1510` — When a droop occurs, a VRM regulation loop or an LC section recenters the die voltage; when the current is later released the same PDN produces a spike of similar magnitude and opposite sign. Figure 9.21 compares a 32 mΩ resistive PDN with a reactive PDN of the same Z0 under a 1.55 A, 40 nsec step.
- `#L1511-L1560` — Both PDNs droop identically at the attack, but the reactive one recovers to near nominal (settling at the DC IR drop, 1.55 A × 8.1 mΩ = 12.5 mV) because the inductor impedance falls at low frequency. When the step ends at 40 nsec the resistive PDN returns instantly while the reactive PDN's inductor current charges the ODC to 39 mV above nominal — about 50 mV above its recentered value — demonstrating the double-sided response to a single-sided current.
- `#L1561-L1610` — In AC steady state (Figure 9.22) a square and a sine wave of 1.55 A peak-to-peak both start with a ~50 mV transient droop and then build to steady state. With a 0.104 Ω peak the sine predicts 0.161 V and measures 0.161 V; the square predicts 0.201 V and measures 0.201 V. The target impedance is 2× conservative for a sine and 1.6× (π/4 × 2) conservative for a square wave.
- `#L1611-L1660` — Reactive elements store energy and create peaks, but if peaks stay below target the worst case is almost always within tolerance, rogue waves aside. Four PDNs (Figure 9.23 and 9.24) are engineered to an identical 50 mΩ peak at 1 MHz with Q from 0 (pure 50 mΩ resistor) to 8, so sharper peaks come with lower Z0.
- `#L1661-L1705` — Table 9.3 shows the constant-peak family: higher Q requires lower inductance, higher capacitance and lower loop resistance, so Z0 falls while `Z0 × Q` stays fixed. This decouples peak height from Q and lets the section isolate the effect of damping on step, sine and square responses.
- `#L1706-L1750` — With a 1 A step (Figure 9.25), the 50 mΩ resistor drops 50 mV, Q = 2 drops 24 mV (its Z0 is 25 mΩ) and has 13 mΩ of path resistance, and higher-Q PDNs have still less DC IR drop. This is counterintuitive only because the peaks were engineered to equal height; the droop follows Z0, while the ringing lasts about Q cycles (2, 5 and 8 dips).
- `#L1751-L1795` — A 1 A peak-to-peak sine (0.5 A amplitude plus 0.5 A offset) gives 50 mV peak-to-peak on the flat 50 mΩ resistor, while reactive PDNs take about Q cycles to build to their maximum and sit centered nearer nominal because their DC resistance is lower. The Q = 2 case slightly exceeded the target (about 6 mΩ) and reached 56 mV; all of them show single-sided current producing two-sided voltage.
- `#L1796-L1846` — A square wave (Figure 9.27) behaves similarly, taking about Q cycles to build, with higher-Q waveforms centered and lower-Q ones offset by DC IR drop; high-Q peaks pass the first harmonic and reach `4/π` more peak-to-peak than the flat resistor. The flat profile consumes the entire tolerance, whereas reactive profiles recenter and use only part of it — except when multiple comparable peaks exist, which motivates the next section.
- `#L1847-L1900` — Rogue waves: several comparable, high-Q peaks let energy from one resonance keep driving the PDN while others are stimulated, superimposing into a composite that exceeds any single peak and the tolerance; the effect was introduced by Xiang Hu. Figure 9.28 is a contrived circuit with three parallel branches engineered to the same Q and peak impedance at three different frequencies.
- `#L1901-L1950` — The three peaks land at 1, 10 and 100 MHz, each 50 mΩ with Q = 4 (Table 9.4), measured at node 3 with a 1 A AC source (Figure 9.29). Each is stimulated separately by a square wave whose edge rate is slowed so the tenth harmonic does not reach the next peak; the 1 MHz burst drives the die to 969 mV at 4.3 µsec, above the 950 mV tolerance line.
- `#L1951-L2000` — The 10 MHz burst (from about 5 µsec) reaches 967 mV at 5.33 µsec, slightly deeper because it also stimulates the 1 MHz peak, and its rise time is limited so the 100 MHz peak stays quiet. The 100 MHz burst (from about 5.4 µsec) reaches 962 mV at 5.62 µsec with all three ringing, still above 950 mV. Isolated stimulation at constant frequency stays within ±5%, confirming target impedance as a robust indicator.
- `#L2001-L2052` — Concatenating the bursts in the right phase produces the rogue wave: 969 mV from 1 MHz alone, 948 mV after compounding 10 MHz, then 930 mV (7% below nominal) after adding 100 MHz, while the load never exceeds 0–1 A. Energy stored in the reactive elements acts as memory, higher Q makes it worse, and if Q were 10 or the peaks above target the excursion would be far larger; real code reaching this sequence is extremely unlikely, but keeping all resonances low-Q is still good practice.
- `#L2053-L2100` — A rogue wave needs three things: multiple comparable peaks at or slightly above target, high Q on those peaks, and a carefully sequenced transient current. Nothing can be done about customer code, so the design removes the first two: a single dominant peak at target cannot produce a rogue wave, and lower Q shortens ringing so responses cannot overlap. A purely resistive PDN is immune (Figure 9.32 stays between 950 mV and 1 V under the same pathological stimulus).
- `#L2101-L2152` — The robust strategy is a flat profile below target, minimum DC resistance (feedback point near the load), and few low-Q peaks; the maximum transient current is usually the only reliable load information. Adaptive voltage positioning / load lines are the opposite philosophy, deliberately sagging under load to cap overshoot at the cost of power. Target impedance is a reference line, not a guarantee: 2× conservative for sine, 1.6× for square, exact for flat broadband, and defeatable only by rogue waves; fully robust, marginally robust and vulnerable PDNs are defined accordingly.
- `#L2153-L2200` — Section 9.17 introduces the switched capacitor load (SCL) because a constant current source draws its programmed current regardless of rail voltage and provides no damping, whereas CMOS dynamic current is proportional to voltage and behaves like a resistive element. Time-varying resistor loads were tried but are cumbersome; switched capacitors match the actual charge-per-edge mechanism.
- `#L2201-L2250` — The SCL reproduces both the ODC noise and the terminal noise, and it damps the PDN as CMOS does. Because CMOS draws more current (lower parallel resistance) at high load, damping is time-varying and nonlinear, which frequency-domain analysis can only average; the time domain is required, and the step response reveals different Q for current attack and release.
- `#L2251-L2300` — The SCL case study reuses the 100 mΩ / 100 MHz / 1.55 A PDN (Table 9.5); 1 GHz gives 1.55 nC per cycle and, at 1 V, a switched capacitance of 1.55 nF. To spread current over the whole clock period the SCL runs a half-clock and switches both Vdd- and Vss-referenced capacitors, producing a Poisson-like profile that decays to zero before the next edge; a Vss-only full-clock variant would produce unrealistic early-cycle droop from ODC ESR.
- `#L2301-L2350` — The ADS schematic (Figure 9.33) has the RLC PDN on the left and the SCL on the right, with `C_load` derived from transient current and clock frequency. A bit sequencer drives the switch: five zeros give a 5 nsec quiet period, then alternating bits give a square wave at half the 1 GHz clock, one charge impulse per rising product-clock edge. Ammeters observe switch current, load current, ODC current and external/BGA current; Figure 9.34 shows the 1.55 nC impulse (1.55 A for 1 nsec).
- `#L2351-L2400` — L1 shapes the pulse with inductive and resistive properties: without it the load capacitors would fill almost instantly from C3 through only R2 and the switch resistance. The RC time constant is chosen as a quarter of the clock period so the current ends before the next edge — smaller tau gives a tall narrow spike, larger tau spills past the cycle (a setup-time violation). R1 carries DC IR drop and power loss for package current while R2 carries clock-edge IR drop; a 60/40 split is suggested, and R1 + R2 form the 250 psec time constant from Chapter 8.
- `#L2401-L2427` — The chosen topology and parameter distribution strongly affect simulated droop, and every PDN simulator makes such assumptions implicitly or explicitly; the simple circuit is a deliberate abstraction for intuition about board/package/die interaction and damping. Figure 9.35 shows the SCL impulse response, matching Figure 9.18's current-source impulse.
- `#L2428-L2475` — SCL impulse response: 29 mV simulated versus 31 mV calculated (the gap is charge from the PDN inductance during the 1 nsec draw). The step response (Figure 9.36) is driven by a half-clock; impulse heights vary slightly with PDN voltage, and the ODC-plus-loop-inductance low-pass filter removes nearly all clock-edge noise from the BGA loop current.
- `#L2476-L2525` — The die sees clock-edge noise on top of the step envelope, giving 56 mV droop rather than the step-only prediction. Figure 9.37 offsets the branch currents: the switch current is bipolar and double magnitude because one capacitor charges while the other discharges, the load current tracks PDN voltage, the ODC supplies the spikes plus a low-frequency damped sinusoid, and the BGA current is smooth; BGA plus ODC current equals load current by Kirchhoff's law.
- `#L2526-L2578` — Resonance with the SCL (Figure 9.38): 100 MHz, 50% duty impulses for about three periods (Q just over 3), giving 196 mV peak-to-peak versus 201 mV for the current source, i.e. the capacitive load adds damping. Figure 9.39 combines impulse, impulse train and anti-impulse: a missing clock edge leaves package current with nowhere to go, charging the ODC and producing a near-mirror-image spike, which previews the danger of clock swallowing.
- `#L2579-L2620` — Section 9.19 ties gating to real CMOS: at 2 GHz the automatically shortened tau makes impulses taller and narrower, so impulse and anti-impulse charge (and droop) are unchanged while dynamic current doubles and the step droop doubles — 106 mV measured versus prediction, again with clock-edge noise superimposed. Clock gating then applies the impulse, step and resonance signatures to the PDN.
- `#L2621-L2665` — Abrupt clock gating gives a step droop approximately `Itransient × Z0`; a PDN whose Z0 is twice target droops twice the tolerance, while one meeting target survives. Repetitive gating can fully stimulate the resonance if Q is not too high for a single peak. Pulse swallowing is gating's cousin and produces the anti-impulse; Figure 9.41 modulates full → half → full clock with 50 mV, 25 mV and 2.5% excursions plus edge noise.
- `#L2666-L2710` — Figure 9.42 walks through swallowing 1-in-4 (1110), 1-in-3 (110), 1/2, 2/3, 3/4, 5/6 and 11/12 (100000000000) on a 12-bit pattern, ending at an 83 MHz subharmonic near the 100 MHz resonance; power falls 12× yet peak-to-peak noise stays substantial. With a 10 nsec resonant period and 1 nsec clock, the clock period is comparable to the PDN time constant, and the PDN's stored energy and pulse history matter; at 2–10 GHz each cycle is less disruptive. Meeting target impedance keeps gating and swallowing marginal-to-reasonably robust.
- `#L2711-L2760` — Section 9.20 treats power gating as an SCL special case: collapse a domain to zero volts with a power switch, keeping always-on upstream logic running. Closing the switch causes inrush and charge sharing within about 1 nsec, too fast for the package inductor to help, so the on-die upstream charge alone is shared; if `Cup = Cdown`, the shared voltage is half the supply, a likely functional failure for upstream timing.
- `#L2761-L2810` — Charge conservation before and after closure gives `Vdd+ = (Cup × Vdd−)/(Cup + Cdown)`, the half-supply result being the equal-capacitance case. The simulation (Figure 9.43) uses 25 nF upstream and 25 nF downstream, plus the same 100 MHz peak. A 1 mΩ switch droops the upstream rail to about 0.5 V and rings at the resonant frequency, while a 1 Ω switch limits the droop to about 16 mV, over a logarithmic sweep from 0.001 Ω to 1 Ω (Figure 9.44).
- `#L2811-L2867` — Figure 9.45 shows the tradeoff: higher switch resistance means less upstream noise but a slower downstream rise. The 100 MHz peak gives a 10 nsec period and a reactive time constant; with Q near 3 equilibrium takes about 3 × 2π = 19 time constants, so the switch RC should be at least ~20× the reactive PDN time constant (Table 9.6). Sequencing several small switches over time is an alternative to a single resistive switch.
- `#L2868-L2929` — The bottom line: target impedance is the primary metric and needs real transient-current knowledge (50% of maximum is a default); PDN current is clock-edge charge smoothed by ODC and package inductance; noise originates in ODC droop that creates `v/L` and `di/dt` (high di/dt from low L is good); impulse, step and resonance cover the fundamentals; characteristic impedance below target gives marginal robustness and all peaks below target gives full robustness; the SCL beats a current source; rogue waves are rare but argue for flat, low-Q profiles; and clock/power gating raises transient current, so power savings must be paid for with lower impedance or accepted risk.
