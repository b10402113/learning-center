# L1 overview

- **Ch1 §1.1 — What is the PDN and why care** — PDN = every interconnect from VRM pads to on-die circuits: board power/ground planes, cables, connectors, all power-supply capacitors (Fig 1.1). Three roles: clean DC power to active devices, low-noise signal return path, EMI mitigation; book focuses on role one. PDN noise closes the eye vertically (voltage noise) and horizontally (jitter), and can cause core setup/hold failures. Measured example: >125 mV Vdd–Vss noise on a microprocessor at 300 MHz, three die locations, two rails (Fig 1.2).
- **Ch1 §1.1 — noise→jitter coupling, 1 ps/mV** — CMOS propagation delay falls as Vds (and Vdd–Vss) rises, so rail noise is direct jitter: high Vdd "pulls in" a clock edge, low Vdd "pushes it out." Measured FPGA clock jitter from PDN noise driven by PRBS gate current (Fig 1.3) gives ≈1 ps jitter per mV of rail noise — the book's rule of thumb. 100 mV pp noise → 100 ps pp jitter; at 2 GHz (500 ps period) that alone consumes the whole timing budget.
- **Ch1 §1.2 — Engineering the PDN: the 5% budget** — Allowed rail noise is roughly ±5% of supply; single-ended CMOS total receiver noise margin is ~15% of signal swing, split equally three ways between reflection, crosstalk, and PDN noise. ADCs and PLLs need <1% PDN noise. Noise must stay under budget from DC to the signal bandwidth, as high as 5–10 GHz. Low-frequency noise is usually VRM noise; above that, the "PDN ecology" (all interconnects die-pads→VRM-pads) presents an impedance profile (Fig 1.4).
- **Ch1 §1.2 — noise = current spectrum × impedance overlap** — Transient gate current flowing through that impedance profile makes die-pad noise regardless of VRM stability (Fig 1.5). Same impedance, slightly different microcode → a current spectral peak lands on a bigger impedance peak → unacceptable noise; noise depends as much on microcode/gate utilization as on hardware. So "passes the boot test" ≠ robust.
- **Ch1 §1.3 — Target impedance Ztarget = ΔVnoise / Imax-transient** — Robust PDN means any code at any frequency with any time signature stays under spec; this sets a max allowable impedance (Fig 1.6 shows a profile under 50 mΩ at all frequencies holding noise under the 5% limit). Example: ±50 mV spec / 1 A worst-case transient = 50 mΩ. If ΔVnoise or Imax vary with frequency, Ztarget is a function of frequency. It is a figure of merit / starting point; true worst-case needs full transient simulation.
- **Ch1 §1.3 — Ztarget relaxes above a knee frequency** — Worst-case current is not flat: max switching gates take time to turn on, so amplitude rolls off (~−20 dB/decade) above f ≈ 0.35/t_rise. Example: 2 GHz clock (0.5 ns period), 20 cycles to build up → 10 ns rise → knee at 0.35/10 ns = 35 MHz, so Ztarget rises with frequency above 35 MHz (Fig 1.7). Effective rise time can range from one clock edge to 100 clock cycles, so the knee is hard to predict without microcode detail.
- **Ch1 §1.3 — PDN ratio = actual peak Z / Ztarget (risk metric)** — Ratio <1 = low risk; ratio 2 may still be acceptable; ratio 10 is almost surely unacceptable. Lower impedance costs more (more components, tighter assembly rules hurting yield, more board/package layers, more die area for capacitance, exotic materials): "buying insurance." Consumer products may accept higher ratio/lower cost; avionics may pay for ratio <1. Engineering process: best-guess Ztarget → design to it → balance cost vs failure risk.
- **Ch1 §1.4 — Sculpting the impedance profile** — Goal: acceptable impedance from DC to the highest rail-current frequency, engineering all elements together. Full ecology (Fig 1.8): on-die capacitance, optional on-package capacitors, package lead inductance, board vias, board power/ground planes, decoupling capacitors, bulk capacitors, VRM; each maps to a distinct feature of the impedance curve (x-axis in MHz). Isolating elements lets you optimize them semi-independently, which is why PDN design is mostly about interfaces between parts.
- **Ch1 §1.5 — Bottom line (10 points)** — Restates: PDN spans die pads→VRM incl. all components; purposes are clean supply, low-impedance return, EMC mitigation; 5% = 1/3 of the 15% budget each for reflection, crosstalk, PDN; noise = transient current spectrum × impedance profile; jitter sensitivity ≈1 ps/mV; die-pad impedance profile is the key quality metric DC→highest frequency; Ztarget bounds worst-case noise; PDN ratio >10 is high risk; sculpting needs element + interaction optimization.

# L2 detail

- `#L1-L18` — Chapter 1 opening: §1.1 defines the PDN as all interconnects from VRM pads to on-die circuits — board power/ground planes, cables, connectors, and all power-supply capacitors; Figure 1.1 is a typical computer motherboard with multiple VRMs and active devices, PDN spanning VRM pads to die circuits.
- `#L19-L43` — Three purposes of the PDN (clean low-noise DC power, low-noise signal return path, EMI mitigation without radiated emissions); the book scopes to role one. Failure modes: vertical eye collapse from voltage noise, horizontal spreading → jitter and reduced eye opening, core setup/hold errors. Tip: bad PDN design raises BER via vertical noise and jitter and can violate setup/hold.
- `#L44-L61` — PDN noise adds to the TX signal and to the RX voltage reference, eating noise margin from other sources. Figure 1.2: measured Vdd–Vss noise on a microprocessor at 300 MHz clock, three die locations and two rails, >125 mV present; often a large fraction appears superimposed on the RX signal.
- `#L62-L101` — Voltage noise → timing: CMOS propagation delay depends on instantaneous rail voltage (higher Vds → larger channel fields → shorter delay), so high Vdd pulls in and low Vdd pushes out a clock edge. Figure 1.3: measured clock jitter on a high-end FPGA test chip whose clock net shared Vdd with PRBS-switching gates. Sensitivity ≈1 ps jitter per mV noise (a rough estimate for many devices); 100 mV pp → 100 ps pp, versus only 500 ps period at 2 GHz.
- `#L102-L138` — §1.2: rail noise must stay under a spec, roughly ±5% of supply; single-ended CMOS receiver noise margin ≈15% of signal swing, split equally among reflection, crosstalk, and PDN noise — the origin of the 5% spec. ADCs/PLLs need <1%; limits hold DC to 5–10 GHz signal bandwidth. Root-cause order: at low frequency noise is mostly VRM noise, so pick a low-noise VRM first, but interconnect impedance still creates die-pad noise — that whole network is the "PDN ecology."
- `#L139-L186` — The ecology produces an impedance profile at the die pads (Fig 1.4); transient current through it makes noise independent of VRM stability. Figure 1.5 pairs the core-rail current spectrum with the impedance profile: left side peaks miss, noise acceptable; right side, a slightly different microcode frequency puts a current peak on a larger impedance peak → noise over limit. Tip: noise depends as much on microcode/gate utilization as on the impedance profile.
- `#L187-L218` — §1.3: boot/test-suite success can be luck; a different suite switching more gates at another loop frequency over an impedance peak can fail the same product. "Robust" = any code, any frequency, any time signature, worst-case noise still under spec. Worst-case current + noise spec together bound the max allowable impedance, called the target impedance, derived from [1] (Smith et al., IEEE Trans. Adv. Packag. 1999).
- `#L219-L267` — Ztarget = ΔVnoise / Imax-transient, where ΔVnoise is max specified rail noise and Imax-transient the worst-case transient current under any operation; both may be frequency-dependent, making Ztarget frequency-dependent. Example: ±50 mV / 1 A = 50 mΩ. Only full transient simulation captures the true worst case; Ztarget is a figure of merit and starting point. Figure 1.6: profile below 50 mΩ at all frequencies with resulting rail noise under the 5% limit under large clocked transient current.
- `#L268-L303` — Worst-case current is not flat in frequency: max amplitude rolls off at high frequency as the maximum number of gates turns on. Effective rise time spans one clock edge to 100 clock cycles; example 2 GHz (0.5 ns period) × 20 cycles = 10 ns, so rolloff starts at 0.35/10 ns = 35 MHz, dropping −20 dB/decade, so Ztarget increases with frequency (Fig 1.7, 50 mV / 1 A). Estimating the knee needs microcode detail; engineering judgment bridges the gap to a cost-effective design.
- `#L304-L346` — Design process: (1) best-guess Ztarget from chip function/applications, (2) make engineering decisions to approach that profile, (3) trade implementation cost against field-failure risk. PDN ratio = actual PDN impedance / target impedance: <1 low risk, 2 possibly acceptable, 10 almost surely unacceptable. Lower impedance costs more (components, tighter assembly rules hurting yield, more board/package layers, more die capacitance area, expensive materials) — "buy insurance." Consumer vs avionics choose different cost/risk balances.
- `#L347-L381` — §1.4: engineer an acceptable impedance profile from DC to the highest rail-current frequency by sculpting the whole ecology together. Figure 1.8 schematic: on-die capacitance, optional on-package capacitors, package lead inductance, board vias, board power/ground planes, decoupling capacitors, bulk capacitors, VRM; bottom of figure maps each design feature to a specific impedance-curve feature (x scale in MHz). Isolating element functions allows independent optimization provided interfaces are respected — hence PDN design is largely about interfaces. PI engineer balances cost, risk, performance, schedule.
- `#L382-L419` — §1.5 "The Bottom Line," ten numbered takeaways: PDN scope (die pads→VRM and all components); its three purposes; 5% = 1/3 of the 15% budget each for reflection, crosstalk, PDN; noise = transient current spectrum × impedance; jitter sensitivity ≈1 ps/mV (varies by chip/technology node); die-pad impedance profile is the top quality metric from DC to highest switching frequency; Ztarget keeps worst-case noise in spec; PDN ratio >10 is high risk; sculpting needs optimizing elements and their interactions; closing "if you care about PDN design, this book is for you."
