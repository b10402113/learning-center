# L1 overview

- **10.11 Switched capacitor load model** — Replaces the crude resistive load with a realistic switched-capacitor model that borrows a fraction of the on-die capacitance (the switch factor) and draws charge impulses each clock cycle. A fictitious inductor L1 plus L_tau/R_tau pulse-shapes the current so an impulse completes just before the clock period expires, and the cubic voltage-dependent leakage source adds damping the spreadsheet cannot capture.
- **10.12 Impulse, step, and resonance response** — Correlates the PRC spreadsheet predictions against time-domain ADS simulation of the three-peak PDN for the three canonical current waveforms. The switched-capacitor load damps the response, giving 47 mV versus a predicted 45 mV impulse droop, 12 A versus 15 A step peaks (q ≈ 2 vs 4), and 903 mV versus a predicted 922 mV resonant p-p, while an ideal current source balloons to 1.588 V p-p.
- **10.13 Individual Q-factors in both domains** — Isolates each of the four damping mechanisms (bump-loop resistance, ODC ESR, leakage, switched-capacitor load) one at a time and confirms a single q-factor story in the spreadsheet, frequency domain, and time domain. All four together give a 91 mΩ peak and q = 2.5, matching the PRC exactly; load damping is the strongest single mechanism (200 mΩ), while board-cap bump-loop loss is the weakest.
- **10.14 Rise time and stimulation of impedance peaks** — Shows that a current edge only excites a resonance when its bandwidth 0.35/t_r exceeds that peak's frequency, so 7.9 ns, 177 ns, and 2.69 µs rise times are the thresholds for the 44 MHz, 2 MHz, and 130 kHz peaks. Beyond roughly five resonant time constants (about one period), the droop collapses — the 20 ns ramp example erases the first droop entirely and 17 ns cuts the droop to 96 mV.
- **10.15 Improvements: loop inductance and selective MLCC values** — Starts from a PDN whose 91 mΩ Bandini peak is 15× the 6.3 mΩ target and works the inductance side of the problem. Mounted-capacitor inductance dominates the 132 pH loop (84 pH of it), so going from 10 to 40 distributed-value caps flattens the mid-band and removes the second peak, but only drops the Bandini peak to 74 mΩ before package and board-via inductance dominate.
- **10.16 A better SMPS model** — Argues the VRM impedance peak near 100 kHz is an artifact of a naive linear model, since a well-compensated switching supply is neither that tall nor that high-Q and its behavior is time-varying and nonlinear. A single artificial 10 mΩ R_damp next to the bulk capacitor plus 10 nH limits the low-frequency peak without distorting the >1 MHz band where power integrity work happens.
- **10.17 On-package decoupling (OPD) capacitors** — Reduces the Bandini Mountain by cutting the bump-loop inductance that resonates with the ODC; mounted loop inductance is the most important property, ESR is second (and often the dominant loss), and capacitance only needs to be about five times the ODC. Two 220 nF package caps move the peak from 62 to 75 MHz and from 74 to 53 mΩ (Z0 = 22 mΩ, q = 2.41), and dropping board caps from 40 to 10 optimized values actually lowers the peak to 51 mΩ (q = 2.04) because the extra inductance forces current through the lossier package path.
- **10.18 Transient response before and after improvement** — Re-evaluates the improved PDN in time domain across three time scales: the first droop falls from 218 to 186 mV, the second droop disappears, and the VRM is now overdamped so the third droop is a gentle 1.5 µs dip. The long-term 31 mV offset is true DC IR drop (10 A × 3.1 mΩ, 312 mW), and the resonant p-p drops from 480 to 285 mV — still five times the 50 mV tolerance.
- **10.19 Re-examining transient current assumptions** — Questions the worst-case 8 A repetitive transient: real loads repeat less at high frequency, so a 5–9 A band (4 A transient) is defensible and raises the resonant-band target impedance from 6.3 to 12.6 mΩ, cutting the droop to 165 mV. Pipeline fill behavior lengthens the effective current ramp, and a deliberate 17 ns ramp (five time constants) yields a 96 mV droop.
- **10.20 Practical limitations: risk, performance, and cost** — Tallies the journey from a 497 mV (50% of VDD) droop to 96 mV and names the residual risk explicitly, noting that consumer markets may accept it while aviation, automotive, and medical may not. The last resort is raising the VRM voltage 50 mV, which costs 10% dynamic power (∝ V²) and 15% leakage (∝ V³) plus thermal and reliability margin.
- **10.21 Reverse engineering the PDN from measurements** — Uses Altera FPGA test-chip data and the fact that impedance, current, and voltage are mutually derivable to reconstruct the PDN from a handful of scope traces. Covers four-point Kelvin sensing, Vdd−Vss subtraction (scope math or transmission-line subtraction), 50 Ω termination, and the probe-resistance divider error, then extracts 30 nC/cycle, 27 nF switched of 313 nF total (9% switch factor), 74 pH, Z0 = 15.4 mΩ, and 4.35 mΩ bump-loop resistance.
- **10.22 Simulation-to-measurement correlation** — Matches impulse (105 vs 104 mV), step (146 vs 150 mV), and resonant (577 vs 584 mV p-p) responses, with the bump-loop loss now the dominant damping mechanism and a combined q of 1.75. The initial resonance sim overshot, which was diagnosed as too little loss; adding 0.6 mΩ in the ball/socket position raised the loop to 4.35 mΩ and reproduced the measured waveform.
- **10.23 Summary of simulated and measured features** — Assembles all inputs and extracted terms into a PRC-style table: 266 MHz and 533 MHz targets of 6.9 and 3.4 mΩ sit far below the 15.4 mΩ Z0 and 25.9 mΩ peak, confirming the FPGA was pushed past its intended operating region. Final q is 1.68, not the 3.9 originally published, because leakage and load loss were omitted.
- **10.24 The bottom line** — Ten closing rules: PDN design is peak management, every peak is frequency + Z0 + q, three current waveforms tell the whole story, the spreadsheet gets you remarkably close, board caps should "do no harm," OPD is cost-effective, and a peak can approach but never go below its characteristic impedance.
