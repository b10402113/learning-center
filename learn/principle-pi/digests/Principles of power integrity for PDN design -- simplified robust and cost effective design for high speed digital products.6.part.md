# L1 overview

- **3.1 Why measure low impedance** — A robust PDN is designed to a target impedance profile from DC to the bandwidth of the fastest signals, and that target can be above 1 Ω in some applications but below 1 mΩ in others. Measurement does three jobs: it verifies a manufactured part meets spec, it validates the simulation tool and the process of turning geometry into a model, and it is the final check that the whole PDN ecology is close to target. When the check fails, measurement is also the fastest debug path.
- **3.2 The V/I definition** — Impedance is the complex ratio of the voltage across a two-terminal device to the current through it, measured with a sine excitation at one frequency. This is the definition inside circuit simulators and low-end impedance analyzers. Its practical ceiling comes from having to measure current directly, so above roughly 100 MHz the reflection definition is usually more cost effective.
- **3.3 Impedance from reflection** — A signal on an interconnect is a propagating voltage difference plus a current-loop wave front, with a direction of propagation and a direction of circulation. Any change in instantaneous impedance creates a reflected wave, and the reflection coefficient relates incident and reflected voltage to the impedances on both sides. In the frequency domain this gives an equivalent, more general definition of input impedance that works to very high frequency.
- **3.4 Measuring with a VNA** — A vector network analyzer separates incident and reflected waves at a port, measuring phase as well as amplitude, against a calibrated 50 Ω source and coax. An oscilloscope only sees the total voltage between two conductors and cannot tell which way the waves are traveling. S-parameters package those ratios: S11 is the reflection coefficient (return loss), S21 the forward transmission (insertion loss), and a little complex algebra turns S11 into DUT impedance.
- **3.5 Two leads in a DIP** — A ceramic DIP soldered to an SMA fixture, measured from 10 MHz to 1 GHz, shows how S11 converts frequency-by-frequency into impedance. Because |S11| sits near 0 dB, essentially all the impedance information lives in the phase of S11, and the phase of S11 is not the phase of the impedance. On a log-log plot the leads look capacitive, turn inductive near 800 MHz, and fit a single 4.8 pF capacitor — the start of measurement-based modeling, or "hacking the interconnect."
- **3.6 A small wire loop** — A 1-inch copper loop measured one-port should look like an inductor below the frequency where its 3-inch circumference is 1/10 wavelength, about 400 MHz. Bogatin's Rule #9 says to anticipate the result before measuring, then explain any difference. The extracted R and L raise three questions: how much inductance is fixture, whether resistance rises from skin depth, and why the data is noisy below 0.1 Ω.
- **3.7 Limits of one-port measurements** — For 1 Ω the reflection magnitude is only about 4% away from −1, and for 0.1 Ω it is 0.996, or about −0.035 dB, which is at the reproducibility floor of a calibration; below 0.1 Ω the S11 method falls apart. A lossless fixture transmission line adds phase that distorts the extracted L and R even when it is perfect, and short fractions of an inch already swamp low-inductance DUTs. Microprobes calibrated to the tip remove fixture length but add dry-contact resistance from 0.05 Ω (gold-gold) up to 2 Ω (oxidized), with residual tip loop inductance of 20 pH/mil, giving a practical floor near 0.05 Ω and 0.1 nH.
- **3.8 The four-point Kelvin technique** — Contact resistance comes from current constricting through tiny real contact areas, roughly 20 mΩ for gold-gold at 100 g and much worse with oxide. Lord Kelvin's fix is to separate the current-carrying leads from the voltage-sensing leads so the high-impedance voltmeter's contact resistance drops out of the measurement. This is the ancestor of DC low-resistance measurement and of the high-frequency two-port method.
- **3.9 The two-port low-impedance technique** — One VNA port drives a current loop into the DUT and an independently connected second port measures the small voltage across it, so shared contact impedance no longer corrupts the result. For a DUT much below 50 Ω the reflection is near −1, the net DUT current is twice the incident current, and S21 reduces to Z_DUT/25 Ω. The exact expression holds for any impedance, but the approximation is only safe above about −20 dB, and the dB arithmetic uses a factor of 20 with 1 Ω as reference (dBΩ).
- **3.10 Two-port wire loop** — Re-measuring the loop with two ports shows S11 is nearly useless while S21 is clean, and Z = 25 Ω × S21 below 1 Ω until the approximation breaks above 10 Ω. Comparing the two setups isolates a 5 nH difference, which is the series inductance of the one-port SMA fixture. The two-port resistance is about 8 mΩ at 10 MHz and rises with frequency, which the next section shows is largely a fixture phase artifact.
- **3.11 Accounting for fixture artifacts** — A second-order model adds a lossless transmission line on each side of the simple R–L DUT, giving four parameters (R, L, Z0, TD); the fitted values 0.005 Ω, 23 nH, 50 Ω, and 42 ps reproduce the measured peak and roll-off near 600 MHz. The fixture's negative phase shift rotates the loop's near-90° inductive impedance toward the real axis, creating the illusion of a frequency-dependent series resistance. No skin-depth explanation is needed, and the model is sufficient to about 300 MHz with the simplest topology that fits.
- **3.12 Measuring a via** — A 50 mil wire shorting a microstrip to the return plane makes a two-terminal via DUT, measured through the microstrip-and-SMA fixture. The same second-order RL-plus-line model fits the full 2 GHz sweep with R = 0.0007 Ω, L = 0.28 nH, Z0 = 50 Ω, and TD = 210 ps, so the apparent frequency dependence is again a fixture artifact. A geometry-based check gives 0.02 mΩ DC, 0.7 mΩ extracted, a plausible skin-depth factor at 10 MHz, and 0.29 nH versus 0.28 nH for inductance.
- **3.13 MLCC capacitor on a board** — A capacitor's intrinsic impedance cannot be separated from its mounting, so the useful measurement is the mounted profile. On an X2Y four-layer two-port fixture board, S21 converts to an impedance that fits a simple series RLC with 0.01 Ω, 175 nF, and 0.41 nH up to about 400 MHz; beyond that, a 0.7-inch, 3.5 Ω transmission-line fixture is needed on each side. The extracted 0.41 nH is ESL, set by mounting rather than the part, and 20 mils of surface trace instead of via-in-pad raises it from 0.2 nH to 0.9 nH.
- **3.14 Advanced: on-die capacitance** — The two-port method also covers the wide dynamic range of on-die capacitance, probed at C4 bumps, through one package ball pair, and through two ball pairs. A 300 kHz to 3 GHz sweep gives an RLC fit of 81.5 nF and 2 pH, with 70 mΩ of composite spreading and shunt resistance; the same capacitance appears when probed through the package. Two pitfalls dominate: uncalibrated probe extensions shift phase between real and imaginary impedance, and widely spaced ports produce spatial attenuation, so the measured Z21 is a transfer impedance that can look artificially low.
- **3.15 Bottom line** — Eleven numbered takeaways restate the V/I and reflection definitions, the ~0.1 Ω one-port ceiling, the two-port Kelvin analogy, Z ≈ 25 Ω × S21, the fixture-delay and insertion-loss artifacts, and the use of the method for capacitor ESR/ESL and on-die capacitance. The recurring theme is that the two-port measurement is an insertion-loss measurement and must be interpreted with the fixture in the model.

# L2 detail

- `#L1-L26` — Chapter 3 opens on measuring low impedance: the design target is an impedance profile from DC to the bandwidth of the fastest signals, ranging from above 1 Ω to below 1 mΩ. Measurement verifies a manufactured part, validates the simulation tool and the geometry-to-model translation, serves as the final PDN acceptance test, and speeds debug when it fails; the challenge is 1 mΩ to beyond 1 GHz.
- `#L27-L66` — Section 3.2 gives the V/I definition: drive a sine voltage across the DUT, measure the complex current amplitude and phase, and take Z(f) = V(f)/I(f). This is the basis of low-end impedance analyzers and circuit simulation, but it is limited by how high a frequency the current can be measured directly, so above about 100 MHz the reflection definition is more cost effective.
- `#L67-L130` — Section 3.3 turns to reflection: a signal is a propagating voltage difference plus a current loop with a direction of propagation and a direction of circulation, and it reacts to the instantaneous impedance at each step. A positive wave traveling left to right carries a clockwise current loop; a change in impedance launches a reflected wave. From the V/I definition on both sides and the boundary conditions, the reflection coefficient ρ = Vr/Vi is derived.
- `#L131-L180` — The reflection coefficient relates the two impedances, and rearranging gives Z2 from the known Z1 and measured ρ. The relation holds in both time and frequency domains; in the frequency domain voltages, ρ, and impedances are all complex, so with a 50 Ω source the input impedance of any two-terminal DUT follows directly. For a discrete ideal element this is just the element impedance.
- `#L181-L231` — For an extended DUT the incident wave also reflects off the fixture and every discontinuity, bouncing multiple times. All those same-frequency sine waves add to another sine at the same frequency, so the measured reflection carries the total integrated input impedance looking into the DUT plus fixture. This general definition is the basis of a measurement method that works to very high frequency, and the instrument that performs it is the vector network analyzer.
- `#L232-L298` — Section 3.4 explains the VNA: "vector" means it measures phase as well as amplitude by separating waves traveling in opposite directions in the same interconnect, with a 50 Ω source calibrated to the end of the coax. Connections are called ports, and each port's impedance as seen by the DUT is a precision 50 Ω. Unlike a scope, which only reads total voltage between two conductors and cannot tell propagation direction, the VNA resolves the two waves; Figure 3.7 shows the one-port schematic, including the return path and reference plane.
- `#L299-L374` — Inside the port, the source drives a precision 50 Ω resistor and coax, so the incident voltage is exactly half the source voltage by the divider, and it is inferred from a separate Vref divider. The internal voltmeter reads Vtotal = Vincident + Vreflected, and because Vincident is known independently the reflected wave is extracted; anything returning is absorbed by the 50 Ω source resistor. S-parameters index every out/in port combination, S11 is the reflection coefficient or return loss, |S21| in dB is insertion loss, and Z is recovered from S11 by complex algebra to whatever frequency the VNA reaches, above 50 GHz.
- `#L375-L430` — Section 3.5 measures two adjacent leads of a ceramic DIP soldered to an SMA fixture, sweeping 10 MHz to 1 GHz with the package shelf open and pins unconnected, so the low-frequency input looks open. S11 is converted to impedance frequency by frequency with Z = 50(1+S11)/(1−S11), doable in SPICE or Excel. Magnitude and phase of Z are related to those of S11 in a complicated way, and since |S11| is nearly 0 dB, all the real information is in the phase of S11 — which must not be confused with the phase of the impedance.
- `#L431-L472` — Impedance versus frequency is easiest to read on log-log axes: an ideal capacitor is a −1 slope, an ideal inductor +1. The DIP leads are capacitive at low frequency and turn inductive near 800 MHz, and a single 4.8 pF capacitor matches the low-frequency data in QUCS or ADS. Fitting an ideal topology to measured S-parameters is measurement-based modeling, sometimes called hacking the interconnect, and it yields a scalable model for exploring what-if questions and root causes.
- `#L473-L557` — Section 3.6 repeats the exercise on a 1-inch diameter copper loop measured one-port with only one of its two SMAs connected. Bogatin's Rule #9 says to anticipate the result: a loop should look inductive, and the model should break down when the 3-inch circumference is about 1/10 wavelength, which puts the limit near 400 MHz. Below that the impedance should rise with frequency and resistance should be small with a slight skin-depth rise; the extracted data indeed looks like an ideal inductor, modeled as R + jωL with R and L pulled from the real and imaginary parts at each frequency.
- `#L558-L620` — Section 3.7 starts the limitations. If the DUT is 1 Ω, |S11| is only about 4% away from −1, still easy to distinguish, but at 0.1 Ω it is 0.996 — roughly −0.035 dB, near the reproducibility noise of a calibration. Resistance measurements near 0.1 Ω are still fairly consistent, but below that the S11 method has fallen apart.
- `#L621-L676` — The fixture adds a second artifact: even a perfect lossless line shifts the phase of S11 and distorts the extracted impedance, shown by simulating 0 to 1 inch of line in 0.25-inch steps around a 0.01 Ω, 1 nH DUT. Extracted inductance grows with fixture length and both L and R appear frequency dependent, so DUT inductance cannot be separated from fixture inductance; de-embedding is possible but harder as fixture contribution grows. The fix is a minimum-length fixture such as a microprobe calibrated to the tip, which then introduces dry-contact resistance: 0.05 Ω gold-to-gold up to 2 Ω on oxidized solder or copper, plus residual tip loop inductance of 20 pH/mil, so 5 mils of scrub means 100 pH. Typical probe floor is about 0.05 Ω and 0.1 nH, which above 100 MHz limits capacitance to below 0.01 µF and inductance to above 0.1 nH — bad for 0.001 µF on-die capacitance and 0.01 nH vias. A different technique, derived from the four-point Kelvin method, is needed.
- `#L677-L758` — Section 3.8 covers contact resistance: dry metal-to-metal contact conducts only through tiny actual contact areas, creating constriction or spreading resistance that falls as contact area and pressure rise. Gold-gold gives about 20 mΩ at 100 g, oxide pushes it past 100 mΩ, and oxidized solder, aluminum, or copper can reach 2 Ω. This dominates series-resistance measurements below 1 Ω. The text sketches Lord Kelvin's career — transatlantic cable analysis, the Telegrapher's Equations, the temperature scale — then contrasts two-wire measurement, where the same leads carry current and sense voltage so contact resistance is included, with the four-wire Kelvin method, where independent voltage leads plus a high-impedance voltmeter keep contact resistance out of the result.
- `#L759-L830` — Section 3.9 builds the two-port technique. One port drives the current loop and the second, independently contacted port measures the voltage across the DUT, so contact paths are not shared. With a DUT far below 50 Ω, port 2 is shunted to near nothing, ρ ≈ −1, the reflected current loop adds in the same circulation direction, and the DUT current is twice the incident current while its voltage nearly collapses — exactly a shorted line. That small voltage propagates to port 2, and S21 = V2/Vincident reduces to Z_DUT/25 Ω, so S21 is a direct, complex measure of impedance.
- `#L831-L896` — Practical dB arithmetic: convert |S21| in dB by Z = 25 × 10^(S21/20), so −20 dB is 2.5 Ω, −40 dB is 0.25 Ω, and −60 dB is 25 mΩ; the first-order form is not valid above about −20 dB because the DUT is no longer much less than 50 Ω. Impedance in dB behaves like an amplitude, so conversion uses 20 rather than 10, with 1 Ω as reference — properly dBΩ, though industry just says dB. Since 28 dB = 20 log(25 Ω), Z[dB] ≈ 28 dB + S21[dB]: −40 dB gives −12 dB = 0.25 Ω and −60 dB gives −32 dB = 0.025 Ω.
- `#L897-L950` — The general case is derived from a lumped model of two ports across the same shunted DUT, with V2 given by the divider of source voltage and DUT impedance. To convert to S21 the incident voltage must be identified: it is set only by the source and the divider of source resistance and internal line, independent of the DUT. The text advises checking V2 for zero, 25 Ω, and infinite DUT impedance in both the lumped and transmission-line pictures.
- `#L951-L989` — With S21 = V2/Vincident, rearranging gives an exact expression for DUT impedance valid across the whole range, reducing to 25 Ω × S21 when the DUT is very small. The caution is numerical: large DUT impedance — small capacitors at low frequency or large inductors at high frequency — causes the same trouble S11 has at small impedance. This exact relation, plus higher source power, averaging, and a narrow IF filter, reaches a −90 dB S21 noise floor, about 1 mΩ, to beyond 1 GHz on any two-port VNA, provided port connections are careful and the section 3.11 artifact is avoided.
- `#L990-L1064` — Section 3.10 re-measures the 1-inch loop with two ports, wiring both SMA signal pins to one end and both returns to the other, over the same band. S11 carries little accurate information for this low-impedance DUT while S21 is clean; Z = 25 Ω × S21 agrees below 1 Ω and the simple relation degrades above 10 Ω. A series R–L interpretation gives R and L, and since S21 is lower than the one-port result by about 5 nH, that difference is the SMA fixture's series inductance. The two-port resistance is about 8 mΩ at 10 MHz and rises with frequency in both setups.
- `#L1065-L1120` — Section 3.11 frames interpretation as building an equivalent circuit and comparing simulation with measurement — "hacking." Agreement only shows the model is consistent with the DUT, not that it is the true explanation, so many consistency checks are worthwhile. The second-order model adds identical lossless transmission lines on both sides of the RL circuit to represent the fixture, giving four parameters: R, L, Z0, and TD.
- `#L1121-L1171` — The fixture's main action is extra phase in S21, which moves imaginary impedance into the real part and grows with frequency. Parameter values are found by manual fit — approximate Z0 as 50 Ω, then adjust TD until the model's impedance matches — resulting in R = 0.005 Ω, L = 23 nH, Z0 = 50 Ω, TD = 42 ps. That model reproduces the peak and drop-off near 600 MHz that a plain RL model misses, and its constant ideal R still produces a simulated real part that looks frequency dependent.
- `#L1172-L1208` — The mechanism is spelled out: each short line adds negative phase that increases with frequency; the loop's inductive impedance sits near 90°, and rotating it toward zero raises the real part, which was wrongly read as resistance. No skin-depth effect is required, and the real part is a property of the whole circuit, not the R element alone. Adding a small shunt capacitor is optional, an RL plus fixture model suffices to about 300 MHz, and the general rule is to start with the simplest model and add complexity only as higher-frequency data demands.
- `#L1209-L1273` — Section 3.12 measures a via: a 50 mil hole drilled through the signal trace of a 50 Ω microstrip on a two-layer board, with a 50 mil wire soldered from trace to return plane. Microstrip and SMAs are the fixture, and the two-port S21 converts to impedance whose RL interpretation looks like falling inductance and rising resistance. The same second-order topology fits with R = 0.0007 Ω, L = 0.28 nH, Z0 = 50 Ω, TD = 210 ps, reproducing the data across the full 2 GHz bandwidth — so the apparent frequency dependence is fixture phase, not the via.
- `#L1274-L1324` — A physical sanity check uses R = ρ·Len/(πr²) and the partial self-inductance of a rod, with ρ = 0.7 × 10⁻⁶ Ω-in, length 0.064 in, radius 0.025 in. Predicted DC resistance is 0.02 mΩ versus 0.7 mΩ extracted, roughly 35× — plausibly noise floor plus skin depth, since at 10 MHz the 0.8 mil skin depth shrinks the effective area from 0.002 in² to 0.00013 in², a 15× rise. The actual R rises as √f but the fixture artifact makes it look like f². Predicted 0.29 nH total inductance matches the 0.28 nH extracted; the section closes by noting that two-port results still need fixture-aware interpretation.
- `#L1325-L1380` — Section 3.13 addresses capacitors: a part's intrinsic impedance cannot be separated from how it is mounted, so the meaningful measurement is the mounted profile, which stays straightforward. An X2Y four-layer fixture board feeds planes from edge SMAs to central mounting pads, and moving the capacitor between pads changes the measured impedance. The measured S21 converts to an impedance matching a series RLC with R = 0.01 Ω, C = 175 nF, and L = 0.41 nH, an excellent fit up to about 400 MHz.
- `#L1381-L1429` — Above 400 MHz the finite fixture matters, modeled as a short uniform transmission line on each side. The interconnect is about 0.7 inches per side; length is hardcoded and only Z0 is fitted, with 3.5 Ω giving good agreement — reasonable because the SMA-to-capacitor connection is a wide plane. Residual mismatch is hard to attribute between real capacitor behavior and unmodeled parasitics, which is the argument for building fixtures that add the fewest artifacts: as short as possible and as close to 50 Ω as possible.
- `#L1430-L1489` — The extracted 175 nF is about 25% below the 220 nF nameplate, not unexpected for bulk capacitance. R = 0.01 Ω is the ESR that dominates damping of parallel resonances. L = 0.41 nH is the ESL, not intrinsic but set by mounting, stackup, and spreading inductance, and it is the single most important capacitor design term because it sets the minimum capacitor count; here the X2Y geometry and near via-in-pad mounting keep it low. Two otherwise similar capacitors measured with different trace lengths gave 0.2 nH for near via-in-pad versus 0.9 nH when 20 mils of surface trace connects pads to vias.
- `#L1490-L1562` — Section 3.14 extends two-port measurement to on-die capacitance in three configurations: bare-die bumps, a single package ball pair, and two ball pairs, each with some uncalibrated fixture extension. Two port pairs reach deeper into the DUT and remove series impedance, though insertion loss and transimpedance can degrade the result. Probing Vdd/Vss C4 bumps with the VNA calibrated to the microprobe tips, the residual tip is modeled as a 50 Ω line of about 1 ps, corresponding to roughly 10 mils of placement variation and distortion. The sweep runs 300 kHz to 3 GHz, S21 converts to Z_DUT, and an optimized series RLC fit yields 81.5 nF and 2 pH of very low spreading inductance.
- `#L1563-L1646` — Impedance can also be extracted point by point, taking the real part as R and deriving C or L from the imaginary part. C is 81 nF at low frequency and starts varying above 40 MHz as inductance takes over; L reaches 2 pH above 1 GHz, near the noise floor; R shows frequency dependence from possible SiO₂ dielectric loss, leakage, or fixture onset. R = 70 mΩ combines Vdd/Vss grid spreading resistance with the capacitor's shunt resistance in a distributed 3-D network. Above 100 MHz the network looks nearly resistive, and between two bump pairs the −50 dB transfer appears as insertion loss: Z21 is a transfer impedance, and spatial attenuation must never be read as low impedance.
- `#L1647-L1752` — Probing through the package raises the DUT-boundary question: same-ball-pair probing includes the balls and initial vias in series, while two ball pairs start the DUT at the package planes and treat the rest as fixture. Low-frequency impedance is the on-die capacitance either way, 79.5 nF for this die. At high frequency the package (higher L, lower R) and die (lower L, higher R) act in parallel, and a simple RLC fit near the self-resonance uses 24 mΩ and 0.28 nH. Rising ESR is explained by current traveling less far on-die and through fewer shunt resistors.
- `#L1753-L1813` — Adding a 1 ps fixture line to the extracted R/L/C shows the resistance increase above 1 GHz could come from microprobe phase, while the rise starting at 10 MHz is real and neither L nor C is very fixture sensitive. A 2 ps shorter probe would produce the observed 1 GHz drop, consistent with tip scrub differences between calibration and measurement. The second package measurement uses different solder balls, removing ball and initial-via inductance and probing the package cavity shunted by the die grid.
- `#L1814-L1888` — Measured SRF inductance is 0.28 nH from one ball pair and 0.12 nH from two; the 0.16 nH difference is balls, initial vias, and package-plane spreading. About 160 pH per probe path acts as extra fixture inductance, modeled as TD = 2 ps (100 pH per side) including an assumed −1 ps tip non-reproducibility. That delay rotates imaginary impedance into the real part and reproduces the >1 GHz resistance, so any real part there is artifact and the 600 MHz glitches may be artifacts. ESR is package-dominated below 100 MHz and die-grid-dominated above 500 MHz, so damping at the 100 MHz resonance is less than bump-level resistance suggests, and accurate >1 GHz representation needs a 3-D model.
- `#L1889-L1932` — The transimpedance / spatial-attenuation mechanism generalizes: widely spaced package balls attenuate the injected signal through inductive and resistive filtering, and at PCB level widely separated ports see attenuation from discrete capacitors between them. Several products have claimed low impedance or low inductance from exactly this setup, but the PDN cannot deliver power at that artificially low value. These two problems — fixture phase rotation and spatial attenuation — must be considered in every two-port measurement.
- `#L1933-L1993` — Section 3.15 lists eleven bottom lines: V/I is universal but not unique; the reflection definition is equally valid to high frequency; one-port VNA accuracy stops near 0.1 Ω; a few ideal elements can match real structures; the two-port method is the Kelvin four-wire analog and reaches very low impedance to very high frequency; Z ≈ 25 Ω × S21; fixture delay must be de-embedded or modeled; two-port is really insertion loss so spatial separation can make impedance look too small; the technique extracts capacitor C, ESR, and ESL; it also measures on-die capacitance by RLC fit or per-frequency algebra; and die-through-package interpretation is best done with a 3-D model.
