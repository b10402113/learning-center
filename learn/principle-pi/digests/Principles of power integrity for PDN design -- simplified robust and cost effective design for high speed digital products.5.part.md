# L1 overview

- **2.1 Why impedance** — The voltage a PDN delivers is judged by its average plus AC ripple, and that ripple comes from the die's current spectrum flowing through the PDN's interconnect impedance. Because it is impossible to test every current waveform a user might run, the PDN's impedance profile becomes a surrogate for design acceptability. That makes impedance the central PDN design metric: design reduces to hitting a target impedance, so understanding how component choice and physical layout shape impedance is the core of the craft.

- **2.2 Impedance in the frequency domain** — Impedance is always V/I, valid in both time and frequency domains, but for ideal C and L elements it collapses to simple complex expressions only in the frequency domain. The `j` term makes impedance complex, giving magnitude and phase (or real part and reactance); a single capacitor falls 20 dB per decade and a single inductor rises 20 dB per decade. This is why PDN impedance is plotted as log impedance versus log frequency, where the slopes read directly as capacitive or inductive behavior.

- **2.3 Calculating or simulating impedance** — Hand calculation becomes tedious as elements multiply, but SPICE and SPICE-like simulators (free QUCS, or Keysight ADS) compute the impedance of any linear, time-invariant network. The trick to an impedance analyzer is a 1 A constant-current AC source: the resulting complex voltage is numerically the complex impedance, magnitude and phase included. A 1 GΩ DC path to ground keeps the simulator from failing on open or capacitive loads, capping the measurable impedance at 1 GΩ — fine for every PDN case.

- **2.4 Real components vs. ideal elements** — Ideal elements exist only in simulation; real components carry parasitics from geometry, material, and Maxwell's equations, so a "capacitor" may behave nothing like an ideal one over parts of its range. A measured 0603 MLCC matches a 182 nF ideal capacitor only up to about 3 MHz, but a series RLC model with R = 18 mΩ, C = 182 nF, L = 1.3 nH tracks it to the 200 MHz measurement bandwidth. The lesson: modest combinations of ideal elements approximate even complicated real structures remarkably well, and only measurement tells you how far the model holds.

- **2.5 The series RLC circuit** — The series RLC is the workhorse model of a real capacitor, and its profile has three regimes: capacitive at low frequency (−90°), inductive at high frequency (+90°), and resistive at the self-resonant frequency (SRF) where the reactances cancel. Capacitance sets only the low-frequency impedance and inductance only the high-frequency end, so the two ends can be tuned independently. SRF is 1/(2π√(LC)) — 159 MHz for 1 nH and 1 nF — and the reactance at resonance is the circuit's "characteristic impedance" (unrelated to a transmission line's).

- **2.6 The parallel RLC circuit** — In a parallel RLC the roles invert: the inductor shorts at DC and the capacitor shorts at high frequency, so the impedance peaks at the parallel resonant frequency (PRF = 1/(2π√(LC)), 50.3 MHz for 10 nH and 1 nF). Without the resistor the peak would be infinite; the parallel resistance damps it. Peaks in any impedance profile are almost always parallel resonances, which is why this circuit is the key to understanding PDN noise.

- **2.7 Resonant properties: q-factor** — Any series or parallel RLC is summarized by three features: resonant frequency, the min or max impedance at resonance, and the broadness of the dip or peak. The resistor sets the extreme value — R_series is literally the minimum of a series circuit, R_parallel the maximum of a parallel one — while the q-factor (Z0/R, or resonant frequency over half-power bandwidth) measures damping. High q means a sharp dip or tall peak and long time-domain ringing; low q means broad and quickly damped; q = 1/2 is critically damped. Designers push q toward 1 to tame PDN peaks.

- **2.8 Examples of RLC circuits and real capacitors** — Every series or parallel RLC has four figures of merit — resonant frequency, characteristic impedance, q-factor, and min/max impedance — and they should be estimated first for any circuit. A worked 1000 μF tantalum (8 nH, 0.05 Ω) has low q and a broad, flat profile; a 10 μF 1206 MLCC (3 nH, 0.003 Ω) has high q and a sharp resonance; a 1 nF 0402 MLCC (1 nH, 0.16 Ω) has ~50× the resistance but comparable q thanks to a higher characteristic impedance.

- **2.9 The PDN as seen by chip or board** — Whether L and C appear series or parallel — and therefore whether you get a dip or a peak — depends on where you measure. From the board looking through the package, package lead inductance is in series with on-die capacitance: a series RLC with a low-frequency dip, hiding the die's high-frequency behavior. From the die looking out, the on-die capacitance parallels the package inductance: a parallel RLC with a peak, with low-frequency impedance set by lead resistance and high-frequency impedance by on-die metallization. Worked values (Cdie = 50 nF, Lpackage = 0.5 nH, Rleads = 0.01 Ω, Rmetal = 0.005 Ω) predict high q and match simulation.

- **2.10 Transient response** — The impedance profile predicts the voltage noise a current produces at each frequency, but frequency-domain analysis assumes steady state and can miss transient behavior such as rogue waves. Simulating an arbitrary current into a VRM-plus-decoupling network shows ringing at the parallel resonance (25 MHz here), and the ringing frequency is intrinsic to the circuit, not the current edge. Only the ringing amplitude tracks the current's energy at that frequency: a 10 ns rise time gives ~35 MHz bandwidth and strong ringing, while a 30 ns rise cuts the 25 MHz content and quiets the noise.

- **2.11 Advanced topic: the impedance matrix** — Impedance generalizes from two terminals to an n-port network, where an n×n complex, frequency-dependent matrix stores every voltage/current ratio. Diagonal elements are self-impedances (the ordinary two-terminal impedance with all other ports open); off-diagonal elements are transfer (mutual) impedances describing how current into one port creates voltage on another. Transfer impedance is coupling, not an ohmmeter reading between ports — 0 Ω means no coupling, and equality with the self-impedance means the ports are fully coupled.

- **2.12 The bottom line** — Ten summary principles: noise is current spectrum times impedance; impedance is V/I in both domains but simpler in frequency; never confuse measurable real components with simulatable ideal elements; simple RLC combinations model complicated structures; series and parallel RLC are the two essential topologies; every RLC has four figures of merit to estimate first; parallel-resonance peaks (≈ Z0 × q) dominate PDN behavior; ringing frequency follows the peak-impedance frequency; and the impedance matrix extends all of this to multi-terminal circuits via self and transfer impedances.

- `#L63-L72` — Tip: impedance is equally well defined in both domains but simpler in the frequency domain, which is why PDN impedance is displayed and analyzed there.
- `#L73-L92` — Time-domain ideal capacitor: I = C·dV/dt, so Z = V/I depends on the waveform slope; correct but awkward because impedance then depends on the actual voltage shape.
- `#L93-L116` — Frequency domain, where only sine waves exist: ideal capacitor impedance is 1/(jωC) and ideal inductor impedance is jωL, giving much simpler forms and faster answers.
- `#L117-L122` — Tip: though performance is judged in time, a shortcut through the frequency domain often gets the answer faster — the main reason to use it.
- `#L123-L157` — The j term makes impedance complex (magnitude/phase or real/reactance); a series R and C gives Z = R + 1/(jωC) with reactance X = −1/(ωC), a −20 dB/decade drop, while an inductor's X = ωL rises 20 dB/decade; log-log plots show these as straight slopes.
- `#L158-L184` — §2.3: pencil-and-paper impedance grows tedious with each element, but SPICE-like simulators (free QUCS, high-end Keysight ADS) handle any collection of ideal linear time-invariant elements with identical results.
- `#L185-L245` — The impedance-analyzer trick: a constant-current AC source keeps current fixed regardless of load, so with a 1 A sine the output voltage in volts numerically equals the impedance in ohms; a 1 GΩ resistor supplies a DC ground path and caps measurable impedance.
- `#L246-L263` — Log-log magnitude plots: capacitor impedance falls with frequency, inductor impedance rises, both at 20 dB per decade; phase is −90°, +90°, and 0° for ideal C, L, and R respectively.
- `#L264-L300` — Kramers-Kronig/causality links magnitude shape to phase: a −20 dB/decade slope implies phase near −90°, a +20 dB/decade slope near +90°, and a flat curve near 0°, so the phase plot can be inferred from magnitude.
- `#L301-L319` — Never confuse capacitance/inductance values with impedance: ideal element values are constant with frequency by definition even though their impedances vary; all these features are computable in any SPICE.
- `#L320-L344` — §2.4: ideal circuit elements (simulated, well-defined) differ from real components (measured, with parasitics from geometry, materials, and Maxwell's equations), even though both are called "capacitors."

- `#L403-L423` — Most PDN structures reduce to two topologies — series and parallel RLC — defined by how L and C connect as seen from the source, with R in series or parallel; understanding these simplifies PDN performance analysis.
- `#L424-L459` — §2.5: series RLC adds element impedances; at low frequency the capacitor dominates, giving −90° phase, and changing C affects only the low-frequency end (1 nF to 100 nF example).
- `#L460-L471` — At high frequency the inductor dominates the series RLC with +90° phase, and changing L (1 nH to 100 nH example) affects only the high-frequency end.
