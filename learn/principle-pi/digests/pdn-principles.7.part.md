# L1 overview

- **Ch4 §4.9 (cont.) — skin depth, internal vs external inductance** — Alloy 42 lead frames have relative permeability ~200 and bulk resistivity ~50× copper, so at DC their internal inductance is up to 200× a copper frame; but above ~10 MHz current crowds to the outer surface and the inductance is almost entirely external, making alloy 42 and copper frames equal in inductance above 10 MHz. Resistance is ~100× copper (50× resistivity, half the current cross section), commonly fixed by 20 µm silver plating so it matches copper above 10 MHz. Skin depth δ = 1/√(π·f·µr·µ0·σ); copper δ is 2.1 µm at 1 GHz and 21 µm at 10 MHz (~1 mil), so a 17 µm board trace is all-external above 100 MHz. Internal self-inductance is the DC-to-high-frequency difference and is <10% of loop inductance: the Figure 4.6 18-gauge loop measured 247 nH total, with 1.28 nH/inch × 11 in circumference = 14 nH internal (<6%). Figure 4.12 shows 100 MHz current density in a 1.4 mil copper microstrip.

- **Ch4 §4.10 — loop vs partial, self and mutual inductance** — Loop inductance is a property of the whole loop, measured as field-line rings enclosed per amp; change any feature and the whole loop changes. Partial inductance splits that total into segment contributions: partial self-inductance (bounded by planes normal to the segment end caps; chief design variable is width — wider spreads current and lowers it) and partial mutual inductance (chief variable is separation — farther apart means fewer shared rings and lower mutual). Mutual rings from the return circulate opposite to the signal's self rings, so L_loop = L_self,sig + L_self,ret − 2M; for identical signal and return conductors L_loop = 2(L_self − M). This yields the three recurring design rules: shorter conductors, wider conductors, signal and return closer together. Figure 4.13 shows self vs mutual field lines.

- **Ch4 §4.11 — exact solutions only for coax, twin rods, rod over plane** — Maxwell's equations give exact per-length L and C for only three geometries (coax, two round rods, rod over plane) [5]; Figure 4.14. Coax: L_len = (µ0/2π)·ln(b/a), µ0 = 32 nH/inch = 32 pH/mil; example a = 2 mm, b = 5 mm. Twin rods: L_len = (µ0/π)·ln(s/r) for s ≫ r; a BGA via pair with r = 5 mil and s = 40 mil gives 21 pH/mil, so a 64 mil board via pair is 64 × 21 pH/mil ≈ 1.3 nH. Rod over plane is half the twin-rod result via the method of images with h = s/2: L_len = (µ0/2π)·ln(2h/r); a 24 AWG wire (10 mil diameter, r = 5 mil) 20 mil above a plane gives 10 nH/inch, so a 1 in wire is 10 nH. Because the ratio h/r sets the value, shrinking wire radius and height together changes nothing — keeping the length short is the dominant lever.

- **Ch4 §4.12 — round-loop approximation and the 25 nH/inch rule** — For a circular loop of radius a and wire radius WR, L_loop = µ0·a·[ln(8a/WR) − 2]; a = 1.75 in, WR = 0.0245 in predicts 247 nH, matching the Figure 4.7 measurement. L scales as a·ln(a), not as area a² or circumference a; Dk is irrelevant because magnetic fields do not interact with dielectrics — only conductor geometry matters. Larger WR lowers L only logarithmically (soft dependence; Figure 4.15 plots 10 and 20 mil radii). Normalizing to circumference for a 10 mil-radius wire over 0.5–2 in radius gives ~25 nH/inch of circumference = 1 nH/mm: the origin of the universal loop rule of thumb (Figure 4.16).

- **Ch4 §4.13 — wide conductors close together: sheet inductance** — For broadside-coupled wide conductors (e.g., two planes with thin dielectric, Figure 4.17), the parallel-plate approximation is L_loop = µ0·Len·h/w = Lsq × n, with Lsq = µ0·h = 32 pH/mil × h(mils) and n = Len/w the number of squares. Every square cut from the same plane pair has the same loop inductance, independent of edge size; thinner dielectric is therefore a direct win for everything cut from the pair. A 2.7 mil dielectric gives 86 pH/square, so a 1000 mil × 100 mil trace pair is 10 squares × 86 pH = 860 pH. L scales with length and h and inversely with width (all first order). Validity requires w/h ≳ 10: error is ~30% at 10:1 and ~300% at 1:1, and at w = h the rule of thumb is ~10 nH/inch. Fringe fields make narrow traces act wider than their geometry (effective width, Figures 4.18/4.19, Keysight ADS).

- **Ch4 §4.14 — loop inductance of any uniform transmission line** — For a uniform line, Z0 = √(L/C) and TD = √(LC), hence total L = Z0·TD (Z0 in Ω, TD in ns, L in nH): 50 Ω and 1 ns gives 50 nH. TD = Len/v with v = c/√Dkeff and c ≈ 11.8 in/ns, so L per length = Z0·√Dkeff/c; for FR4 (Dkeff ≈ 4) L_len ≈ Z0/6, and every 50 Ω FR4 line is 8.3 nH/inch regardless of cross section. Consequence: any feature that raises Z0 raises L, and closer/wider conductors lower both. Example: a 10 mil wide microstrip with 5 mil dielectric, 2 in long → 2 × 8.3 = 16.6 nH, whereas the sheet estimate (33 nH) is 2× high because the aspect ratio is small. IPC microstrip and stripline Z0 approximations (Eqs 4.65/4.66) convert to L through the same relation; transmission-line approximations are good below aspect ratio ~3, the parallel-plate form above ~3 (Figure 4.21). Use a 2D field solver for offset stripline, non-rectangular conductors, or accuracy.

- **Ch4 §4.15 — the 25 nH/inch (1 nH/mm) rule of thumb** — Two independent estimates converge on the same number: the 10 mil-radius circular loop normalized per inch (Figure 4.16) and the straight-wire partial self-inductance L_self ≈ 2·Len·[ln(2Len/R) − 1] nH, which for 30 AWG (10 mil diameter), 1 in long is ~25 nH. So a narrow conductor with a far return (e.g., a via) is roughly 25 nH/inch = 1 nH/mm. Use it for order-of-magnitude work; switch to §4.14 for wide conductors close together.

- **Ch4 §4.16 — extracting loop inductance from 3D-field-solver S-parameters** — 3D solvers are the only tool for arbitrary geometry and fringe-dominated features such as via clearance holes, but "with incredible power comes the possibility of incredible harm": getting a relevant, trustworthy answer requires partitioning the design and learning the flow. Output is S-parameters at user-defined signal-return ports. Worked example: BGA power/ground via pair, 8-layer 62 mil board, 10 mil vias on 40 mil pitch, modeled in Simbeor; the twin-rod estimate is 21 pH/mil × 62 mil = 1.30 nH. Short port 2 (0 Ω) to form a loop, simulate S11, then Zin = 50·(1+S11)/(1−S11) and L = Im(Zin)/(2πf). Extracted L is 1.5 nH vs the 1.3 nH estimate; the extra comes from non-uniform current entering/leaving and end-region fields. S-parameters are accurate but non-scalable black boxes; a scalable circuit topology model makes parameter sweeps easy. Quasi-static validity holds below ~λ/20: 0.6 GHz for 1 in in air, 0.3 GHz/in with dielectric, so the 0.06 in via stays inductive to ~5 GHz — the extraction is flat to ~5 GHz. Figures 4.22–4.27.

- **Ch4 §4.17 — the bottom line** — Ten rules: (1) PDN loop inductance sets high-frequency impedance and peak heights, so lowering it is a top design guideline; (2) inductance = magnetic-field rings per amp; (3) it is also stored magnetic energy per amp; (4) do not confuse loop inductance with field density; (5) quasi-static is valid below ~λ/20, i.e. 0.6/length-in-inches GHz in air; (6) exact only for three round-conductor geometries; (7) shorten, widen, bring signal and return together; (8) every square of a plane pair has the same sheet inductance; (9) the parallel-plate approximation is good until width < ~10 dielectric thicknesses; (10) equal-Z0 lines in the same material have equal loop inductance per length.
