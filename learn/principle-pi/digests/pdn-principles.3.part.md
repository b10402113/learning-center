# L1 overview

- **Ch2 §2.1 — Why do we care about impedance?** — PDN is acceptable if chip voltage (average plus AC ripple) stays in spec, and that depends on the chip's current spectrum as much as on the PDN; board probes can look clean while die pads ring. To first order, noise = (current spectrum drawn by chips) × (PDN interconnect impedance), so with worst-case die current from gate-utilization or simulation plus a measured/simulated impedance you can predict worst-case-realistic noise. Because end-user code cannot be reproduced in the lab, the PDN's impedance profile becomes the surrogate acceptance metric, and design reduces to hitting a target impedance. Impedance is therefore *the* design metric.
- **Ch2 §2.2 — Impedance in the frequency domain** — Z ≡ V/I holds in both domains and extends to multi-terminal/transfer impedance. In time domain an ideal cap gives I = C·dV/dt so Z = V/(C·dV/dt) — correct but useless, since it depends on waveform shape; in frequency domain Z_C = 1/(jωC), Z_L = jωL, ω = 2πf. Complex Z is reported as magnitude+phase or real+imaginary (reactance X): series RC gives Z = R + jX, X_C = −1/(ωC), X_L = +ωL. Single C falls 20 dB/decade (−1 slope on log-log) with phase −90°, single L rises 20 dB/decade (+1) with phase +90°, R is flat at 0°. Hence log-log |Z| vs log f is the standard PDN plot.
- **Ch2 §2.3 — Calculating or simulating impedance** — Hand algebra explodes with element count; any collection of ideal linear time-invariant elements can be solved in SPICE. QUCS (free/open source) and Keysight ADS give identical results. The "impedance analyzer" trick: drive the unknown with an ideal AC *constant-current* source — a 1 A, 0° sine makes the complex node voltage numerically equal to the complex load impedance (|V| = |Z| in ohms, ∠V = ∠Z). Fig 2.1 sine current; Fig 2.2 analyzer circuit plus ideal R/L/C plots; a 1 GΩ shunt resistor supplies the DC path most simulators demand and caps the measurable impedance at 1 GΩ (ample for PDN). Phase is not independent information: by Kramers-Kronig (causality), a −20 dB/decade slope implies ≈ −90° phase, +20 dB/decade ≈ +90°, flat ≈ 0°, so the |Z| shape reveals the phase. Fig 2.2/2.6 confirm. Never confuse an ideal element's *value* (C, L, R constant with frequency by definition) with its *impedance* (frequency dependent).
- **Ch2 §2.4 — Real components vs ideal elements** — Ideal elements have defined properties and are what you simulate; real components are physical structures with parasitics, measurable via two-port VNA (Ch3), and the word "capacitor" hides the difference. Measured 0603 MLCC on a four-layer test board (Fig 2.3) matches an ideal 182 nF cap only to ~3 MHz (Fig 2.4); a series RLC with **R = 18 mΩ, C = 182 nF, L = 1.3 nH** matches magnitude and phase all the way to the 200 MHz measurement bandwidth (Fig 2.5) — the model may be good beyond it. S-parameter behavioral models can be embedded in simulators but are risky without knowing the measurement setup. Design rule: essentially every PDN structure is approximated by a series RLC, a parallel RLC, or a combination.
- **Ch2 §2.5 — The series RLC circuit** — Z_series = R + jωL + 1/(jωC); it is the canonical real-capacitor model. Three bands (Fig 2.6, R = 0.01 Ω, L = 1 nH, C = 1 nF): low frequency C-dominated at −90° phase, high frequency L-dominated at +90°, and at the crossing the reactances cancel leaving only R. C sets only the low-frequency end (Fig 2.7, 1→100 nF); L sets only the high-frequency end (Fig 2.8, 1→100 nH). Resonance **SRF = 1/(2π√(LC))**, conveniently **SRF[MHz] = 159/√(L[nH]·C[nF])**; L = 1 nH, C = 1 nF gives 159 MHz, exactly the impedance minimum. At resonance the reactance magnitude equals the *characteristic impedance* **Z0 = √(L/C)** — an RLC-circuit property, unrelated to transmission-line Z0.
- **Ch2 §2.6 — The parallel RLC circuit** — Same three elements in parallel (Fig 2.9, R = 1 kΩ, L = 10 nH, C = 1 nF): L shorts at DC and rises with frequency, C falls with frequency, and their parallel combination peaks where the reactances are equal, clamped by the resistor (infinite peak without it). **PRF = 1/(2π√(LC))**, i.e. PRF[MHz] = 159/√(L[nH]·C[nF]); L = 10 nH, C = 1 nF gives 50.3 MHz, exactly the peak. Rule: peaks in an impedance profile are almost always parallel RLC; understand the levers on peak height to engineer them down.
- **Ch2 §2.7 — Resonant properties of series and parallel RLC** — Three descriptors: resonant frequency, min/max impedance at resonance, and broadness of the dip/peak. Resonance f = 1/(2π√(LC)) with L in nH and C in nF giving MHz; reactance at resonance = **Z0 = √(L/C)**. R sets the extreme: series Zmin = R_series exactly, parallel Zmax = R_parallel exactly; larger R makes the series dip shallower/broader (Fig 2.10, L = 10 nH, C = 10 nF, three R values) while the outer C and L asymptotes barely move. **q-factor** (written lowercase q to avoid confusion with capacitor charge Q) = Z0/R = f_res/(half-power bandwidth) = energy stored/energy lost per cycle; for the series circuit **q = Z0/R_series** and **Zmin = Z0/q**. L = 10 nH, C = 10 nF gives Z0 = 1 Ω, so R = 1 Ω gives q = 1, and Fig 2.10's three resistors correspond to q = 10, 1, 0.1. **q = 1/2 is critical damping** (fastest settling, no ringing); higher q rings longer with a sharper dip (current ringing), lower q is sluggish. Applied to parallel circuits the same math holds with voltage ringing: **Zmax ≈ q·Z0** when R is in parallel with L and C (a different form, Z0²/R, when the loss sits in series with L or C); Fig 2.11 shows q = 0.1, 1, 10 for the same L, C. With Z0 = 1 Ω the peaks come out 10, 1, 0.1 Ω — a 10:1 swing in peak height from damping alone. Design rule: engineer q ≈ 1 at PDN resonances by raising R, lowering L, or raising C.
- **Ch2 §2.8 — Examples of RLC circuits and real capacitors** — Four figures of merit for any RLC: f_res (MHz) = 159/√(L[nH]·C[nF]), Z0 = √(L/C), q = Z0/R_series, and Zmin = R_series = Z0/q (series) or Zmax = q·Z0 (parallel, R in parallel). Compute these first for every circuit. (1) Large tantalum, **C = 1000 µF = 10⁶ nF, L = 8 nH, R = 0.05 Ω** → very low q, flat broad profile; AVX data in Fig 2.12 confirms. (2) 1206 X5R MLCC, **C = 10 µF = 10,000 nF, L = 3 nH, R = 0.003 Ω** → q large because R is tiny, so a sharp resonance (Fig 2.13, AVX used an aggressive L = 1 nH, hence higher SRF than the on-board estimate). (3) 0402 X7R, **C = 1 nF, L = 1 nH, R = 0.16 Ω** → R ~50× larger (fewer parallel plates) but the higher Z0 keeps q comparable to the big cap.
- **Ch2 §2.9 — The PDN as viewed by the chip or by the board** — Series/parallel names describe how L and C are seen from the measurement/simulation source, and the loss element may be series or parallel (often still called ESR). LC in parallel → impedance maximum; LC in series → minimum. Four topologies in Fig 2.14 (source position × LC combination × damping position). The same die/package structure looks completely different from the two sides (Fig 2.15): from the **board**, package-lead L is in series with on-die C → series RLC with a dip, and the impedance rises with frequency on package inductance; from the **die**, on-die C is in parallel with package-lead L attached to the low-impedance board → parallel RLC with a peak. Consequence: the board can only ever see the die's low-frequency impedance; the die's high-frequency local PDN is hidden behind package inductance and its own low C impedance. Losses: R_leads + R_metalization add in the series loop, so replace R with **R_equivalent = R_leads + R_metal** in all four figures of merit (order of series elements is irrelevant). Small-die example: **C_die = 50 nF, L_package = 0.5 nH, R_leads = 0.01 Ω, R_metal = 0.005 Ω → R_eq = 0.015 Ω**; the computed q-factors are high, i.e. a peaked response, and simulated min/max match the estimates (Figs 2.16, 2.17; Fig 2.17 caption uses L = 0.1 nH). Practical corollary: how you hook up a PDN measurement changes what you measure.
- **Ch2 §2.10 — Transient response** — The impedance profile predicts the voltage noise a current produces: each current component times |Z| at that frequency. Frequency-domain analysis is only the steady-state response (every tone assumed on forever); rare "rogue wave" cases need transient analysis (Ch9). Transient simulation is easy (Fig 2.18: VRM modeled by series R+L, two series-RLC decoupling caps, ammeter for current), but read the frequency domain first (Fig 2.19 shows a PRF near 25 MHz). Current bandwidth follows **BW[GHz] ≈ 0.35/RT[nsec]** (10 ns → 35 MHz; 30 ns → ~10 MHz), so a 10 ns edge excites the 25 MHz peak and rings, while a 30 ns edge puts less energy there and rings less (Fig 2.20). Key: the ringing period is 40 ns at the 25 MHz PRF regardless of rise time — the ringing frequency is set by the circuit's peak impedance, not by the current waveform; only the amplitude tracks the current energy at that frequency. Ringing on a measured PDN rail is thus a fingerprint of impedance peaks, which is why parallel resonances dominate PDN design.
- **Ch2 §2.11 — Advanced topic: the impedance matrix** — For n ports (each port = a terminal pair with V defined across it and I in one terminal/out the other) there are n voltages and n currents, and every V/I pairing is captured by a matrix with rows = voltage port j, columns = current port k: V_j = Σ_k Z_jk·I_k. Valid for linear, passive, time-invariant networks: one input sine produces only that frequency (no harmonics), superposition holds, and each Z_jk is complex and frequency dependent. Diagonal elements are **self-impedances** — obtained by leaving all other ports open (I = 0), then Z_jj = V_j/I_j, identical to the two-terminal impedance. Off-diagonal elements are **mutual / transfer impedances (transimpedance)** — Z_jk = V_j/I_k with the other ports open; they measure *coupling*, not a resistance between the ports (an ohmmeter between ports would read something else). Limits: 0 Ω = no coupling; equal to both self-impedances = ports effectively shorted together. Fig 2.23 contrasts a star of resistors returned to a zero-ohm plane (Z11 = R_A, Z22 = R_B, Z12 = Z21 = 0) against a delta of resistors (Z12 = Z21 → R_C, approaching the self-impedances as R_C grows). Simulation recipe (Fig 2.24): force 1 A into one port with the other open, read the voltages — V1 = Z11, V2 = Z21, then swap ports for Z22 and Z12; any SPICE works. Worked example: two RLC branches, L = 5 nH, R = 0.005 Ω, C1 = 1000 nF, C2 = 100 nF, sharing a **5 nH** lead → self-impedances differ at low frequency but converge at the high-frequency limit (same equivalent L), and **Z12 = Z21 = the impedance of the 5 nH common inductor** (Fig 2.25). The formalism extends later to distributed elements and plane-cavity waveguides.
- **Ch2 §2.12 — The bottom line** — Ten takeaways: (1) PDN noise = die current spectrum × PDN impedance profile; (2) Z = V/I in both domains, simpler in frequency domain for C/L; (3) real components are measurable, ideal elements are simulatable — don't conflate; (4) simple RLC combinations describe complicated real structures; (5) master series RLC and parallel RLC; (6) always estimate the four figures of merit first (f_res, Z0, q, min/max Z); (7) parallel-resonance peaks, sized by Z0 × q, matter most; (8) transient ringing frequency = peak-impedance frequency; (9) the impedance matrix generalizes Z to multi-terminal circuits via ports; (10) diagonal = self-impedance, off-diagonal = transfer impedance/coupling, small transfer Z = little coupling.
