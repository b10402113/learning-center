# L1 overview

- **Ch9 §9.13 (cont.) — target impedance vs. time- and frequency-domain response** — A 1.55 A step lasting 40 nsec into a 32 mΩ PDN: resistive PDN holds the full 50 mV droop; reactive PDN with Z0 = 32 mΩ droops the same (Istep × Z0) but recenters to the DC IR drop, 1.55 A × (5.1 + 3) mΩ = 12.5 mV. When the current releases at 40 nsec the inductor kicks the die up 39 mV (≈50 mV above the recentered point), so a single-sided current produces a double-sided voltage response. Figure 9.21, 9.22. Frequency-domain view: 1.55 A peak-to-peak square/sine into an impedance peak of 0.104 Ω gives 0.161 V p-p (sine) and 0.201 V p-p (square), matching I × Zpeak; the target-impedance metric is 2× conservative for a sine at resonance and π/4 × 2 ≈ 1.6× for a square wave, because the peak is a filter that extracts the 4/π first harmonic.

- **Ch9 §9.14 — reactive elements, q-factor, and peak impedance** — Four PDNs engineered to the same 1 MHz resonance and the same 50 mΩ peak but q = 0, 2, 5, 8 (Table 9.3, Figures 9.23–9.27): higher q requires lower L, higher C, lower loop R, so Z0 falls while Z0 × q stays fixed. Step response is proportional to Z0, not to the peak: 50 mV for the flat 50 mΩ resistor, 24 mV for q = 2 (13 mΩ DC path), less still for q = 5/8, all inside ±5% of 1 V. q ≈ number of visible ringing cycles (2, 5, 8 dips) and equals the resonance's "time memory." Sine and square excitation: high-q PDNs need 2, 5, 8 cycles to build up, but the settled p-p noise is 50 mV regardless of sharpness (q = 2 overshoots to 56 mV because its peak exceeded target by 6 mΩ); square-wave p-p is 4/π times the resistive case. Conclusion: peak-below-target is conservative for step loads and roughly exact for steady-state resonance; flat resistive profiles consume the entire tolerance while reactive peaks recenter around nominal.

- **Ch9 §9.15 — rogue waves** — A PDN with three comparable, high-q peaks (1, 10, 100 MHz, each 50 mΩ, q = 4, Figures 9.28–9.31, Table 9.4) meets target everywhere, yet sequential excitation lets energy from one resonance drive the next before it decays. Isolated square-wave trains each stay legal: 969 mV at 4.3 µsec (1 MHz), 967 mV at 5.33 µsec (10 MHz), 962 mV at 5.62 µsec (100 MHz). Concatenating the trains (10 MHz starts at 5 µsec, 100 MHz at 5.4 µsec, current always 0–1 A) compounds them: marker M4 = 969 mV, M5 = 948 mV (first violation of the 950 mV / 5% line), final droop 930 mV = 7% below Vdd. Named by Xiang Hu [7]–[9]. Higher q (10+) or peaks above target make it far worse. Probability is vanishingly low (phase-aligning three resonances), but the mitigation is to keep the impedance flat and q low; economics still permits one Bandini Mountain.

- **Ch9 §9.16 — robust design strategy under rogue waves** — Root cause is threefold: multiple comparable peaks at/above target, high q, and a carefully sequenced current train. Current waveforms are not controllable (a customer application always exists), so design the PDN: one dominant peak at target makes rogue waves impossible; multiple peaks at low q shorten ringing overlap; a fully resistive PDN cannot accumulate at all (Figure 9.32 shows the same pathological train staying between 950 mV and 1 V). Keep impedance flat below target, minimize DC resistance, and move the regulator feedback point to the load so the response centers on nominal. Contrast adaptive voltage positioning / load lines [6], which deliberately sag to −5% under load to cap the high-side overshoot at the cost of power. Target impedance is a reference line, not a guarantee: 2× conservative for sine, 1.6× for square, exact for flat FDTIM-chosen regions, violated only by rogue waves. Robustness tiers: all peaks ≤ target = fully robust; only Z0 ≤ target = marginally robust; Bandini Mountain above target = vulnerable to customer code.

- **Ch9 §9.17 — clock-edge current impulses and the switched capacitor load (SCL)** — A constant current source draws current regardless of rail voltage and adds no damping; real CMOS current is proportional to voltage (resistive) and therefore damps the PDN, with damping changing over the transient — a nonlinear, time-varying effect that frequency-domain analysis can only average. The SCL [10] switches charge instead: 1 GHz clock, 1.55 A average, Table 9.5 gives a 100 mΩ / 100 MHz peak, Q = CV yields 1.55 nC per edge and 1.55 nF switched per cycle. A half-clock topology with capacitance on both Vdd and Vss is preferred so current flows on both edges and decays in a Poisson-like profile to zero before the next edge; a Vss-only, full-clock variant produces unrealistic ODC ESR droop. In the ADS schematic (Figure 9.33) a bit sequencer drives the switch, L1 (with R) spreads the impulse over the clock period with tau = 1/4 of the clock period (250 psec for 1 GHz), and the 250 psec R1 + R2 split is assumed 60% series / 40% on-die. Impulse response (Figure 9.35) matches the constant-current case of Figure 9.18.

- **Ch9 §9.18 — impulse, step, resonance, anti-impulse, and clock-frequency scaling** — Simulated impulse droop 29 mV vs 31 mV calculated (the difference is charge supplied by package inductance during the 1 nsec draw). Step response shows a 56 mV envelope with clock-edge noise riding on it; the package loop current is nearly spike-free because on-die C plus loop L form the low-pass filter, and KCL gives I_BGA + I_ondie = I_load (Figure 9.37). Resonance at 100 MHz builds to 196 mV p-p in about three periods (q ≈ 3), slightly below the 201 mV predicted for an ideal current source because the SCL adds damping. A missing clock edge creates an anti-impulse: current already flowing in the inductor charges the on-die capacitance, producing a spike and ringing opposite to the impulse — the mechanism behind clock swallowing. Doubling the clock to 2 GHz keeps the same charge per impulse (same impulse/anti-impulse droop) but doubles dynamic current, so the step droop doubles to a measured 106 mV.

- **Ch9 §9.19 — clock gating and clock swallowing on real CMOS** — Gating a clock on abruptly produces a step whose droop ≈ Itransient × Z0 of the Bandini Mountain: fine if Z0 ≤ target, 2× the tolerance if Z0 is 2× target; repetitive gating drives the resonance, tolerable only while q ≲ 1.6 for a single peak. Swallowing is gating of individual pulses and is far more disruptive: 12-bit patterns 1110…, 110…, 1010…, 100100…, 10001000…, 100000100000, 100000000000 give 1/4, 1/3, 1/2, 2/3, 3/4, 5/6, 11/12 duty reduction, first causing an anti-impulse then a new steady state with up to ~2× the p-p noise of full clock; the 1/12 pattern is an 83 MHz subharmonic near the 100 MHz resonance. Reduced average power does not mean reduced noise: impedance is unchanged and the PDN still rings. Because the 100 MHz / 10 nsec resonance has a time constant comparable to a 1 nsec clock, pulse history and exact timing matter — at 2, 5, 10 GHz many cycles fit inside one time constant and single pulses matter less.

- **Ch9 §9.20 — power gating** — Collapsing a domain removes leakage, not just dynamic power; the always-on upstream domain must keep running while the downstream domain charges. Closing a low-resistance gate in <1 nsec shares charge before the package inductor can deliver any, so with Cup = Cdown the upstream rail instantly halves (25 nF / 25 nF example, Figures 9.43–9.45), likely breaking upstream timing. Charge conservation gives the final Vdd+ from Cup·Vdd− / (Cup + Cdown). Switch resistance trades upstream droop against downstream rise time: R_switch = 1 mΩ gives ~500 mV upstream droop and familiar ringing; 1 Ω gives ~16 mV but a slow ramp (sweep 0.001–1 Ω). Size the gate RC so the external PDN supplies the charge: reactive time constant from the 100 MHz / 10 nsec peak, and with q ≈ 3 equilibrium takes 3 × 2π ≈ 19 time constants; rule of thumb, RC ≥ 20× the reactive PDN time constant. Alternative: turn on a sequence of small switches staggered in time.

- **Ch9 §9.21 — the bottom line** — Ten rules: (1) target impedance is the primary metric and needs real transient-current knowledge; (2) if unknown, start at half the maximum current; (3) PDN current is charge impulses at clock edges, filtered into a slow board-level ramp by on-die C and package L; (4) die noise is droop across the on-die capacitance, and the resulting V/L drives di/dt — high di/dt from low L is good because it restores charge faster; (5) three canonical waveforms (impulse, step, resonance) cover the fundamental cases; (6) marginally robust = keep every Z0 below target; (7) fully robust = keep every peak below target; (8) use an SCL rather than a current source for damping and for gating/swallowing studies; (9) rogue waves are real but extremely unlikely, so flatten the profile or lower q; (10) power-saving clock and power gating raise transient current and therefore demand lower impedance — cheaper silicon or higher risk.
