# L1 overview

- **Ch7 §7.1 — Signal integrity and planes** — Plane DC voltage is irrelevant to Z0 or signal quality: a 5 V or 12 V plane is as good a return as ground. The problem is the *transition*: when a signal via changes layers, return current must flow between the two planes, through the cavity impedance, generating a voltage that launches a propagating wave in the cavity. Any other via picks it up as long-range crosstalk.
- **Ch7 §7.2 — Why the peak impedances matter** — Cavity modal peaks are almost never a power-integrity problem (package lead inductance hides them from the die rails) but are a signal-integrity/EMC problem. A 6 in × 6 in FR4 cavity with a VRM shows a parallel peak at 55 MHz (VRM inductance vs cavity capacitance) and modal resonances from ~0.5 GHz; a 200 ps edge (BW = 0.35/0.2 ns = 1.7 GHz) excites 55 MHz, 0.5 GHz and 1 GHz. One 200 ps edge into 50 Ω gives 6 mA return current, 5 mV victim noise = 1.5% of a 300 mV swing; 10 simultaneous aggressors give ~15%. Figure 7.1: victim vias at opposite corners see the same magnitude — the noise is long range.
- **Ch7 §7.3 — Lower impedance and higher damping** — Loss rises with frequency, so higher-order 2D modes are damped more. Thin dielectric is the single most effective knob: it lowers spreading inductance, lowers Zcavity, raises conductor loss. Figure 7.2: 6 in cavity, 30 mil vs 3 mil FR4. Table 7.1 ranks h as inversely proportional to impedance and conductor loss; Dk helps only as √Dk. Figure 7.3: 3 in cavity, 4 mil, Dk = 3.5/5/10 — higher Dk lowers resonant frequency, impedance and raises damping. Never trade thicker h for higher Dk.
- **Ch7 §7.4 — Shorting vias** — Ideal return path is a matched coax (no cavity coupling); practical surrogate is uniformly distributed shorting vias. One center via does nothing. Figure 7.4: 1/4/9/16/25/36/49 vias on a 6 in cavity push the first peak up. Model: fres = 1/(2π√(Lvia/n · Ccavity)), Ccavity = parallel-plate, Lvia ∝ h·k. Uniform spacing svia = Len/√n; field-solver fit gives k = 6, i.e. λmin = 6·s. Rule: s ≤ λ/6 → s[in] ≤ 1/f[GHz] → s ≤ 3·RT[ns] (FR4, 6 in/ns). Equivalent to ≥2 shorting vias across the rising-edge spatial extent. Example: 1 GHz needs 1 in spacing regardless of cavity thickness (Figure 7.6, 3 mil and 10 mil). Wild River test board (Figure 7.7): 0.25 in → 4 GHz, 0.1 in → 10 GHz, 0.04 in → 25 GHz.
- **Ch7 §7.4 continued** — Shorting vias require both planes at the same voltage → the robust rule: use the same voltage (normally Vss/ground) for all return planes. Differential pairs excite the cavity far less (opposite return-current circulation cancels; < −80 dB vs single-ended when tightly coupled), but mode conversion from skew/asymmetry creates common current that excites the cavity anyway. Alternative: 7 return vias around a signal via form a mini-coax (Figure 7.8, 2.92 mm connector footprint); minimally add ≥1 return via per signal via.
- **Ch7 §7.5 — Many DC blocking capacitors** — When planes differ in voltage, capacitors replace shorting vias. Capacitance value is almost irrelevant (>100 nF is enough); mounting inductance rules. 100 nF with 2 nH has SRF = 1/(2π√(2 nH·100 nF)) ≈ 11 MHz; above that it looks inductive and behaves like a via. Figure 7.9: 25 caps, 2 nH, 10 nF/100 nF/1 µF/10 µF give identical suppression; ESR 25 mΩ each → 1 mΩ effective for 25. Figure 7.11: for a 10 mil cavity, mounting inductance must be < ~50 pH to match via-only suppression.
- **Ch7 §7.6 — Counting DC blocking capacitors** — Lblocking_cap = Lmount + Lvia, Lvia ≈ LLen-via·h. ncaps = (Lmount + Lvia)/Lvia; spacing scales as 1/√ncaps, so scaps = svia·√(Lvia/(Lmount+Lvia)). Mounting inductance grades: 0.2 nH ultimate, 0.5 nH best practical, 1.0 nH well engineered, 2.0 nH typical. For 10 mil cavity with Lvia = 0.1 nH: 2 nH → 1/42 λ (7× closer than vias, ~50× the caps). Suppressing below 1 GHz (λ = 6 in) → 150 mil spacing = 7 caps/linear inch ≈ 50 caps/in² ≈ 5,000 caps on a 10 in board — impractical. 3D check (Figure 7.12): 1 in spacing, 4 vs 20 mil, 2 nH caps; predicted 140 MHz, simulated 228 MHz vs 1.08 GHz for vias (~1/5). With 1 nH caps, density for RT = 1 ns is 1 cap/in²; RT = 0.3 ns needs ~10 cap/in².
- **Ch7 §7.7 — Capacitors that carry return current** — Second criterion: dI/dt plane bounce. Vnoise = Lcaps·nsig·(dI/dt) with I = Vsig/Z0 and dI/dt ≈ Vsig/(Z0·RT); 10% bounce for 50 Ω and 1 nH gives nsig/ncaps ≈ 5·RT[ns] (Eq. 7.37), i.e. 2.5 signal vias per cap at RT = 0.5 ns; 2 nH doubles the cap count so each 0.5 ns signal needs its own cap. Take the larger of the resonance-driven and bounce-driven densities (Figure 7.13, RT = 1/0.5/0.3 ns). A 5 in × 5 in board: 25 × 15 = 375 caps with <1 nH each. PCIe Gen III (8 Gbps, UI 125 ps, RT < 50 ps) would demand an impractical DC-blocking density → same-voltage planes plus shorting vias is the answer.
- **Ch7 §7.8 — Suboptimal capacitor count** — With too few caps, the mounted cap creates a new low-frequency parallel peak: 100 nF, 2 nH, 28 mΩ on a 6 in cavity resonates at ~89 MHz (Figure 7.14). Below SRF the cap dominates; above it the profile is set by ESL + spreading inductance and is identical for all C values (Figure 7.15). A 6 mA, 0.4 ns edge excites everything below ~1 GHz — here the 89 MHz peak; an 89 MHz clock drives it hard and the whole cavity bounces together, reaching ~90 mV pp (Figure 7.16). Same noise measured at widely separated points: cavity-wide, not local.
- **Ch7 §7.9 — Spreading vs mounting inductance** — Lcaps = ESL/n, Ccavity = ε0·Dk·A/h (ε0 = 0.225 pF/in), fres = 1/(2π√((ESL/n + Lspread)·Ccavity)); Lspread ≈ 1 square of sheet inductance. Push fres above signal BW by: low ESL per cap, many caps in parallel, small low-capacitance cavity. Thin dielectric lowers both Lspread and modal peak height even though it raises Ccavity. Larger cavity → lower peaks but lower frequencies; smaller cavity → higher frequency but higher peaks. Keep return planes continuous (no "power puddles" crossing) or return current adds noise.
- **Ch7 §7.10 — Damping the few-capacitor parallel peak** — Damping comes from capacitor ESR (conductor loss in plates dominates; more plates in parallel → lower ESR). 0402 MLCC: ESR ≈ 28 mΩ at 100 nF, ≈ 3.5 mΩ at 10 µF. Q = √(L/C)/ESR_eff, Zpeak ≈ ESR_eff·Q = √(L/C)·... ; two regimes: Lspread ≪ ESL/n (Zpeak independent of n — adding caps raises fres but lowers ESR_eff, effects cancel) vs Lspread comparable. Worked case: C = 10 µF, h = 2 mils, A = 5 in × 5 in, Dk = 4.3, ESL = 2 nH, ESR = 3.5 mΩ, n = 1 vs 9 → predicted 32 and 98 MHz, simulated 31 and 91 MHz; predicted Zpeak 48 Ω vs simulated 50 Ω (Figure 7.17): 9× the caps cuts the peak < 2×. Higher ESR is better: pick small-value caps for ESR, or controlled-ESR parts (Figure 7.18).
- **Ch7 §7.11 — Cavity losses** — Plane series resistance competes with cap ESR: 1/2 oz copper = 1 mΩ/square DC, 17 µm thick, skin depth 6.6 µm at 100 MHz (~40%), so 2.5 mΩ per plane, ~5 mΩ for both planes in series — about the ESR of a 10 µF cap, halving Q. For 0.1 µF (28 mΩ) cavity loss matters little. With loss included (Figure 7.19) the 10 µF peak drops 50 Ω → 12 Ω while the small-cap peak is unchanged. With many low-ESR large caps, Zpeak falls with n (Figure 7.20); with high-ESR caps, caps dominate the loss and more caps only shift frequency.
- **Ch7 §7.12 — Multiple capacitor values** — Peaks inside the return-current spectrum mean risk; without the transient current waveform you cannot predict the noise. For a fixed cap count, exact values barely change the ESL-vs-Ccavity peak. Figure 7.21: 12 × 1 µF vs 12 × 0.01 µF (2 nH); the 200 MHz peak is ~1/3 lower with 0.01 µF but a huge 3 MHz VRM-inductance resonance appears (bare board + VRM resonates ~20 MHz). Figure 7.22: 3/3/3 of 1/0.1/0.01 µF vs 1/2/9 gives the same 200 MHz peak. Mixed values are marginally better; count and ESL dominate. Match values to known spectral peaks when you have them.
- **Ch7 §7.13 — Controlled ESR capacitors** — 1 µF/10 µF parts with ESR engineered to 0.2–1 Ω. Zpeak ∝ √(L/C)/... drops linearly with ESR: raising 10 µF ESR from 0.0035 Ω to 1 Ω cuts the peak ≥20× (Figure 7.23); no effect on higher-frequency modal peaks. Works with 16–25 caps in parallel (Figure 7.24). Side effect: at low frequency the cavity impedance floors at ESR/n, which sets another minimum cap count: for 50 Ω signals, 1 Ω ESR and a 10% noise limit, nsig/ncaps = 50 Ω/(1 Ω) × 0.1 → 5 signals per cap (Eq. 7.38–7.42), matching the 1 nH/1 ns ESL estimate. With controlled-ESR parts both ESL and ESR matter.
- **Ch7 §7.14 — Design principles summary** — Ordered playbook: (1) same-voltage return planes + shorting vias at < λ/6 (s[in] ≤ 3·RT[ns]); (2) otherwise different-voltage plane pairs with the thinnest practical dielectric, still shorting same-voltage planes; (3) if via density is unattainable, ≥1 return via per signal via; (4) DC blocking caps with minimum mounting inductance; (5) density to push resonance past signal BW (≥ ~1/in² at 1 nH); (6) density for <10% bounce tied to signal-via density; (7) take the larger; (8) use controlled ESR to cut peaks, which may set the PI target-impedance floor; (9) pick values by spectrum if known, otherwise any mix is equally risky. Only a 3D full-wave run with the real transient current gives an accurate noise estimate.
- **Ch7 §7.15 — Advanced: planes as transmission lines** — A cavity probed at its center is four radial transmission lines plus spreading inductance per branch (Figure 7.25); a meshed array of unit cells (W-element or RLC bedspring) models higher modes and arbitrary shapes in any SPICE simulator (Figure 7.26). Unit cell: each line length = Len/2, Z set so each line carries 1/4 of Ccavity, delay = Len/(2·c/√Dk), Z = √(L/C) of that quarter. Example Len = 5 in, Dk = 4.3, h = 10 mils. Spreading inductance ≈ 1/2–1 square; start with 3/4 square split into four parallel feed inductors (Figure 7.27: SRF and first resonance match the field solver; 64+ cells needed for higher modes). Useful for fast what-if on added caps and channel-to-channel crosstalk.
- **Ch7 §7.16 — The bottom line** — 10 numbered takeaways: return current through cavity impedance makes SI/EMI noise, rarely a PI problem; noise frequencies follow cavity peaks and return-current spectrum; push peaks above signal BW, then lower their height; thin dielectric is the best height reducer; same-voltage planes plus shorting vias is the best frequency pusher; DC blocking caps are poor shorting vias; they need far higher density; their mounting inductance is property #1 and ESR #2; suboptimal cap count means simulate the actual current spectrum.
