# L1 overview

- **Ch10 §10.12 (tail) — the resonance response and a third current model** — Figure 10.15 stimulates the three-peak PDN with an ideal 8 A p-p square wave and with the switched-capacitor load. The switched-capacitor model self-damps: in the 8 A high-current phase the load current sags with the drooping rail, in the 1 A low phase leakage peaks up, so the resonance is smaller. PRC spreadsheet predicted 922 mV p-p, the switched-capacitor simulation gives 903 mV, the undamped ideal current source gives 1.588 V p-p. Bump current goes negative during part of the cycle (energy shuttling between on-die C and package L); Table 10.13 tallies the PRC figures of merit for all three loops.

- **Ch10 §10.13 — individual q-factors in frequency and time domain** — Each of the four damping mechanisms (bump-loop R, ODC R, leakage, switched-cap load) is simulated alone, then all together. Z0 = 36 mΩ extracted from the reactance crossing matches PRC's 36 mΩ; individual q = Zpeak/Z0. With all damping, Zpeak = 91 mΩ (PRC 91 mΩ), combined q = 2.5 (PRC 2.5). Individual peaks: load damping 200 mΩ at 8 A, leakage 333 mΩ, ODC ESR 523 mΩ, bump-loop 787 mΩ. Time domain back-calculates Z_peak = 89 mΩ from 8 A p-p and the p-p voltage, giving the same q = 2.5 from a third independent route (PRC, frequency domain, time domain). Key non-obvious result: for load damping alone, p-p noise is nearly independent of current level — a 1 A transient sees ~2 Ω peak impedance and 2252 mV, an 8 A transient sees 0.200 Ω and 2065 mV, because current × impedance is roughly constant. Sharp tall peaks build slowly; low-q resonances never build up. Leakage damping: 44.06 MHz, 419 mV p-p at 1 A, 333 mΩ. ODC ESR: 250 ps RC → 2.5 mΩ for 100 nF, 653 mV over 500 ns, 523 mΩ. Bump-loop loss is dominated by board-cap ESR plus skin effect and is the least important mechanism (989 mV, 787 mΩ).

- **Ch10 §10.14 — rise time and stimulation of an impedance peak** — A 1 ns current step has bandwidth 0.35/1 ns ≈ 350 MHz and excites all three peaks: Bandini droop at 16 ns, second droop at 180 ns, third at 2 µs (plotted on a log time axis, Figure 10.19). Bandwidth estimate BW = 0.35/tr (eq. 10.43) predicts loss of stimulation when tr > 0.35/f_peak: 7.9 ns for 44.1 MHz, 177 ns for 1.98 MHz, 2.69 µs for 130 kHz. Simulations confirm: first droop disappears above ~8.4 ns, second above 177 ns, third above 2.69 µs, with a gradual transition. Resonance period T = 2π√(LC); response time constant τ = √(LC) = 1/(2πf); deepest droop comes at about ¼ cycle ≈ 2τ; the peak's influence is essentially gone when BW < f_peak/2, i.e. tr > ~5τ ≈ one period. A 20 ns ramp on the switched-capacitor load (5τ of the first resonance) makes the first droop vanish entirely (Figure 10.24). Pipelines with scheduled fill/empty times are the architectural lever that guarantees long rise times.

- **Ch10 §10.15 — improving a three-peak PDN: loop inductance and selective MLCC values** — Default PRC misses the 6.3 mΩ target badly: Bandini peak 91 mΩ = 15× target, and even Z0 = 36 mΩ is 6× target; step and resonance droops far exceed the 5% (50 mV) budget. Menu: package capacitor, optimized mid-frequency board caps, extra VRM damping, lower transient current, longer current rise time, and finally raising the rail. Board-cap loop inductance is 132 pH total — 84 pH from mounted capacitors, 29 pH package, 19 pH board BGA vias — so caps dominate. Doubling 10 → 20 high-frequency caps drops 84 → 45 pH, 40 caps → 23 pH, versus 48 pH of irreducible package+via inductance; beyond ~40 caps there is no return. Replacing 10 × 1 µF with a distributed set (1 × 10 µF, 1 × 4.7 µF, 2 × 2.2 µF, 4 × 1 µF, 6 × 470 nF, 10 × 220 nF, 16 × 100 nF; eight values 22 µF–100 nF available in the model, Figure 10.25) flattens impedance and kills the second peak, but the Bandini peak only drops 91 → 74 mΩ (Z0 26 mΩ, q 2.85). Board-only fixes are capped by package inductance.

- **Ch10 §10.16 — a better SMPS model** — The VRM peak near 100 kHz in the default model is unrealistically sharp; a well-compensated SMPS has low q there, and true SMPS behavior is time-varying and nonlinear so only time-domain simulation is accurate — yet a linear model is still needed for frequency-domain work. Fix: a fictitious R_damp = 10 mΩ shunting the bulk capacitor to the ideal source, plus 10 nH series L chosen to match the VRM transient, sized so the model reproduces the expected full-load VRM droop and droop time constant (Figure 10.27). Valid only above the bulk-cap series resonance and for windows shorter than a few µs. Result: profile unchanged above 1 MHz, realistic below (Figure 10.28). With 40 board caps: Zpeak = 74 mΩ, Z0 = 26 mΩ, q = 2.85 — Bandini still dominates.

- **Ch10 §10.17 — on-package decoupling (OPD)** — Three OPD placements: top side beside the die/bumps, embedded in the substrate under the die, bottom side displacing balls. Mounted loop inductance is the #1 property, ESR is #2 (often the dominant Bandini loss), capacitance is #3 — roughly 5× ODC is enough because the series combination of package and die capacitance is what shunts the peak. Two 220 nF package caps with 150 pH mounting each move the Bandini peak 62 → 75 MHz and 74 → 53 mΩ, Z0 = 22 mΩ, q = 2.41 (Figure 10.30). Then trading 40 board caps for 10 value-optimized ones raises loop inductance (75 → 65 MHz) but *lowers* the peak 53 → 51 mΩ because more current is forced through the higher-ESR package caps and board ESR rises with fewer parallel parts; Z0 = 25 mΩ, q = 2.04. Fewer caps can therefore be electrically equal or better at lower cost — the step response prefers 40 caps (lower Z0), the resonant response prefers 10 (lower q). Net: 91 → 51 mΩ peak, 36 → 25 mΩ Z0 (Figure 10.33). Simple hand estimates (two 30 mΩ ESR caps in parallel → 15 mΩ → q 1.7) fail because resonant current splits between package and board paths; a circuit simulator is required.

- **Ch10 §10.18 — transient response before and after improvement** — Time-domain verification on three scales (Figure 10.34): first droop 218 → 186 mV at ~15 ns (limited because 10 board caps were chosen for damping, Z0 = 25 mΩ not 22 mΩ); second droop at ~150 ns is eliminated but leaves a flat offset — this is not IR drop but the step current flowing through the flat 3–30 MHz mid-band impedance, which still sits above the 5% target. Third region is overdamped at 130 kHz with a slight 1.5 µs droop; long-term settling is 10 A × 3.1 mΩ = 31 mV of true DC IR drop, and PDN DC loss is 312 mW (PRC lines 61–62). Bump current ramps faster in the improved PDN because loop L is lower (larger V/L), showing higher resonant frequency and more damping. Resonance response: worst droop 480 → 285 mV — still >5× the 50 mV tolerance, so physical PDN work is exhausted at this point.

- **Ch10 §10.19 — re-examining transient-current assumptions** — The 8 A p-p worst case is unlikely to repeat at the resonant frequency; assuming only half repeats (5–9 A) cuts the resonant transient to 4 A, moves the target impedance in the resonance band from 6.3 to 12.6 mΩ, and lowers the droop to 165 mV (Figure 10.37). Transient current is inherently a function of frequency — full swings take time, so repetition rate falls with frequency — hence target impedance should be a function of frequency too. Pipeline architecture supports this: a 20-stage pipeline releases one latch in cycle 1, two in cycle 2, … twenty in cycle 20, producing a smooth ramp. A 17 ns ramp (>5τ of the Bandini peak) drops the droop to 96 mV (Figure 10.38); residual contributors are capacitor ESR, clock-edge noise, and small reactive effects near 100 ns. Risk remains from cache misses and unexpected events.

- **Ch10 §10.20 — practical limitations: risk, performance, cost** — Full arc: 497 mV droop (50% of a 1 V rail) → 96 mV after physical, model, and current-profile improvements; still ~2× the 50 mV / 5% tolerance. Acceptable in consumer markets, often not in aviation, automotive, or medical. Last resort is raising the VRM setpoint by 50 mV: dynamic power rises ~10% (∝V²), leakage ~15% (∝V³), thermal and reliability profiles degrade. Judgment should come from measured data on similar architectures — the more known-good prior products, the higher the starting point on the learning curve.

- **Ch10 §10.21 — reverse-engineering PDN features from measurements (Altera FPGA)** — PDN impedance, transient current, and transient voltage are mutually derivable: know two, get the third. Fixture: 50 Ω non-current-carrying Kelvin sense lines from package/die bumps to scope, Vdd and Vss sense centered in a checkerboard of current-carrying balls/vias/bumps, equal-length traces, 50 Ω termination at the scope. Vdd − Vss must be measured differentially; only the loop inductance and Vdd − Vss are unique, partial inductances are not. Two subtraction methods: instrument subtraction (works on scopes, awkward on spectrum analyzers) or transmission-line subtraction (Vdd on coax center, Vss on shield, works on both, but sense stubs must be electrically short). Probe resistance forms a divider with the 50 Ω termination — 2 Ω of socket/die metal gives 4% attenuation error; sense on the package side of the bump. Measured: leakage 3 A, 11 A at 266 MHz, 19 A at 533 MHz, clock-edge noise 105 mV, Bandini at 33 MHz, 30 mV droop 100 ns after a step. Derived: 8 A / 16 A dynamic current, 30 nC per 533 MHz cycle, 27 nF switched ODC, 313 nF total ODC, 9% switch factor, 74 pH first-loop inductance, Z0 = 15.4 mΩ, board R 3.75 mΩ plus 0.6 mΩ socket = 4.35 mΩ bump-loop R. Switched-capacitor load: 27 nF, 200 ps time constant, 100 A peak, >1000 A/ns di/dt (harmless — delivered by ODC), 30 nC area.

- **Ch10 §10.22 — simulation-to-measurement correlation** — Impulse: measured clock-edge droop 105 mV vs simulated 104 mV (Figure 10.42); the 200 ps impulse width trades clock-edge noise between impulse and step responses. Loss budget (Table 10.18) gives combined q = 1.75; frequency domain peaks at 33.88 MHz / 25.9 mΩ with Z0 = 15.4 mΩ at 33.11 MHz, so q ≈ 1.68, close to the hand calculation. Here the bump-loop resistance dominates damping, unlike the three-peak example where load damping dominated. Step (25 clock edges at 266 MHz): measured 146 mV vs simulated 150 mV. Resonance (533 MHz gated at 33 MHz, 8 clocks on / 8 off, 15 ns max and 15 ns min): measured 577 mV p-p vs simulated 584 mV and spreadsheet 588 mV. The first resonance simulation was far too noisy; since impulse and step matched, the error had to be q/damping, and adding 0.6 mΩ at the ball/socket position (bump-loop R 3.75 → 4.35 mΩ) fixed it. Real hardware has more loss than first-pass models assume.

- **Ch10 §10.23 — summary of simulated and measured features** — Table 10.19 collects inputs, extracted parameters, and results in PRC form. Target impedances are 6.9 mΩ at 266 MHz and 3.4 mΩ at 533 MHz — far below both Z0 (15.4 mΩ) and Zpeak (25.9 mΩ), consistent with the large measured droops; this FPGA was pushed well past its intended operating region. The final q = 1.68 means the PDN is well damped by normal standards; the original publication assumed q = 3.9 and Zpeak = 63 mΩ, apparently because leakage and load damping were omitted. Required measurements: PDN voltage, leakage current, current at a few clock frequencies, impulse response, resonant frequency — the impulse gives ODC via clock-edge droop, the resonant period gives loop inductance.

- **Ch10 §10.24 — the bottom line** — PDN design is peak management. Three terms characterize every peak: resonant frequency, characteristic impedance, q-factor. Three current waveforms (clock-edge impulse, step, resonant square wave) fully describe the voltage response. The spreadsheet/PRC is the right starting point and tracks circuit simulation closely. Board caps should "do no harm": enough low-inductance parts that mounted-cap inductance is a small fraction of the Bandini loop. Optimize values for damping; OPD is the cost-effective way to cut the peak. A peak can never go below its Z0, but damping can approach it, and load current is often the largest Bandini loss. The model-to-hardware match on a real FPGA validates the whole approach.
