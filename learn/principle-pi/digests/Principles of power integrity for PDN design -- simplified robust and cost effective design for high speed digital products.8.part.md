# L1 overview

- **5.1 Why use capacitors** — Capacitors fill the impedance gap between the VRM and the chip clock, and an ideal capacitor's impedance falls as 1/(ωC): a 0.1 µF part is about 1 Ω at 1 MHz. The catch is that a real capacitor's equivalent series inductance reverses that trend at high frequency, so the chapter's job is to connect physical construction to the impedance profile.
- **5.2 Equivalent circuit models** — A mounted 0603 MLCC is modeled with remarkable accuracy by a three-element series RLC, where C, ESL and ESR are all defined relative to that model in a specific mounting and measurement configuration. The same model also fits X2Y-style parts (R = 0.01 Ω, C = 180 nF, L = 0.43 nH) and scales to arrays of capacitors.
- **5.3 Combining identical capacitors** — n identical capacitors in parallel yield Cn = n·C1, Ln = L1/n, Rn = R1/n, so the impedance curve keeps the same SRF and q-factor but scales down everywhere. Parallel paralleling reduces mounting inductance but not spreading inductance, making it a direct way to engineer lower ESL.
- **5.4 Parallel resonance between two capacitors** — Two capacitors with different SRFs create an impedance peak between them, the signature parallel resonance that is the single most important feature of any PDN. The PRF occurs where the two reactances cross, roughly at the resonance of the series-combined L and C, and can be predicted from L1, L2, C1, C2.
- **5.5 Peak impedance at the PRF** — The peak height depends on three knob groups: bring capacitor values closer (within a factor of two, about three values per decade), raise ESR, and lower ESL. Simulations show the C1/C2 ratio of 0.5–2 is where peak reduction is steepest, and realistic ESR (which rises as C falls) further lowers peaks when values are far apart, motivating controlled-ESR parts.
- **5.6 Engineering the capacitance** — Capacitance comes from overlapping plates: C = 0.225·Dk·n·A/h with A in in² and h in mils, and a bare-air quarter-sized pair gives about 1.4 pF. On a board, capacitance per area in nF/in² is roughly 1/h(mils), so a 10 in × 10 in plane pair at 3 mils is only about 30 nF — planes exist for low inductance, not bulk capacitance.
- **5.7 Temperature and voltage stability** — High-Dk ferroelectrics bring high fields and non-linear polarizability, so capacitance shifts with voltage and temperature. Class 1 (NP0/C0G, Dk 20–40) is stable and used for filters; class 2 (barium titanate, Dk 200–14,000) is the decoupling workhorse rated by EIA codes such as X5R (−55 to +85 °C, ±15%) and X7R (to +125 °C), and DC bias can dominate the loss.
- **5.8 How much capacitance is enough** — The honest answer needs time-domain simulation including the VRM, since LDOs and SMPS are non-linear and time-variant. A first-order estimate comes from making the bulk capacitor impedance hit target at the frequency where the VRM stops working: Cbulk = 1/(2π·fVRM-max·Ztarget), or equivalently I·Δt/ΔV, and the two derivations agree.
- **5.9 ESR first- and second-order models** — ESR has two loss mechanisms: dielectric loss, modeled with a parallel RC tied to the dissipation factor (G = 2πfC·Df), and plate current redistribution that raises resistance as √f. Lowest ESR falls in the 10–100 MHz band, and nominally identical 100 µF 1210 parts vary threefold (0.003–0.01 Ω) between vendors, so a constant R fitted at SRF is often good enough.
- **5.10 Estimating ESR from spec sheets** — For a given body size, more plates means more capacitance and less series resistance, which yields usable empirical laws: ESR ≈ 0.32 Ω coefficient for 0603 X5R and 0.20 Ω for 0402 X5R, both scaling as C^−0.6-ish. This lets you compute ESR in SPICE from capacitance alone, and shows q-factor only creeps down with larger C (about 4 to 2 for 0603 at 2 nH).
- **5.11 Controlled ESR capacitors** — Vendors build parts with deliberately larger series resistance (100 mΩ to 1.2 Ω), by narrowing plate metallization or adding a printed resistor; Murata's 0816 1 µF series offers 0.1/0.22/0.47/1 Ω. The point is not a lower minimum impedance but a lower parallel-resonance peak and much lower q-factor, which damps VRM and die-to-package resonances.
- **5.12 Mounting inductance** — A capacitor's total ESL splits into mounting inductance, cavity spreading inductance, and via-pair inductance to the package. Using via-pair and microstrip formulas, a typical 0603 on a 10-mil cavity with 30-mil traces comes to about 2 nH; backside BGA mounting, reverse-aspect-ratio bodies, X2Y/IDC structures, via-in-pad, short wide traces and a shallow cavity all push this toward 0.2–0.5 nH.
- **5.13 Vendor S-parameter models** — Vendor S-parameters are measured in a specific series fixture, so their inductance reflects the fixture, not your board. They may also be silently "corrected" for passivity (identical S11 = S22 to eight decimals), corrupted yet published, or measured at 100 Hz on a bridge so a "10 nF" part is really 5.8 nF.
- **5.14 Analyzing vendor S-parameters** — You can still use them responsibly: short port 2, get one-port S11, convert to complex Z, then extract C from the low-frequency imaginary part, ESL from the high-frequency imaginary part, and ESR from the impedance minimum. The extracted 47 nF 0402 example fits C = 42.6 nF, ESL = 0.71 nH, ESR = 0.0298 Ω, but the flat-R model underestimates damping above SRF by more than 2×.
- **5.15 Higher-bandwidth capacitor model** — When mounting inductance drops well below 1 nH, the capacitor reveals distributed behavior: at 300 MHz a 1 µF part measures 0.4 Ω where the RLC model says 0.9 Ω. A lossy transmission line (≤10 sections, X5R Df ≈ 0.01) captures falling inductance and rising ESR with frequency; the plate stack behaves as a quarter-wave open stub, so its true L is 2.47× the lumped ESL, and the model doubles as an embedded-capacitor two-port.
- **5.16 Bottom line** — Ten takeaways restate the chapter: RLC models work to about 10 MHz, identical parallel parts scale, two different values create the critical parallel-resonance peak, and the three knobs are ESL, value spacing and ESR. Mounted ESL below 4 nH is always achievable, below 2 nH is a good design, below 0.5 nH is excellent — and below 1 nH you must switch to the transmission-line model.

- `#L229-L269` — Tip: the parallel resonant peak is the most important feature of a PDN, caused by the low-SRF branch raising impedance while the high-SRF branch lowers it; the PRF is where the two reactances cross, with the stated formula and a worked example (L = 2 nH, 10 nF and 1000 nF) matching Figure 5.6.
- `#L270-L299` — Section 5.5: the peak impedance at PRF is hard to compute analytically but is set by three term groups involving L1, R1, R2 and C2; the three design knobs are larger C in the high-SRF branch (values closer together), smaller ESL in the low-SRF branch, and larger R1 and R2.
