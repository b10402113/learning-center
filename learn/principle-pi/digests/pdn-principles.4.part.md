# L1 overview

- **Ch3 §3.1 — Why measure low impedance** — PDN design is designing to a target impedance profile from DC to the bandwidth of the fastest signals; targets span >1 Ω down to <1 mΩ. Simulation designs each element and the whole ecology, but measurement verifies the part, the simulator, and the physical-to-model translation, and it is the debug tool when the ecology misses target. The hard requirement: resolve 1 mΩ up to >1 GHz.
- **Ch3 §3.2 — V/I definition** — Z(f) = V(f)/I(f) with complex V and I (Figure 3.1): drive a sine, measure current amplitude and phase. This is the basis of low-end impedance analyzers and of SPICE's impedance probe. Its ceiling is the highest frequency at which current can be measured directly; above ~100 MHz the reflection definition is cheaper.
- **Ch3 §3.3 — Reflection definition** — Signals are a voltage wave plus a circulating current loop that always propagates (Figure 3.2); any change in instantaneous impedance launches a reflected wave (Figure 3.3). ρ = (Z2−Z1)/(Z2+Z1), so Z2 = Z1(1+ρ)/(1−ρ), and with Z1 = 50 Ω, Zin = 50(1+ρ)/(1−ρ). Because all reflected sines at one frequency sum to one sine, ρ encodes the total integrated input impedance of fixture plus DUT, including every multiple bounce (Figures 3.4–3.5).
- **Ch3 §3.4 — VNA and S-parameters** — A VNA separates incident from reflected waves, unlike a scope that only sees total voltage (Figure 3.6). Its port is a source behind a precision 50 Ω resistor feeding precision 50 Ω coax (Figure 3.7); incident is half the source voltage, measured as Vref, and reflected is Vtotal − Vincident. S11 = reflected/incident = return loss, S21 magnitude in dB = insertion loss, and Zin = 50(1+S11)/(1−S11) is pure complex algebra to >50 GHz with no model assumed.
- **Ch3 §3.5 — DIP-lead example** — Two adjacent leads of a ceramic DIP soldered to an SMA (Figure 3.8), measured 10 MHz–1 GHz. S11 sits at ~0 dB so all impedance information lives in S11 phase — do not confuse phase of S11 with phase of Z. Log-log impedance (Figure 3.11) shows capacitive behavior to ~800 MHz then inductive; a single 4.8 pF capacitor fits the low-frequency data (Figure 3.12). This fitting loop is "measurement-based modeling" / "hacking the interconnect."
- **Ch3 §3.6 — 1-inch wire loop, one port** — A 1-inch copper loop (~3-inch circumference) on one VNA port (Figure 3.13); 1/10 wavelength lands at ~400 MHz, so below that it must look like an inductor. Extracted Z is an ideal series RL, R = Re{Z}, L = Im{Z}/ω (Figures 3.14–3.15). Above 400 MHz L climbs from distributed effects, and the data goes noisy below ~0.1 Ω. Three open questions: fixture inductance vs loop inductance, skin-depth vs artifact, and the 0.1 Ω noise floor.
- **Ch3 §3.7 — One-port limits** — 1 Ω gives |S11| = 0.9608; 0.1 Ω gives −0.996, i.e. −0.035 dB, at the calibration reproducibility limit, and below 0.1 Ω S11 collapses. A lossless fixture line still rotates phase: simulating 0→1 inch in 0.25-inch steps on an ideal 0.01 Ω + 1 nH load inflates extracted L and fake frequency-dependent R (Figure 3.16). Microprobing calibrated to the tip (Figure 3.17) removes fixture length but adds contact resistance: 0.05 Ω gold-to-gold to 2 Ω oxidized solder/copper; 20 pH/mil means a 5-mil scrub shifts L by 100 pH. Practical floor ≈0.05 Ω and 0.1 nH (Figure 3.18), which above 100 MHz limits C to <0.01 µF and L to >0.1 nH — fatal when on-die C is 0.001 µF and via L is 0.01 nH.
- **Ch3 §3.8 — Kelvin four-wire** — Dry-metal contact constricts current into tiny real contact area (spreading/constricting resistance): ~20 mΩ gold-to-gold at 100 g force, >100 mΩ with oxide, up to 2 Ω on solder/Al/Cu. Two-wire sensing measures DUT plus both contact resistances. Lord Kelvin's fix: separate two current leads from two voltage leads; the high-impedance voltmeter makes voltage-lead contact resistance irrelevant (Figure 3.19). This is the basis for both DC and RF low-impedance measurement.
- **Ch3 §3.9 — Two-port Kelvin VNA** — Port 1 drives the current loop (Vincident/50 Ω), port 2 senses the voltage with independent contacts (Figure 3.20). Near-short DUT gives ρ ≈ −1, voltage collapses, net DUT current ≈ 2× incident, and S21 = Z/25, so Z = 25 × S21 for Z ≪ 50 Ω; phase of S21 equals phase of Z. In dB, Z = 25 × 10^(S21dB/20), with 20log(25) = 28 dB: −20 dB → 2.5 Ω, −40 dB → 0.25 Ω, −60 dB → 25 mΩ. Impedance in dB behaves like amplitude (20, not 10), referenced to 1 Ω ("dBΩ"): 0 dB = 1 Ω, −20 dB = 0.1 Ω, −60 dB = 1 mΩ. Exact form Z = 25×S21/(1−S21) holds for all Z and reduces to 25×S21 when small; the approximation fails above ~−20 dB (10 Ω). With high power, averaging, and narrow IF filtering, −90 dB S21 is routine, i.e. ~0.8 mΩ, enabling <1 mΩ to >1 GHz.
- **Ch3 §3.10 — Loop, two ports** — Same 1-inch loop with both SMA signal pins on one end and both returns on the other (Figure 3.23). S11 is useless here; S21 converts directly with no model or fitting (Figures 3.24–3.25). Two-port inductance is 5 nH lower than one-port — that difference is the SMA fixture's series inductance. Two-port R at 10 MHz is ~8 mΩ versus a much noisier one-port value.
- **Ch3 §3.11 — Fixture artifacts** — "Hacking": fit an equivalent circuit, compare to measurement; agreement only proves consistency, never correctness, so stack many consistency checks. Second-order model adds identical lossless transmission lines (Z0, TD) either side of the RL loop (Figure 3.27), four parameters R, L, Z0, TD. Fixture phase shifts imaginary impedance into the real part, faking frequency-dependent R; at low frequency the lines vanish, so R and L come from the low-frequency asymptote, then Z0 ≈ 50 Ω and TD are tuned for best fit. Fitted start of the loop: R = 0.005 Ω, L = 23 nH.
