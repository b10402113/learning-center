# L1 overview

- **Ch6 §6.11 (cont.) — spreading inductance between two contact points** — Current density in a cavity is wildly non-uniform: it spreads radially from one via contact and pinches down into the other, and outside a region of diameter D ≈ 2s there is almost no current (Figure 6.22, HyperLynx PI), so only a 3D field solver can compute it. Cavity 10 in square, 10 mil thick: impedance looks like a pure inductor up to ~60 MHz (the 1/20-wavelength limit) and the 1 MHz value gives the loop inductance — 2.55 mΩ for 0.25 in spacing and 4.0 mΩ for 2.5 in (Figure 6.21). Ten-times the spacing raises L by less than 2×; sheet inductance is 32 pH/mil × 10 mil = 320 pH/sq and the pair always lands near two squares. Empirical form: L_point-point = k·L_point-cavity with k ≈ 2, i.e. L ≈ 2·(µ0h/2π)·ln(2s/d) = (µ0h/π)ln(2s/d); at s = 1 in, d = 10 mil the log term is 5.3 giving 54 pH/mil × h (h = plane spacing in mils, µ0 = 32 pH/mil). Priorities: cavity thickness first order, spacing and contact diameter both only logarithmic — hence multiple vias per capacitor pad widen the effective contact diameter, not just the via loop.

- **Ch6 §6.12 — capacitor/cavity parallel resonance** — A real capacitor is a series RLC with SRF = 1/(2π√(L_mount·C)); 2 nH mounting plus 100 nF puts SRF near 10 MHz. Below SRF the cap is capacitive, above it inductive. Mounted across a cavity it is in parallel: capacitances add at low frequency, the cap is invisible at high frequency, and in between the cap's inductance resonates with the cavity's capacitance at f = 1/(2π√(L_mount·C_cavity)), producing an impedance *peak* (Figure 6.24, L = 2 nH, C = 100 nF, R = 25 mΩ). The ~10 MHz dip is the sum of capacitor ESL + via mounting + plane spreading inductance; the ~100 MHz dip is the measurement port's spreading inductance against plane capacitance and is largely unaffected by the discrete cap.

- **Ch6 §6.13 — when capacitor location matters** — At an observation point the cap presents L_mount in series with L_spreading, so two regimes: Case 1 L_mount ≫ L_spread → cavity "transparent", position irrelevant; Case 2 L_spread ≫ L_mount → position matters. Quantified with L_spread ≈ 54 pH/mil × h at s = 1 in, d = 10 mil: a 2 mil cavity gives ~0.1 nH against 2 nH mounting (transparent, Figure 6.25), while 20 mil gives 1.22 nH at 2 in and 0.75 nH at 0.2 in (log terms 5.99 vs 3.70), so moving 10× closer buys only ~40% (Figure 6.26). Figure of merit ≈ 41 pH/mil × h; requiring spreading < 20% of a 2 nH mounting (< 0.4 nH) yields the rule: **h > 10 mil not transparent, h < 10 mil transparent** on server boards — the real reason thin dielectric always matters. Counter-case: a 1 in × 0.2 in power trace is 5 squares; at 2 mil dielectric (64 pH/sq) that is 320 pH against a 500 pH well-mounted cap, so traces break transparency even in thin cavities.

- **Ch6 §6.14 — saturating the spreading inductance** — In a transparent cavity n capacitors behave as n parallel lumped caps, L ∝ 1/n; once the parallel ESL falls below the spreading inductance from the cap field to the observation point, L stops dropping — the spreading inductance is "saturated" and more caps buy nothing. Ring model: L ≈ (µ0h/2π)·ln(b/a) from the BGA power/ground contact radius a to the capacitor ring radius b; with h = 10 mil, a = 0.25 in, b = 1 in → ~71 pH, so when total parallel ESL < 71 pH the drop is saturated. Three knobs (Eq. 6.78): thinner cavity, larger a (spread BGA P/G pads outward), smaller b (caps closer to package). Distribute caps uniformly around the package to keep current paths from overlapping; caps placed under the BGA can help when saturated.

- **Ch6 §6.15 — modal resonances viewed as a transmission line** — The high-frequency region of a cavity impedance is periodic peaks from wave propagation, boundary reflection, and constructive interference. For a uniform line with delay TD = Len/v, the round-trip phase is Δθ = 2·TD·f cycles; when Δθ = n (integer) the returning wave adds in phase on every pass and voltage builds without bound. Same boundary conditions at both ends (both high or both low versus Z0) → half-wave condition Len = n·λ/2, f_n = n·v/(2Len); different boundary conditions add a half-cycle flip → quarter-wave odd multiples (1/4, 3/4, 5/4…). Modal frequencies are intrinsic (length and material only); whether they are *excited* depends on signal spectrum and the source/line impedance mismatch — bigger mismatch, bigger peaks, but the frequencies never move. Worked case: 6 in FR4 → 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 GHz, uniformly spaced 0.5 GHz (Figure 6.28, ADS, 0–1.5 GHz sweep; Figure 6.29 half-wave fits).

- **Ch6 §6.16 — input impedance maps the modes** — Z_in = Z_port·(1+S11)/(1−S11); for a 50 Ω port into an open 50 Ω line S11 = 1 at resonance so Z_in peaks (formally infinite without loss). High Z_in means a small injected current makes a large voltage — the practical definition of a modal resonance, and why the cavity impedance curve is a direct mode map. The *shapes and heights* of the peaks depend on Z0, terminations, and loss; the peak *frequencies* do not. Figure 6.30 shows a 6 in 50 Ω FR4 line with a zoom comparing 1 Ω and 50 Ω lines at the n = 1 peak.

- **Ch6 §6.17 — attenuation damps the peaks** — Conductor and dielectric loss both rise with frequency, so the multiply-reflected waves weaken and peak heights drop; with enough round-trip loss Z_in flattens to Z0 (Figure 6.31). Atotal = Aconductor + Adielectric with Aconductor = 4.34·RLen/Z0 and Adielectric = 4.34·GLen·Z0, and Z0 ≈ (h/w)√(µ/ε) from the parallel-plate model (Figure 6.32). Dielectric loss cancels every geometry term and depends only on Dk and Df, rising linearly with f (dipole rotation). Above the skin-depth limit RLen = 2/(σδw), so the width cancels against Z0's 1/w and conductor attenuation becomes **independent of line width but inversely proportional to dielectric thickness**. Thin dielectric therefore wins twice — lower Z0 (lower peaks) and higher conductor attenuation — the single most effective way to kill modal peaks (Figure 6.33, 1 mil vs 10 mil). Higher Dk lowers Z0 and adds damping, but never accept a larger h to get it. 2D arrays of small TL segments [2]–[5] were the 1990s/2000s homemade PI tool; today full-wave 3D solvers output S-parameters, with port setup the critical skill.

- **Ch6 §6.18 — cavity modes in two dimensions** — A cavity propagates in X, Y, and diagonally, so modes carry two indices: f_mn = (v/2)·√((m/Lx)² + (n/Ly)²) with v = c/√Dk; for a square cavity f_mn = (v/2L)·√(m² + n²). A 6 in FR4 square (v ≈ 5.9 in/ns) gives 0.49·√(m²+n²) GHz: 1,0 at 0.5; 1,1 at 0.71; 2,0 at 1.0; 2,1 at 1.12; 2,2 at 1.41; 3,0 at 1.5; 3,1 at 1.58; 3,2 at 1.8; 4,0 at 2.0 — matching the simulated corner-probed impedance to 2 GHz (Figure 6.34). Modal frequencies are intrinsic and independent of probe location; Figures 6.35/6.36 show the standing-voltage patterns and the odd/even suppression. Driving from the cavity center puts a null on every odd mode (the reflected wave returns a half cycle out of phase and cancels), so only even-even modes appear: 2,0, 2,2, 4,0 below 2 GHz (Figure 6.37). Excitation efficiency, not mode existence, depends on injection point.

- **Ch6 §6.19 — transfer impedance as a spreading-inductance probe** — Self-impedance from one port is always polluted at high frequency by that port's spreading inductance. The impedance matrix separates this: V = Z·I, diagonal Z11/Z22 are self-impedances (equal to one-port measurements), off-diagonal Z21 = V2/I1 (with I2 = 0) is the transfer impedance — an impedance *shared* by the two points via the radial current wave, not an impedance *between* them. Same pads: simulated self = transfer, but measured self carries uncalibratable probe residuals while transfer stays clean. Circular cavity 3 in diameter, 10 mil thick, port 1 at center, ports 2–4 at growing R (Figure 6.39/6.40): self-impedance shows higher spreading L and lower SRF dip as the probe nears the edge, while modal peak frequencies stay fixed; transfer impedance's 2,1 dip frequency rises with R, its high-frequency spreading-inductance floor collapses, and at the rim the cavity looks almost purely capacitive up to the first mode. Measurement proof: a ~1 mil buried cavity probed with microprobes gives 80 pH one-port (probe contact + via + spreading), same value two-port on the same via; but two-port on **opposite ends of the via pair** cancels the via loop and reveals ~20 pH of pure spreading inductance — the order of the 32 pH sheet inductance for 1 mil — with only faint, thin-dielectric-damped modal ripples (Figures 6.41/6.42).

- **Ch6 §6.20 — the bottom line** — Ten claims: a cavity is two planes in close proximity with three impedance regions; low frequency is capacitive (and measures laminate Dk); the mid dip is an LC self-resonance whose L is the spreading inductance and C the parallel-plate capacitance; modal resonances dominate the top; effective in-cavity characteristic impedance is often well below 0.1 Ω; spreading inductance follows current constriction by geometry; it scales with the log of spacing; a mounted cap adds a parallel resonance of its inductance with cavity capacitance; modal frequencies depend only on cavity dimensions and dielectric; very thin dielectric is the most effective way to damp the high-frequency modes. References [1]–[7]: Xu/Hubing closed-form plane input impedance, Novak lossy thin-dielectric PDN, Smith/Anderson/Roy power-plane SPICE models, Lee/Barber MCM planes, Smith/Roy/Anderson frequency- and time-domain plane models, Na et al. package plane transient simulation, Pannala et al. resonance-driven ground bounce in lossy thin films.
