# L1 overview

- **Ch5 §5.1–5.3 — why capacitors, the series RLC model, and n identical capacitors in parallel** — Capacitors fill the impedance gap between where the VRM stops working and the chip's clock. An ideal capacitor has Zc = 1/(ωC): 0.1 µF is about 1 Ω at 1 MHz, and impedance falls forever with frequency (Figure 5.1). A real mounted MLCC, however, is modeled accurately by a single ideal series RLC up to well past its resonance (Figure 5.2, 0603 part; Figure 5.3, 0603 X2Y with R = 0.01 Ω, C = 180 nF, L = 0.43 nH), where "C", "ESL", and "ESR" are the equivalent element values needed to match measurement in a specific mounting. Putting n identical RLCs in parallel gives exactly the same profile shape with Cn = n·C1, Ln = L1/n, Rn = R1/n, so SRF = 1/(2π√(LC)) and the q-factor are unchanged and impedance drops by n at every frequency. Only the mounting loop inductance divides by n; spreading inductance from capacitor to load combines in a geometry-dependent way.

- **Ch5 §5.4–5.5 — parallel resonance and the peak impedance** — Two capacitors with different SRFs produce a new peak between them: a parallel resonance, called "the most important feature of any PDN." The peak sits where the two branch reactances are equal, X1 = X2, giving PRF = (1/2π)·√((L1+L2)/(L1·L2·(C1+C2))) (L in nH, C in nF, result in MHz); 2 nH with 10 nF ∥ 1000 nF gives 5 MHz, matching simulation (Figure 5.6). At resonance the current circulates through the series combination of the two C's and two L's. Peak impedance (Eq. 5.12) is set by three term groups — L1 (ESL of the lower-SRF branch), R1 and R2 (ESR of each branch), and C2 (capacitance of the higher-SRF branch) — and falls when the two capacitor values are brought within a factor of two (~3 values per decade), when ESL is lowered, and when ESR is raised. Zero ESR gives a minimum impedance of one ESR at SRF; equal capacitors halve it because the R's are in parallel.

- **Ch5 §5.6–5.7 — capacitance from geometry and dielectric stability classes** — Parallel-plate C = ε0·Dk·n·A/h with ε0 = 0.225 pF/in, A in in², h in mils: a 1-inch "quarter" pair 100 mils apart in air is ~1.7 pF. For board planes, capacitance per area in nF/in² is roughly 1/h(mils): 3 mil dielectric gives 0.3 nF/in² and a 10 × 10 in board only ~30 nF, which is why planes are for low inductance, not capacitance (Chapter 6). Real parts hold ±20%. Class 1 (NP0, magnesium niobate, Dk 20–40, <30 ppm/°K, stable but low value, used for filters); Class 2 (barium titanate, Dk 200–14,000, ferroelectric, the decoupling choice); Class 3 (very high Dk, not for MLCCs). EIA codes: X5R = −55 to +85 °C ±15%, X7R extends to +125 °C. Higher applied voltage reduces Dk and capacitance, and voltage sensitivity varies by vendor even within one material code — measure at the application voltage.

- **Ch5 §5.8 — how much bulk capacitance** — Frequency domain: bulk C must hold the PDN below Ztarget where the VRM runs out, Cbulk ≥ 1/(2π·fVRM-max·Ztarget). Example: 50 mΩ and 10 kHz gives ~300 µF (318 µF); when the required bulk is only ~100 µF, ceramics can supply it. Time domain: the bulk cap supplies charge until the VRM responds, Cbulk ≥ Istep·Δt/ΔV with ΔV ≤ 5%, and since Δt ≈ 1/(2π·f), both derivations give the identical first-order answer (50 mΩ, 10 µs ≈ 16 kHz). This is only a starting point: only time-domain simulation including the VRM is rigorous, because LDO/SMPS large-signal behavior is nonlinear and time variant, and a peak from VRM output inductance resonating with bulk C can destabilize the loop.

- **Ch5 §5.9–5.10 — the two ESR loss mechanisms and a spec-sheet ESR model** — ESR = conductor loss in the thin plates + dielectric loss in the ceramic. Dielectric loss is a parallel RC with G = 2πf·C·Df (Df = dissipation factor), so the real part rises toward low frequency; conductor loss comes from current crowding into the bottom plates to minimize loop inductance and follows R(f) = Rdc·√(f/F0), rising with frequency. The combination makes ESR minimum around the SRF, typically 10–100 MHz for ~1 nH parts (Figures 5.17–5.19; AVX 0402 10 nF, ESL 0.4 nH). Part-to-part spread is large: 15 nominally identical 1210 100 µF parts measured 0.003–0.01 Ω ESR, a 3× range that directly moves predicted peak impedance (Figure 5.20). A constant R matched to the SRF minimum predicts impedance as well as a frequency-dependent model over 10–100 MHz, and is smaller than vendor spread (Figure 5.21). From AVX SpiCAP data, ESR vs C fits one form for both body sizes, ESR = k·(C/nF)^−n, with k = 0.32 Ω for 0603 X5R and 0.20 Ω for 0402 X5R; with q = (1/R)·√(L/C) and 2 nH ESL this gives q ≈ 4 at low C and ≈ 2 at high C (Figure 5.23).

- **Ch5 §5.11 — controlled-ESR capacitors** — Vendors sell MLCCs with deliberately larger series resistance (100 mΩ to 1.2 Ω, independent of C), made by narrowing plate metallization or adding a printed resistor. Murata's 0816 reverse-aspect 1 µF comes in 0.1/0.22/0.47/1 Ω versus a typical 0.016 Ω (Figure 5.24); TDK's 10 µF family spans 20 mΩ–1 Ω at 0.6 nH (Figure 5.25). The benefit is not a higher single-cap minimum but a lower maximum parallel-resonance peak when caps are combined, plus low q for large C — useful for damping the VRM/bulk resonance and on-die-to-package resonance (Figure 5.26). Trade-off: available capacitance is smaller than a standard part in the same body.

- **Ch5 §5.12 (opening) — mounting inductance is one of three ESL pieces** — For a capacitor decoupling a BGA, the total loop splits into board mounting inductance, spreading inductance through the power/ground cavity, and the via-pair loop up to the package (Figure 5.27). This chapter engineers the mounting portion; Chapter 6 covers spreading.
