# Digest — UX/UI Design Course by Designership (part 6: Lean Branding)

## Overview (L1)

- **Lean Branding module** — Turns the wireframed Acomda AI project into a visual direction: formalize a lean brand strategy (3 "we are" + 3 "we are not" values + early-adopter proto-persona), learn colour fundamentals (wheel, meanings, cultural context, combinations), pick a primary brand colour, and assemble a moodboard of interior/UI/photography references that becomes the north-star guide for all UI design decisions.

## Sections (L2)

### Welcome to Lean Branding

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/6.0 Welcome to Lean Branding Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#welcome-to-lean-branding]]`
- Summary: Module opener for the Lean Branding track. It previews the whole arc — the lean branding process, forming a brand for the Acomda AI project, understanding and using colour, and creating a brand from scratch.
- Key claims: The module covers process, project application, colour, and hands-on brand creation; the final outcome is a self-built lean brand for Acomda AI.
- Learner-relevant: Sets expectations and motivates why visual/brand choices come right after wireframes.

### My lean branding process

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/6.1 My lean branding process Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#my-lean-branding-process]]`
- Summary: Frames visual styling as the layer applied on top of wireframes, with real consequences. It outlines the lessons ahead: how to think about colour, choose a palette, find inspiration, and finalize a UI direction.
- Key claims: The visual layer affects first impressions, accessibility, branding, and usability; identical functionality can give different first impressions based on styling; this is a lean branding process, not a full branding course.
- Learner-relevant: Gives the learner confidence that UI can be designed from scratch without formal brand-design training.

### What is a lean brand strategy

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/6.2 What is a lean brand strategy Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#what-is-a-lean-brand-strategy]]`
- Summary: Designers get pushback because they sell the output rather than the journey, narrative, story, and strategy. The lesson shows how to sell a design change by surfacing pain points and presenting a solution, then defines the three inputs of a lean brand strategy.
- Key claims: The "sell" is the strategy and journey, not the final design; identify pain points, objectives, story, and needs when proposing a visual direction; define 3 "we are" values, 3 "we are not" values, and the target audience — exactly three keeps meaning from being diluted; different values and target markets produce entirely different visual directions for the same UI (e.g. exclusive/prestige/elegant → dark, sharper corners, high-end photos vs community/warmth/fun → lighthearted, orange splashes, rounded components).
- Learner-relevant: Equips the learner to justify brand/UI direction to stakeholders and translate values into concrete visual attributes.

### Form your lean brand strategy

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/6.3 Form your lean brand strategy Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#form-your-lean-brand-strategy]]`
- Summary: A hands-on activity to write your own lean brand strategy using a provided word list: pick three "we are" values, three "we are not" values, and set the target market / early-adopter proto-persona.
- Key claims: Steps are define 3 "we are", 3 "we are not", then set target market or early adopter proto-persona; pick values that resonate (the words need not be perfect); the exercise teaches how a lean strategy informs design decisions and sets guardrails/boundaries; there is no right or wrong answer; example — Acomda AI prioritizes innovative, efficient, fun-filled and avoids exclusive, negative, unsafe, targeting couples in Australia struggling to find accommodation.
- Learner-relevant: Produces the learner's own written brand strategy that later anchors colour choice and the moodboard.

### Color wheel explained

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/6.4 Color wheel explained Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#color-wheel-explained]]`
- Summary: Introduces the colour wheel as the foundational tool for building a palette — a circular arrangement that shows relationships between primary, secondary, and tertiary colours.
- Key claims: Primary colours are red, blue, yellow and cannot be made by mixing; secondary colours are made by mixing equal amounts of two primaries — green (blue+yellow), orange (red+yellow), purple (red+blue); tertiary colours mix a primary with an adjacent secondary and there are six of them — red-orange, yellow-orange, yellow-green, blue-green (instructor calls blue-purple "blurple"), and red-purple; the wheel is simple but effective for understanding how to use colour in projects.
- Learner-relevant: Gives the learner the vocabulary and mixing rules needed to reason about palette construction.

### The meaning of each color

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/6.5 The meaning of each color Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#the-meaning-of-each-color]]`
- Summary: Explains that colours evoke emotions and associations, so understanding their symbolic meanings helps designers and marketers make informed decisions. It walks through the conventional meanings of each hue applied to the same design to show how feeling changes.
- Key claims: Red = passion, love, energy, power, danger (attention-grabbing, highlights important elements); blue = calmness, stability, trust, reliability (corporate/professional); green = growth, nature, renewal, environmental consciousness (good luck/health/prosperity in some cultures); yellow = happiness, optimism, warmth, energy (stimulates mental activity); orange = enthusiasm, creativity, warmth (red's energy + yellow's happiness, attention-grabbing); purple = royalty, luxury, creativity, spirituality, mystery, elegance; pink = love, romance, femininity, playfulness, innocence; brown = stability, reliability, earthiness, nature, simplicity; black = power, elegance, formality, mystery (also mourning/negativity in some contexts); white = purity, innocence, cleanliness, clarity, minimalism; grey = balance, reliability, stability, professionalism (light greys common to balance UI and avoid distracting colour); industry choices: professional-yet-light-hearted → vibrant blue, fun-and-warm → orange, premium-and-exclusive → dark mode. These are general rules of thumb that experienced designers can break.
- Learner-relevant: A concrete colour-meaning reference (aligns with the video's downloadable cheat sheet) to map a brand objective to a hue.

### Meaning of each color differ in different contexts

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/6.6 Meaning of each color differ in different contexts Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#meaning-of-each-color-differ-in-different-contexts]]`
- Summary: Shows that colour meaning shifts across cultures and contexts (weddings vs funerals vs traffic lights). The takeaway is to design for the context of your specific audience.
- Key claims: Red — Western: passion/love/danger; Chinese: good fortune/happiness/prosperity (celebrations, weddings, New Year); traffic: stop/warning. White — Western: purity/innocence/cleanliness (wedding dresses); Eastern (China, Japan, Korea): mourning/death/sadness (funerals). Green — nature/growth/environmental awareness; US financial context: money/success; traffic: go/safe to proceed. Yellow — happiness/optimism/energy; Egyptian: eternal life (tomb paintings); some countries: cowardice/deceit (e.g. "yellow-bellied" in the US). Blue — calmness/stability/trust/professionalism; Middle Eastern: protection and warding off evil. All are general rules that can be bent; a cheat sheet is provided.
- Learner-relevant: Prevents cultural missteps when choosing brand colour for a target market.

### Understanding color combinations

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/6.7 Understanding color combinations Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#understanding-color-combinations]]`
- Summary: Argues UI colour combinations differ from brand combinations because UI has limited real estate. Colour in UI should bring focus to specific elements and signal element state/intention, not cover the whole screen.
- Key claims: Most of the screen is neutral (white, light grey, or a dark background); colour is reserved for buttons, images, and small components such as tags or star ratings; primary and sometimes secondary colour are used mainly for buttons and branded CTAs; examples — Airbnb (white everything except CTAs), Facebook (white/light-grey with blue CTAs), Spotify (dark neutrals with green primary action plus photographic colour); the instructor prefers safe, harmonious combinations anchored on a single primary colour balanced by neutrals; don't overuse colour "for the sake of it", use it intently and in small doses.
- Learner-relevant: Gives a concrete rule for UI palettes — one primary plus neutrals — with real-app examples.

### Form your color palette

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/6.8 Form your color palette Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#form-your-color-palette]]`
- Summary: An activity to choose a primary brand colour (a hex code) for the project, draw a filled rectangle, and paste the hex into the project input. The learner can take inspiration from competitors' palettes or use a colour they already have in mind.
- Key claims: Select a single primary brand colour and use it consistently; the instructor's chosen colour is `4F46E5`, a crisp strong blue; blue is a very common brand colour, represents the innovative, efficient, fun-filled vibe sought for Acomda AI, and works with a light interface to still feel fun; the hex is pasted into the project to move one step closer to finalizing brand identity.
- Learner-relevant: Produces the concrete primary colour the learner will carry into all subsequent UI styling.

### Formalize your lean brand

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/6.09 Formalize your lean brand Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#formalize-your-lean-brand]]`
- Summary: The capstone activity: bring everything together by setting "we are"/"we are not" values, choosing primary (and optional secondary) colour, and building a moodboard. The moodboard mixes interior-design assets, UI design inspiration, and photography styles to define the platform's potential direction.
- Key claims: Moodboard columns are labelled office / UI / photo; use interior design as a reference for brand feeling (e.g. Pinterest searches like "blue office space"); find UI references on Dribbble and photo references on Unsplash, Pexels, iStock Photo, or Shutterstock (iStock/Shutterstock preferred for library size); photography should be consistent with the brand — well-lit with good contrast, matching a light high-contrast blue UI or richer/darker for an exclusive dark brand; the moodboard becomes the north star/reference for all subsequent UI design decisions.
- Learner-relevant: Delivers a finished lean brand artifact (values + colours + moodboard) that directly guides the upcoming UI design stage.
