# Digest — UX/UI Design Mastery Course (part 11: Launch your career)

## Overview (L1)

- 1. Embracing Possibilities: Unleash Your Full Potential — Motivational opener for the career section: UX/UI is reachable from almost any background, no degree or prior experience required. Frames the common student fears (no interviews, seemingly inaccessible jobs) and promises concrete tips and a student success story, with the caveat that the learner must work hard and apply what they learn.
- 2. Land UX/UI Design Job: Tips and Tricks to Succeed - part 1 — First half of the job-search playbook: keep learning, surround yourself with design, learn from the best designers and companies, and gather recommendations/social proof. Explains how to display recommendations on LinkedIn and the portfolio, and how to earn them via free work for businesses/charities or freelance gigs on Freelancer/Fiverr/Upwork.
- 3. Land UX/UI Design Job: Tips and Tricks to Succeed - part 2 — Second half of the playbook: network (online and offline), get real-life experience even without prior roles (volunteer, freelance, internship, competitions/workshops), practice confidence and speak the design language, and develop a positive, non-arrogant attitude, since recruiters assess personality as well as skills.
- 4. How to Create a Strong Case Study: A Step-by-Step Guide — Defines a UX/UI case study and gives a full portfolio case-study template: title/subtitle, project date, role, summary (problem statement, goals, KPIs), tools, final-design mockups, design process, and results. Stresses quality over quantity (~4 case studies, best one on top, recruiters spend ≤3 minutes), targeting the industry you want, and showing process—not just pretty images.
- 5. Build an outstanding online portfolio — Treats the portfolio as personal branding aimed at recruiters and other designers. Lists the three essentials (intro/about, case studies, professional social/contact info), two build paths (Dribbble/Behance vs. own website), website-build options (code vs. builders like Webflow/Wix), and domain/hosting basics (GoDaddy), recommending your own website.
- 6. Get Ready to Apply for UX/UI Design Jobs — Short readiness lesson: do market research first, focus on industries with strong UX/UI demand, stay flexible about applying across industries early on, and take advantage of booming demand plus onsite/hybrid/remote flexibility and good pay with positive user impact.
- 7. Where to Find UXUI Design Jobs — Job-search channels and strategy: LinkedIn first (filters, alerts, recruiter/team outreach), plus Otta, Indeed, and Dribbble/Behance job boards; search all related titles (Interaction/Digital/UX/Product Designer); expect and overcome degree/experience requirements; apply broadly and keep interviewing; diagnose rejections; use Glassdoor to screen companies.
- 8. Create a Job-Winning CV — CV/A4-first impressions plus the LinkedIn profile. Covers what to include (personal details, professional summary, reverse-chronological work experience, education/courses, skills/tools, LinkedIn link), a seven-point quality checklist, common mistakes to avoid, inspiration examples, and how to build a strong, discoverable LinkedIn profile.
- 9. Nail the Interview Process — Maps the typical UX/UI interview funnel (screening call, hiring-manager video, portfolio presentation, design/whiteboard challenge or critique, competency interview, offer) and coaches on first impressions and the two most common questions ("tell me about yourself", "why do you want to work for us"), with sample follow-up questions and a final reminder to apply the course before applying for jobs.

### 2. Land UX/UI Design Job: Tips and Tricks to Succeed - part 1

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui1/11. Launch your UX.UI Design career/2. Land UXUI Design Job Tips and Tricks to Succeed - part 1.en.srt#land-uxui-design-job-tips-and-tricks-to-succeed-part-1]]`

- Learner-relevant: Converts "I have no network/experience" into concrete first moves—how to build the credibility signals recruiters look for before you have a job.

### 3. Land UX/UI Design Job: Tips and Tricks to Succeed - part 2

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui1/11. Launch your UX.UI Design career/3. Land UXUI Design Job Tips and Tricks to Succeed - part 2.en.srt#land-uxui-design-job-tips-and-tricks-to-succeed-part-2]]`
- Summary: Continues the playbook with networking, experience-building, confidence, and attitude. It explains why networking matters ("your network is your net worth", ~31% of job seekers find roles via professional connections, referrals are often rewarded), and how to gain real-life experience when you have none (volunteer, freelance/paid projects, internship, competitions/workshops—freelancing being the fastest). It closes with confidence, design vocabulary, and a positive-but-not-arrogant attitude.
- Key claims: networking builds relationships and opens doors, online (LinkedIn, social media, design sites, workshops) and offline (conferences, company events)—start on LinkedIn; recruiters prioritize experience, so manufacture it via freelancing (most recommended), free volunteering, internships, or competitions/workshops; freelancing is just you—a professional website or your portfolio plus a client channel (Fiverr/Upwork/Freelancer) or direct outreach; confidence wins (the two-doctor analogy) and comes from real knowledge/experience; learn the design language (user research, KPIs, design system, storyboards, conversion rate, A/B testing); attitude matters—recruiters assess personality and "easy to work with", so avoid arrogance.
- Learner-relevant: Turns the "need experience to get experience" deadlock into an actionable path, and coaches the soft skills (confidence, language, attitude) interviews actually test.

### 4. How to Create a Strong Case Study: A Step-by-Step Guide

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui1/11. Launch your UX.UI Design career/4. How to Create a Strong Case Study_ A Step-by-Step Guide.mhtml#how-to-create-a-strong-case-study-a-step-by-step-guide]]`
- Summary: Defines a case study as an in-depth breakdown of a project—the problem, approach, solution, and impact—used to show recruiters your qualities, and states you cannot have a portfolio without them. It argues quality over quantity (~4 case studies, best one first; ~80% of recruiters spend ≤3 minutes), recommends targeting the industry you want to work in, and provides a full template plus a writing-then-design workflow (draft the copy in Figma, then make it visually pleasing, then export to Behance or your site).
- Key claims: a case study must show your design process and decisions, not just attractive final images; aim for ~4 case studies with the best at the top—one strong project beats several mediocre ones; focus case studies on your target industry (e.g., B2C music for a music-streaming employer, complex B2B for a high-tech company) to signal product/business understanding; create it in Figma and export; the template sections are title/subtitle, project date/duration (e.g., 1–3 months), your role and collaborators, project summary (problem statement + goal + KPIs), tools used, final-design mockups as a teaser, design process (research → define → ideate → prototype → test, adjusted per scope/resources/deadlines), and results (success metrics, reflections, next steps, lessons, reviews); avoid jumping straight to design; include a problem-statement example (specific vs. broad) and KPI examples (unique users, retention, conversion, time per task, satisfaction).
- Learner-relevant: The centerpiece of the portfolio—this gives the exact structure and content recruiters expect, and the quality bar (process, industry fit, brevity) that separates hires from the pile.

### 5. Build an outstanding online portfolio

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui1/11. Launch your UX.UI Design career/5. Build an outstanding online portfolio.en.srt#build-an-outstanding-online-portfolio]]`
- Summary: Frames the portfolio as your personal brand, whose audience is recruiters and other designers, so it should foreground UX/UI skills over unrelated work. It notes users form an opinion in ~50 ms and recruiters may sift 100 portfolios, so it must stand out; lists the three essentials; compares platform profiles vs. a personal website (recommending your own), and covers builders, domains, and hosting.
- Key claims: you need an online portfolio to land a job, whatever your direction; build it as a personal brand for recruiters/designers and de-emphasize non-UX skills like drawing; the three essentials are an intro/about statement, case studies, and professional social media/contact info (LinkedIn, CV); add a pro-tip of lovely transitions/animations/3D to instantly attract attention; two build options are Dribbble/Behance profiles (free, fast) or your own website (costs more and takes longer but looks far more professional, experienced, and credible)—aim for your own while keeping Behance/Dribbble for feedback; build via code (HTML/CSS/JS) or a website builder (recommended: Webflow, Wix); you need both a domain name (your web address, keep it professional and recognizable, e.g. johnamora.com) and web hosting (where files live; both available via GoDaddy), then connect the site to the domain per the builder's instructions.
- Learner-relevant: Gives the portfolio's purpose, must-haves, and a concrete build/publish path—turning "make a portfolio" into a checklist with clear trade-offs.

### 7. Where to Find UXUI Design Jobs

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui1/11. Launch your UX.UI Design career/7. Where to Find UXUI Design Jobs.en.srt#where-to-find-uxui-design-jobs]]`
- Summary: A channel-by-channel guide to finding UX/UI roles. LinkedIn is the top source (filters by location/work setting, job alerts, visible recruiters and design teams to connect with for rapport/referrals), followed by Otta and Indeed and also Dribbble/Behance job boards. It explains the interchangeable job titles to search, how to handle steep stated requirements, why to apply broadly, how to diagnose a stalled search, and how to vet employers via Glassdoor.
- Key claims: LinkedIn is the best place to find UX/UI jobs—many great companies advertise there, with location/work-setting filters, new-role notifications, and recruiter/design-team contacts for referrals; also use Otta and Indeed (plus the most popular sites in your country) and Dribbble/Behance job boards; search every related title—Interaction Designer, Digital Designer, UX Designer, Product Designer (Product Designer increasingly common and sometimes means accounting for business needs)—because asking only for "UX/UI designer" can hide low-competition gems; entry-level postings often demand a bachelor's degree and 3+ years, which is beatable with effort and the course's advice; apply to as many roles as possible (tailoring slightly) and keep interviewing until a contract is signed, since a single rejection can leave you at zero while multiple offers let you negotiate; if you never reach first interviews, something is wrong (weak online presence, case studies, CV/portfolio)—ask recruiters for feedback; use Glassdoor to screen companies and avoid those rated below 3 stars with many bad reviews.
- Learner-relevant: The tactical job-hunt playbook—where to look, what to search, how many to apply to, and how to interpret and fix rejections.

### 8. Create a Job-Winning CV

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui1/11. Launch your UX.UI Design career/8. Create a Job-Winning CV.mhtml#create-a-job-winning-cv]]`
- Summary: Covers the CV as the first impression (often before the portfolio) and the LinkedIn profile as a powerful CV-plus network asset. You typically submit a CV plus a portfolio link, and the portfolio is more important, but the CV still decides whether you get seen. It details what to include, a seven-point checklist, common mistakes, example CVs, and how to build a professional LinkedIn profile.
- Key claims: recruiters scan CVs for ~6–7 seconds in an F-shaped pattern, and ATS software parses/organizes CV data, so optimize for both; write for your target audience, mirror the job description's keywords/skills; include personal details (name, job title, email, phone, location, portfolio—use "UX/UI Designer" even if currently elsewhere), a professional summary, reverse-chronological work experience (design projects you did yourself count), education/courses, skills and tools (even partial knowledge, explained at interview), and a LinkedIn link (build with resume.io or from scratch); seven tips: keep to one page and concise, highlight quantified achievements, tailor to the job with matching keywords/tone, start bullets with strong action verbs, use white space and save as PDF, include a portfolio link, and proofread; mistakes to avoid: spelling/grammar errors, inconsistent design, missing portfolio link, not tailoring, listing duties instead of accomplishments, wrong length, hobbies, and a personal photo; LinkedIn is the most popular professional network and free—use a professional photo, compelling headline, strength-showcasing summary, relevant experience, key skills, industry connections, and shared relevant content; a complete LinkedIn profile can attract recruiter invitations without applying.
- Learner-relevant: The document that gets you read—gives the exact CV sections, the quality checklist, the pitfalls, and the LinkedIn profile that compounds the CV.

### 9. Nail the Interview Process

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui1/11. Launch your UX.UI Design career/9. Nail the Interview Process.en.srt#nail-the-interview-process]]`
- Summary: Teaches how to excel at each interview stage. It maps the typical UX/UI funnel, coaches first impressions (smile, energy, eye contact, confident body language, visible passion, since the first moments are decisive), and deep-dives the two most common questions with model answers. It lists other likely questions and ends with a reminder to apply and complete the course's work before applying for jobs.
- Key claims: the typical process is screening video/phone interview → hiring-manager video (often head of design) → portfolio presentation of case studies → design/whiteboard challenge or design critique → competency-based interview → offer; nail the first impression with a smile, positive energy, eye contact, confident body language, and visible passion for the role and company; "tell me about yourself" needs a short, practiced, design-focused story (who you are, why UX/UI, your experience, how you keep improving) delivered without filler and with a smile; "why do you want to work for us" must avoid negatives about past employers (or they'll fear you'll do the same to them) and must not cite salary/unemployment/disliking your job—instead research the company and cite its mission, values, or product with passion; other likely questions include your design process and methods, a challenging recent project, user research/testing experience, design heroes, favorite great-UX examples, what makes a great UX designer, and where you find inspiration; apply everything learned in the course before applying—applying without doing the work leads to frustration and rejection.
- Learner-relevant: Gives a stage-by-stage interview game plan plus scripted prep for the two questions that shape the outcome—directly converting the unit's portfolio/CV work into an offer.
