# Digest — UX/UI Design Course by Designership (part 5: Wireframes)

## Overview (L1)

- 5.0 Welcome to Wireframes — 33-second module opener; frames the wireframes module: what wireframes are, how to create them, implementing engagement/growth models during ideation, and building your own wireframes for the project.
- 5.1 What is a wireframe — defines a wireframe as a basic visual layout emphasizing functionality, layout, and UX; covers lo-fi vs mid/high-fi forms and when to pick each based on project complexity and stakeholders.
- 5.2 Sketches vs low-hi-fi wireframes vs rapid prototyping — compares lo-fi, mid-fi, high-fi, and rapid prototyping; each suits different goals: personal brainstorming, team alignment, stakeholder presentation, or fast user feedback.
- 5.3 F Pattern — explains the F-pattern of digital content scanning through three examples (Google results, social feeds, landing pages) and how to use it to place high-value elements.
- 5.4 F pattern challenge — three hands-on activities applying the F-pattern to a CRM dashboard, an article's reading experience/typography, and a mobile mailbox's CTA placement.
- 5.5 My IA priority framework — teaches that prioritization requires an objective, then ranks screen elements by importance relative to that objective, using a Snapseed editing screen and the Apple MacBook Air buy page.
- 5.6 IA priority challenge — three activities ranking components by visual hierarchy and re-prioritizing a search results layout as the objective shifts (refine search, show as many accommodations as possible).
- 5.7 Create your wireframes — the module's main activity: build all project wireframes in Whimsical from the job stories, sitemap, and user flow; the instructor then walks through his desktop, tablet, and mobile wireframes.
- 5.8 What is the - Fogg behaviour model — introduces Dr BJ Fogg's model (Motivation, Ability, Trigger must converge for behaviour), illustrated with a red-light example and a personal Strava cycling story.
- 5.9 Fogg behaviour model - Challenge — applies the Fogg model to five key Accom.ai actions (search, click listing, use filters, book, pay) plus retention, defining motivation/ability/trigger for each.
- 5.10 Optimize your wireframes — follow-up activity asking the learner to make small Fogg-driven optimizations to existing wireframes to encourage key actions, keeping changes minor and testable.

### 5.1 What is a wireframe

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/5.1 What is a wireframe Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#what-is-a-wireframe]]`
- Summary: With structure and flows understood, focus shifts to how each page is laid out. A wireframe is a basic visual representation of a site/app layout and flow, made of simple shapes, lines and labels, outlining structure and content before visual design/branding. Covers lo-fi vs mid/high-fi and choosing based on complexity and stakeholders.
- Key claims: Wireframes outline structure/content using simple shapes, lines and labels; they range from lo-fi (hand sketches/basic digital) to mid/high-fi (Figma/Whimsical with more detail); simple projects with few stakeholders can start lo-fi and move quickly to design, while complex multi-stakeholder projects may need mid/high-fi for approval first; wireframes help visualize layout, surface issues early, keep focus on solving UX/UI problems without visual distraction, and enable rapid iteration.
- Learner-relevant: Establishes the core artifact for the module and the rule of thumb for choosing fidelity.

### 5.2 Sketches vs low-hi-fi wireframes vs rapid prototyping

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/5.2 Sketches vs low-hi-fi wireframes vs rapid prototyping Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#sketches-vs-low-hi-fi-wireframes-vs-rapid-prototyping]]`
- Summary: Breaks wireframing approaches into lo-fi, mid-fi, high-fi, and rapid prototyping. Lo-fi are messy notebook scribbles for personal brainstorming; mid-fi add visual cues and are used by nimble teams in Whimsical/Figma; high-fi are polished software mockups for large/complex/regulated orgs; rapid prototyping produces interactive prototypes for real user insight early.
- Key claims: Lo-fi is quick and for internal use only — never present it; mid-fi clarifies specific requirements and works for fast-moving teams wanting general alignment; high-fi is best for presenting polished concepts to stakeholders, common in mature organizations with rigorous approval (insurance, government); rapid prototyping builds something interactive (Figma variables, Protopie, even an engineer-built app) to get real feedback and catch issues early; there is no right/wrong level — pick the one that fits the project and moment.
- Learner-relevant: Gives a decision guide for which fidelity to produce at each project stage.

### 5.3 F Pattern

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/5.3 F Pattern Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#f-pattern]]`
- Summary: The F-pattern describes how people tend to scan digital content: a horizontal sweep near the top, a second shorter sweep, then a vertical scan down the left side. Three examples show it in action — Google search results, social media feeds (Facebook, Twitter, Threads), and conversion-focused landing pages.
- Key claims: Google places the most relevant result first and users scan down the left, reading titles; social feeds put newest content at the top with users scanning left-side headlines/descriptions; landing pages place a value headline up top, a supporting subheading, and a graphical element on the right, while users scan the left before deciding to act; understanding the pattern lets designers keep users engaged and drive desired actions.
- Learner-relevant: Introduces the scanning model that drives layout prioritization in later lessons.

### 5.4 F pattern challenge

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/5.4 F pattern challenge Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#f-pattern-challenge]]`
- Summary: Three activities applying the F-pattern. Activity 1 places heading, "Add customer" CTA, and sidebar navigation on a CRM/customers table wireframe (title and CTA in primary top-left real estate, add-customer lower for breathing room). Activity 2 restructures an article (Figma Config 2023 updates) using heading hierarchy (H2/H3, never duplicate) and a scannable bulleted summary. Activity 3 places search bar, new-email, and filter CTAs on an iOS mailbox, weighing thumb reach.
- Key claims: Identify where the F "sits" to find primary real estate; prioritize orientation first (title/nav), then contextual content, then actions; apply the F-pattern to typography by making headings obvious and scannable; keep heading levels consistent and never duplicate them; on mobile, place frequent actions (new email) low for thumb reach but keep search/refine grouped high; the pattern applies across IA, reading experience, and mobile app design.
- Learner-relevant: Converts the F-pattern concept into repeatable placement decisions across desktop and mobile.

### 5.5 My IA priority framework

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/5.5 My IA priority framework Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#my-ia-priority-framework]]`
- Summary: Ranking elements without an objective is meaningless — prioritization always requires a clear objective. The instructor demonstrates with a Snapseed brightness-editing screen (brightness scale > the photo > menu) and an Apple MacBook Air buy page, asking the learner to rank five components 1–5 by the page's objective (help the viewer select the right model).
- Key claims: You cannot prioritize without an objective — the objective defines the ranking criterion; priority reflects each element's importance relative to the screen's objective; on the Mac buy page the heading frames the page, the size selector is the goal of the step, then devices/specs, then the CTA, then "what's in the box"; Apple positions a "chat with a specialist" prompt directly under the tabs, signaling high priority for pre-purchase questions; the most effective layout is the one aligned to the screen's objective.
- Learner-relevant: Provides the objective-first rule that governs all later layout prioritization.

### 5.6 IA priority challenge

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/5.6 IA priority challenge Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#ia-priority-challenge]]`
- Summary: Three activities on a "Lake Krakenback" search-results wireframe (header, title, filter icon, results). Activity 1 ranks components 1–4 by visual hierarchy (header 1, results 2, title 3, filter 4). Activity 2 re-prioritizes for the objective "encourage users to refine results" — pull filters into a left sidebar, moving filter from 4 to ~2 and results to 3. Activity 3 re-prioritizes for "show as many accommodations as possible" — go full-width image grid, demote filters.
- Key claims: There is no single correct ranking — responses differ; different objectives demand different priority orders; to raise an element's priority, move it into primary F-pattern real estate (e.g., filters to a left sidebar); re-ranking based on a new objective changes user interaction and conversion; all three designs are valid — each is geared to a different objective/hypothesis (people know what they want vs need discovery).
- Learner-relevant: Practises re-prioritizing a real layout as stakeholder objectives and hypotheses change.

### 5.7 Create your wireframes

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/5.7 Create your wireframes Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#create-your-wireframes]]`
- Summary: The module's main activity: using the client job stories, sitemap, user flow, and competitor research, create a full set of Whimsical wireframes (desktop first, then tablet and mobile). The instructor demonstrates Whimsical (wireframe mode, E for elements, F for frames, R rectangle, T text) then walks through his own wireframes for the Accom.ai platform, page by page, explaining each layout decision.
- Key claims: Use Whimsical for fast simple wireframes (E elements, F frames, R rectangle, T text); apply the Fogg model in the header by asking destination/dates/guests/occasion instead of a bare CTA; use a logged-out "turn on personalization" toggle as a growth hack that prompts login; homepage surfaces popular accommodations/occasions and a host-acquisition CTA; results page puts filters in a left sidebar (primary real estate), adds neighbouring-suburb results to avoid dead ends, and shows a map; listing detail keeps info left / CTA right (sticky), categorizes amenities, and surfaces cleanliness/bathroom/kitchen photos per research; checkout consolidates sign-in and payment on one page (easier to split 1→3 than 3→1); land on My Bookings (not details) after booking for context; host landing page addresses motivation (passive income), process steps, USPs, host protection, and FAQs; tablet stacks two columns and keeps the CTA visible at top, mobile uses one column, sticky bottom CTAs within thumb reach, and accordions; wireframes never translate perfectly into final design.
- Learner-relevant: The end-to-end worked example and the artifact the rest of the course's design phase builds on.

### 5.8 What is the - Fogg behaviour model

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/5.8 What is the - Fogg behaviour model Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#what-is-the-fogg-behaviour-model]]`
- Summary: Opens with a Tradezilla push-notification example contrasting a junior literal-requirement design with a senior's strategic thinking (how to entice, remove friction, and time the delivery). Introduces Dr BJ Fogg's model: Motivation, Ability, and Trigger must converge at the same moment for behaviour. Illustrated with a red-light example and a personal Strava cycling story.
- Key claims: Behaviour happens only when Motivation (desire: pleasure/pain, hope/fear, social acceptance/duty), Ability (time, money, physical/mental effort, technical know-how), and Trigger (internal/external cues) converge; product success is whether users act, not the Figma file; UX designers must raise motivation (benefits, social proof, emotional appeals), reduce friction (remove obstacles, simplify, guide), and make triggers timely, relevant and personalized; the model scales from tiny UI details to major life behaviour changes.
- Learner-relevant: Introduces the behavioural framework used to optimize wireframes for action in 5.9–5.10.

### 5.9 Fogg behaviour model - Challenge

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/5.9 Fogg behaviour model - Challenge Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#fogg-behaviour-model-challenge]]`
- Summary: One activity identifying the key moments in the Accom.ai experience where a visitor behaviour is encouraged — make a search, click a listing, engage with filters, make a booking, process payment — then defining Motivation, Ability, and Trigger for each, shown by hiding activities until the previous one is answered. A final activity targets retention (getting users to return).
- Key claims: The five key encouraged actions are search, click listing, use filters, book, and pay/complete booking; for making a search, motivation = book a unique accommodation meeting trip needs, ability = easy/trustworthy, trigger = relevant questions or "new listings added"; replace bare CTAs with search fields and relevant questions ("where, dates, guests, occasion") to motivate; trigger examples include "100 unique accommodations added today" and popular-accommodation showcases; for clicking a listing, trigger can be view counts, discounts, or a "high demand" fire badge; for retention, motivation = a fragmented planning process needing one place, ability = easy single-place storage, trigger = prompt to upload/build itineraries after booking; realistic metrics (per-listing views) should look good even for a new platform.
- Learner-relevant: Turns the Fogg model into concrete, deployable wireframe tactics for activation and retention.

### 5.10 Optimize your wireframes

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/5.10 Optimize your wireframes Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#optimize-your-wireframes]]`
- Summary: The module's closing activity: review all your wireframes and make small Fogg-driven optimizations that encourage user action. The instructor gives examples (e.g., "100 unique accommodations added today", a "trip intent" dropdown) and stresses small, quick additions over major new features, with real users being the true test.
- Key claims: Reflect on what motivation, trigger, and ability can be enhanced in the experience; add small touches like a daily-unique-accommodations line or a trip-intent dropdown; focus on encouraging the key actions (search, select listing, use filters, book, pay); there are no bad ideas — only more or less impactful ones, and only user testing reveals true impact; implement minor enhancements, not major feature additions.
- Learner-relevant: Closes the ideation module by applying the Fogg model to refine existing wireframes.
