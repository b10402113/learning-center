### 3.1 What is a sitemap

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/3.1 What is a sitemap Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#what-is-a-sitemap]]`
- Summary: On receiving a brief, a designer first answers three questions — what problem, why solve it, and how to solve it — and forms a high-level strategy before touching wireframes or UI. A sitemap is the tool that maps key pages and their connections, giving stakeholders a shared bird's-eye view. The lesson also covers an advanced SEO angle: URL structuring for humans and search engines, plus HTML sitemaps.
- Key claims: Understand problem/why/how and plan strategy before details, to avoid major rework; sitemaps visually map pages and their flow for stakeholders, aiding planning and alignment; build a sitemap at the start of the design process, and even on tight deadlines at least scribble one; SEO specialists use sitemaps to shape URL structure (descriptive slugs, not `/blog/1`, `/blog/2`); HTML sitemaps list all pages with links and help search engines crawl/index them, as seen in Zillow, Airbnb, and Booking.com footers.
- Learner-relevant: Establishes why sitemapping precedes wireframing and how the same artifact serves UX planning and SEO.

### 3.2 How to create a sitemap

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/3.2 How to create a sitemap Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#how-to-create-a-sitemap]]`
- Summary: A worked walkthrough in FigJam building a two-level sitemap from Designership's header navigation. The instructor demonstrates the courses tab, then pauses for the learner to build the rest from the live site, and reviews the full result (courses, products, blog, success stories, and external links). Covers node conventions for pages vs components and mandatory vs optional paths.
- Key claims: Start from top-level categories and expand into sub-pages; derive the sitemap from actual link/URL structure rather than nav labels; label nodes with URL paths (e.g., `/courses`, `/courses/figma`) for accuracy; connect truly optional pages with a side arrow rather than a vertical funnel arrow; use a borderless rectangle for links that leave the site (community, enter academy); modals and filters/features are not shown as pages; success-story detail pages use the article title as the slug.
- Learner-relevant: Gives the concrete FigJam mechanics and visual conventions for building and communicating a sitemap.

### 3.3 Create your sitemap

- Locator: `[[sources/ui-ux-design/20261007/ui2/3.3 Create your sitemap Lesson Summary (Li).en.srt#create-your-sitemap]]`
- Summary: The module's main activity: build a sitemap for the Accom.ai accommodation-booking platform from the client's job stories. The learner first bolds the job stories that justify their own page, then builds two separate sitemaps — logged-out and logged-in — because the header and accessible features differ by auth state. The instructor walks through every job story to align on page-vs-component decisions and displays his own sitemaps.
- Key claims: Not every job story maps to a page — some are components/features (search field, personalization toggle, filters, sort, payment fields), so don't create pages for them; pages worth their own node include support/help, login, signup, become-a-host, homepage, search results, listings, checkout/booking details, my bookings, booking details, cancellation, contact-host, profile settings, change password, email preferences, and forgot password; keep the checkout funnel to one page (fold sign-in/create-account and payment in) to limit drop-off; confirmation is better as a modal than a page; create two sitemaps (logged-out vs logged-in) since features and header priorities differ; there is no single correct sitemap — interpretation and general direction matter.
- Learner-relevant: Applies the job-story → sitemap approach on the real course project, producing the artifact later wireframing builds on.
