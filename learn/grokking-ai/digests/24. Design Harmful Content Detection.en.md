# L1 overview

- Design a system that detects harmful content (content breaking the platform's rules, e.g. harassment or scams). Each new post is one item needing a decision, so this is a **judging** question like fraud, and two fraud lessons still apply: an **adversary** who studies the system and changes behavior, and **asymmetric costs** of the two mistakes.
- Three problems are new. First, the input is **multimodal** — a meme mixes a photo, words drawn on it, and a caption, and they must be judged together. Second, the costs are not money: a missed harm (**false negative**) leaves a harmful post up; a wrongful removal (**false positive**) silences someone innocent, and "no spreadsheet can balance those two." Third, **benign volume is huge** — well under 1% of posts break a rule. Context also matters: a slur from an outsider can be harassment, from a member of that group it can be reclamation, taking the word back.
- The interviewer wants a system of checks, not one classifier: at 200 million new posts and comments a day, a model smart enough to judge context costs too much, and a model cheap enough for every post is too simple. So split the work into a **risk funnel** — cheap checks look at everything, expensive checks see only items that still look risky.
- The main metric is **prevalence**, the share of content **views** that land on harmful content — by uploads a harmful post seen by 2 million people and one seen by 10 are each one miss, but by views the viral miss is 200,000 times larger. Measure it by sampling views: 100,000 random views per harm category (e.g. harassment) per week, labeled by reviewers; if 50 land on harassment, prevalence is 0.05%. The sample does not use the model, so it catches harm the model missed. Add a guardrail: the **false removal rate**, the share of removals that were wrong, since over-removing would also cut prevalence.
- Labels must come from people because no product outcome gives them. Two feeds: **reviewer decisions** from the review queue (the list of posts waiting for a human) and **appeals** (a user asks for another look; an overturned removal becomes a labeled false positive). **User reports are noisy** — evidence, not labels — so a report on the meme only raises its review priority. **Active learning** sends the items the model is least sure about to reviewers first; a score near 0.5 sits where one label teaches the most.
- Adversaries try to **evade** detection: **leet speak** swaps letters for numbers (a 4 for an A) — defend by **normalizing** text back to standard spelling; **homoglyphs** are lookalike letters from another alphabet — map them back; some put harmful text in a screenshot — defend with **OCR** (Optical Character Recognition, reading text out of images). For the meme, features include the OCR'd words, its caption, and its 900,000 followers.
- Baseline: **hash matching** (fingerprints of known harmful media) plus keyword rules — it misses most new harm, and the new meme image matches no hash. The full risk funnel has four stages: stage 1 **hashes and rules** (a **perceptual hash** is a short fingerprint that still matches after small edits like cropping); stage 2 the **fast classifier** — small models score every remaining item and their only job is to decide what advances, so no score here removes a post; about 1% pass (that share is the **pass rate**, and those items are the **flagged slice**); stage 3 **heavy multimodal models** that read photo and caption together, one of which can be an **LLM classifier** that reads the post plus the **policy** (the written rules) and judges — it handles sarcasm better than a small model but costs far more per item; stage 4 **human review**, reached by about 0.1% of items. "A recommendation funnel keeps items a user would like; this one filters for risk."
- The **action ladder** turns scores into actions: **auto allow** (keep up as usual), **reduce / age gate** (keep up but stop recommending it to non-followers, or show only to adults), **auto remove** (take down without a person). For each harm category two thresholds split the heavy-model score: above the high one auto remove, between them human review, below the low one allow. A separate **borderline score** can reduce lawful-but-awful posts (crude jokes that break no rule). Trading wrongful removals against harmful views is a values choice, so **trust and safety** (the team that writes the policy) sets thresholds with **legal**; a **policy engine** stores each threshold as configuration so it changes without a new model.
- Trace: at 7:15pm the meme arrives, no hash matches, OCR reads the on-image words, and the fast classifier flags it — so the post goes live but **reduced**. About three seconds later the heavy model scores harassment at 0.6, between the thresholds, so it goes to human review. The queue orders by **model uncertainty × reach** (how many people are likely to see it soon), so the uncertain, large-audience meme moves up and a reviewer removes it at 7:40pm; the next day a second reviewer upholds it on appeal, and both decisions become labels.
- Some harmful posts pass the fast classifier and dangerous ones spread fast, so **reach-aware routing** rescores a post with the heavy models each time it crosses a **view tier** (a fixed view count such as 100,000 views) and moves uncertain ones to the front of the queue. **Appeals** are needed from day one — a person deserves another look, and without appeals the system rarely learns where it removes too much.
- When a model is slow or down, **fail open** lets the post go live without that check while **fail closed** holds it — choose per harm category in advance (prefer fail closed where spread is dangerous). Adversaries keep changing tricks so classifiers retrain often (weekly) on reviewer decisions and appeals; the hash database updates within minutes. A new model passes three checks: **back test** on labeled posts it never trained on, **shadow test** on live posts whose scores are never used, then a **canary** on a small share. A sudden drop in flags may mean a new trick; a brand-new kind of harm is handled by rules and human review until enough labels exist.
- Cost: if the heavy model costs 1,000× more per item, the fast classifier on all 200 million items is 1 unit; the heavy model on 1% (2 million items) is 10 units — so the funnel costs ~11 units against 1,000 for heavy on everything. The fast stage's pass rate controls the bill: each extra tenth of a percent adds 200,000 heavy calls a day. At 0.1% that is 200,000 review decisions a day, about 800 reviewer shifts daily at ~250 per shift — so set the review threshold with the reviewer team.

# L2 detail

- `#L1-L139` — Harmful content detection as a risk funnel: multimodality and context (harassment vs reclamation), view-weighted prevalence sampled independently of the model with a false-removal guardrail, reviewer/appeal labels vs noisy reports plus active learning, evasion defenses (leetspeak normalization, homoglyphs, OCR), the four stages from perceptual hashes to LLM classifiers to 0.1% human review, the allow/reduce/age-gate/remove action ladder with category thresholds owned by trust and safety and legal, the 7:15pm meme trace with uncertainty × reach queueing, reach-aware view-tier rescoring, fails open vs closed, retraining and backtest/shadow/canary gates, and the 11-vs-1,000 unit cost model with reviewer capacity.
