# L1 overview

- An interview gives one question and about 45 minutes, and the interviewer can grade only what was said; a common failure is spending 25 minutes on requirements and running out of time before risks and costs, so three skills matter: recognition (name a known design quickly), pacing (reach every step on time), and depth on request (go deeper only where asked).
- Recognition is a move in three parts made in the first two or three minutes: name the family by what the system produces (a score → predictive, a funnel narrowing many items; content → generative, a fixed pipeline; actions → agentic, a loop until the task ends), name the closest known system from the course, and name the one or two requirements that make this question different — for fake reviews, the family is predictive and the match is fraud detection, with the item changed from a payment to a review (text, star rating, and an account behind it, with fakes arriving in coordinated rings).
- Recognition is not copying (drawing an old design unchanged answers a different question) and not memorizing (a memorized answer breaks when one number changes); say the match out loud so the interviewer can correct it, since a wrong match found at minute 2 costs little and at minute 25 costs most of the interview.
- The clock splits 45 minutes across seven steps: 0–3 frame the problem, 3–8 define success, 5 minutes to map the data, 5 to pick the model, 12 for the serving path (minutes 18–30, the largest share and where most follow-ups land), 8 to keep it working, and 5 to count the costs, ending at minute 43 with 2 minutes of slack; the times are a plan, not a rule — borrow minutes back from a later step, give predictive questions more time on data and labels, and skip to the architecture if asked.
- Each step ends with an exit criterion, a short sentence said out loud that closes the step and states its decisions (e.g. "this is fraud detection for reviews"); stated targets in one sentence, such as a 300 ms limit and ~10,000 requests per second.
- The fake-review run: late labels and an adversary, a primary metric of the share of review views landing on fake reviews with a guardrail of real reviews removed by mistake, 500,000 reviews a day published within one minute, inputs of text, account age and history, and whether the account really ordered, with shared devices and payment cards linking a ring and labels arriving days to weeks late from the trust team; a paid campaign can post 50 reviews in an hour, so recent-review counts must be fresh within minutes.
- Model choice starts with a baseline rule set (e.g. hold any five-star review from an account created in the last hour) and steps up to a gradient-boosted tree model; the serving path queues new reviews, scores each within seconds, and routes by score — low publishes, middle publishes but keeps the review out of the restaurant's average until a later check, high holds it for a human — while a nightly graph job follows account links to catch a whole ring that per-review scoring would miss; if scoring fails, reviews wait, and after one minute they publish but stay out of the average until scored, so every arrow has a time limit, a cost, and a failure behavior.
- Step 6 keeps it working: reviewer decisions become labels, each new model must keep recall without removing more real reviews, watch the daily share of held reviews because a sudden drop can mean the adversary found a gap, and each weekly version runs first in shadow mode, then takes a growing traffic share, with weekly retraining.
- Step 7 counts costs in two ledgers: the failure ledger (top failures each with a response — mistaken removals get an appeal path, and a ring may learn the hold threshold, which the graph job and often-changed rules answer) and the cost ledger (cost per request × requests); the hold threshold sets team size — 0.5% of reviews held is 2,500 a day, about 42 reviewer hours or about 5 reviewers on 8-hour shifts, so people, not the model, were the largest cost and a candidate who stops at the serving path never finds that out.
- The follow-up "what if rings write reviews with an LLM" shows the text checks weaken but account, order, and device signals are harder to fake — the design never relied on text alone — and recognition took one sentence and gave the answer its whole shape.
- How to use the module (seven case studies): run each before reading it — read only the question, set a 45-minute timer, speak out loud — then read the walkthrough and note what you missed; each has mid-level, senior, and staff answers (find the one your run matched), re-run variations a few days later, and the module ends with a critique lesson ranking a design's problems by severity; the text-to-image case puts the pressure on GPU cost.
