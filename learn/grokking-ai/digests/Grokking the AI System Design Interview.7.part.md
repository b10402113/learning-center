# L1 overview

- Lesson 21 — Design Search Ranking — Adds a typed query to the funnel: query understanding fixes spelling, expands synonyms, and turns attributes into hard filters; lexical (BM25) and semantic (two-tower ANN) retrieval run together; a two-level L1/L2 ranker and a business layer finish the page, trained on in-session preference pairs. 100M products, 10K QPS, p95 under 300 ms.
- Lesson 22 — Design Ad Click Prediction — Adds a price: pCTR is multiplied by bid in a second-price auction, so probabilities must be calibrated, not just correctly ordered. Covers eligibility as a hard contract filter, delayed conversion labels, distillation, a Platt/isotonic calibration layer, near-online learning, and budget pacing. 5M ads, ~100K requests/s, 50–100 ms.
- Lesson 23 — Design Fraud Detection — Judging shape with an adversary who causes drift on purpose. At 0.2% fraud accuracy is useless, so the goal is lowest total cost of errors; two calibrated thresholds yield approve, human review, and step-up/block. Covers censored labels and a capped exploration slice, velocity and graph features, why rules stay, and per-segment score monitoring.

# L2 detail

- `#L1-L25` — Lesson 21 vocabulary: query, query understanding, inverted index, lexical retrieval (BM25), semantic retrieval, ranker/learning to rank, NDCG, preference pair, reformulation, hard filter, business layer.
- `#L26-L56` — Part 1: frame — search is the recommendation funnel plus a query, so the user's typed words replace guessing from history; running query "red runing shoes sz 10"; 100M products, 10K QPS, p95 under 300 ms; scope excludes autocomplete, image search, and the ad auction.
- `#L57-L89` — Part 2: define success — Recall@500 grades retrieval, NDCG@10 grades ranking (graded results, position discount, normalized, from a judged set); online the primary metric is search conversion and guardrails are zero-results, reformulation, and p95 latency.
- `#L90-L168` — Part 3: map the data — catalog, search logs, and judged sets; query frequency is Zipfian (head queries worth caching, long tail where search fails); clicks are position-biased, so learn from in-session preference pairs and check them with traffic swaps and propensity weighting.
- `#L169-L303` — Part 4: pick the model — baseline is BM25 plus popularity; query understanding does normalization/spelling, synonym expansion, and intent/attribute extraction (confident attributes become hard filters); lexical and semantic retrieval cover each other's failures; gradient-boosted ranker with query/product/pairwise features; L1 trims 500 to ~100 and L2 orders them; LLMs are offline graders only, not the live path.
- `#L304-L429` — Part 5: serving path — cache check ~2 ms, query understanding ~15 ms, parallel lexical/semantic retrieval ~50 ms across shards (scatter-gather with timeouts and replicas), L1 ~20 ms, L2 ~60 ms, business layer ~10 ms, fresh price/stock at render time ~20 ms, all under 180 ms; personalization reweights only within relevance; sponsored slots need a relevance floor.
- `#L430-L440` — When nothing matches: distinguish no inventory from vocabulary mismatch, then relax the least confident filter, try semantic results, and show marked close alternatives; log every zero-results query.
- `#L441-L492` — Part 6: keep it working — the inverted index updates within seconds while the ANN index cycles every few hours or nightly, which is another reason to run both; the retraining loop (daily/weekly rankers, synonym and spelling updates, nightly embeddings) with three gates (offline NDCG, shadow, A/B test); a monitoring table of signals.
- `#L493-L530` — Part 7: costs — three levers (cache hit rate, L1/L2 split, no per-item LLM call); an LLM on 100 candidates would be ~1M calls/s and ~$40M/day; 51.2 GB of raw vectors for 100M products; what breaks (ANN down → lexical keeps serving, and a stale cache is the dangerous silent failure, fixed by caching only the ranking).
- `#L531-L544` — In the interview: the 30-second answer plus a follow-up on how app-store search changes (exact title/brand dominates, shorter funnel).
- `#L545-L566` — Common mistakes (jumping straight to embeddings, training on raw clicks, caching the whole page) plus key takeaways and the handoff to ad click prediction, where the predicted probability must be accurate, not just well ordered.
- `#L567-L591` — Lesson 22 vocabulary: advertiser, campaign, CTR, pCTR, conversion, pCVR, bid, auction, eligibility, budget pacing, near-online learning.
- `#L592-L706` — Part 1: frame — every prediction becomes money, so numbers must be calibrated and targeting/budget are contracts; the auction ranks by bid × pCTR (Ad A at 4 cents beats Ad B's higher bid); second-price pricing means the winner pays just enough to beat the runner-up (A pays ~$1.51); scope is feed ads, 5M active ads, ~100K requests/s, 50–100 ms.
- `#L707-L767` — Part 2: define success — three parties (platform revenue, advertiser cost per conversion, user hide rate and time in app) plus latency; offline AUC checks order while calibration checks honesty (halving every pCTR keeps AUC but doubles the error), log loss punishes both; miscalibration works through Ad A's overcharge and Ad C's wrong win; calibration hides per segment, so draw a calibration curve per segment.
- `#L768-L836` — Part 3: map the data — impression logs, click events, conversion events, campaign data, plus bot filtering; the delayed feedback problem (seconds for clicks, days for conversions) leaves clicks provisionally negative, treated by wait-windows, importance weighting of late positives, or two-stage modeling; feature freshness table.
- `#L837-L927` — Part 4: pick the model — baseline is historical click rate blended with category average for new ads; two heads for pCTR and pCVR (only 1 in 1,000 impressions converts, 1 in 20 clicks converts); the scorer must be distilled to fit the sub-100 ms auction; a calibration layer (Platt scaling or isotonic regression) remaps raw scores, fitted per segment.
- `#L928-L1052` — Part 5: serving path — eligibility ~10 ms (targeting, budget, policy) then retrieval to ~500 ads, features ~15 ms, distilled scorer ~15 ms, calibration ~1 ms, auction ~2 ms, about 55 ms total; eligibility runs first as a hard filter; budget pacing spreads spend; two arrows leave serving (the event stream for freshness and the pacing loop for budgets).
- `#L1053-L1115` — Part 6: keep it working — near-online learning is standard because campaigns start every minute and creatives decay; guard fast updates with log loss and calibration-ratio checks, rollback, and budget-split A/B tests; monitor calibration per segment, stream lag, pacing, latency, hide rate, fallbacks; price a zero-history campaign with priors plus capped exploration.
- `#L1116-L1159` — Part 7: costs — at 100K requests/s × 500 ads the scorer runs 50M predictions/s; distillation, eligibility-first, and fetching user features once keep it affordable; the biggest cost is a wrong probability; failure ledger with warm fallbacks and a fail-closed eligibility check.
- `#L1160-L1178` — In the interview: the 30-second answer plus follow-ups on an overcharging advertiser, a brand-new campaign, and why not the biggest model.
- `#L1179-L1187` — Same design, other questions: sponsored search (query becomes the dominant signal, relevance floor) and sponsored restaurants (pCVR central, orders within an hour, restaurant must be open and deliver).
- `#L1188-L1201` — Common mistakes: stopping at AUC, treating eligibility as a ranking feature, and ignoring the three clocks.
- `#L1202-L1212` — Key takeaways and the handoff to fraud detection, which moves from ranking to judging.
- `#L1213-L1238` — Lesson 23 vocabulary: payment/transaction, fraud, chargeback, false decline, step-up authentication, rules engine, velocity feature, graph feature, censored label, exploration slice.
- `#L1239-L1282` — Part 1: frame — a payment processor at 10,000 TPS peak, fraud under 1% (using 0.2%), ~50 ms budget with ~10 ms for the model; the adversary studies the model and causes drift on purpose; the two errors (chargeback vs false decline) have asymmetric costs.
- `#L1283-L1303` — Part 2: define success — at 0.2% base rate, approving everything is 99.8% accurate, so accuracy is meaningless; instead use precision, recall, and PR-AUC (which suits rare fraud better than ROC-AUC).
- `#L1304-L1349` — Part 2 continued: three actions, not two — below 0.05 approve, 0.05–0.20 route to human review, above 0.20 step-up or block; step-up is the middle option because a thief with only a card number usually fails it, while a wrong step-up costs a real customer only a delay.
- `#L1350-L1418` — Setting thresholds by expected cost — worked table (approving costs chance of fraud × $80, declining costs chance of good × $5, review costs ~$4); thresholds at 0.05 and 0.20, revisited monthly and after each model; offline PR-AUC plus precision/recall, online total cost of errors (fraud losses, false-decline rate, review hours).
- `#L1419-L1523` — Part 3: map the data — labels from chargebacks (2–8 weeks), analyst decisions (hours to days), customer reports (days), and step-up results (minutes, only a hint); blocked payments have censored labels, fixed by a capped exploration slice (~1 in 1,000, under $50, with a daily loss budget, never overriding hard rules) and probability weighting.
- `#L1524-L1570` — Part 3 continued: four feature groups — payment (in the request), history (up to a day, nightly), velocity (within seconds, streaming), and graph (minutes to hours); velocity catches card-testing bursts, graph features catch fraud rings that each account hides alone.
- `#L1571-L1614` — Part 4: pick the model — rules engine plus logistic regression baseline, upgrade to a gradient-boosted tree on tabular data; three training needs (downsample or weight then recalibrate, split by time, weight recent data); rules stay for hard constraints, instant response to novel attacks, and explainability; rule sprawl is cured by hit-rate and precision metrics plus owners and expiry dates.
- `#L1615-L1718` — Part 5: serving path — rules ~2 ms, features ~20 ms, model ~10 ms, decision ~5 ms, inside the 50 ms budget; a $240 headphones payment scores 0.03 and is approved, with the chargeback arriving five weeks later; fail open under an amount cap and fail closed above it, with every fallback tagged.
- `#L1719-L1775` — Part 6: keep it working — watch score distributions and rule hit rates per segment, because labels lag; a sudden drop in flags is an attack hypothesis first; a jump in false declines points at the feature pipeline before the model; evaluate a new model by backtest on labeled history, shadow scoring, then canary; when fraud grows but the team does not, hold the queue size and move both thresholds in.
- `#L1776-L1835` — Part 7: costs — an example million payments splits into 2,000 step-up/block, 1,000 review, 997,000 approve, giving 80% recall and 75% precision in the top band and ~$160,000 of total error cost dominated by missed fraud; ~20 cores at 500 payments/s (plan 40), ~200,000 feature reads and 40,000 counter updates/s, and logs kept at least 8 weeks.
- `#L1836-L1854` — Part 8: the 30-second answer plus follow-ups on six-week-old labels, 10x fraud growth with a fixed team, and adapting the design to account takeover (the unit becomes the session with behavioral features).
- `#L1855-L1874` — Part 9: five mistakes — one classifier with one threshold, quoting accuracy, training only on observed outcomes, choosing between rules and the model, and celebrating a drop in flags.
- `#L1875-L1886` — Key takeaways: fraud adds an adversary, success is lowest total cost of errors, two thresholds give three actions, labels are slow and censored, rules stay, and per-segment scores warn before labels do.
