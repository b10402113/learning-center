# L1 overview

- Capstone practice question: a food delivery company does about 500,000 orders a day and shows an ETA (Estimated Time of Arrival) at checkout, "like 30 to 40 min," that must stay accurate as the company grows into new cities; the design must learn from real deliveries from day one. Answer in seven steps, from framing to counting costs, and state scope (the estimate at checkout open, in every city; live updates after ordering are out).
- Turn "accurate" into numbers: **MAE** (mean absolute error, the average miss in minutes — predicting 32 for a 38-minute delivery misses by 6), **P90 error** (the miss 90% of predictions stay under; if 20 minutes, one order in 10 misses by more than 20 and the average hides it), and the **on-time rate** (percent delivered inside the shown window). "MAE measures the model and the on-time rate measures the promise." The estimate must appear within 100 ms for 99% of requests.
- Features split into three freshness buckets, each with its own job: **entity features** (slow, e.g. restaurant average prep time, from a nightly batch job over the data warehouse), **streaming aggregates** (seconds-scale, e.g. orders in the kitchen now, from Flink over a Kafka event stream), and **request-time context** (only at checkout, e.g. route distance). Both jobs feed both feature stores — an online store (latest value per restaurant and zone, read at serving) and an offline store (every value with timestamp, for training) — so training and serving share feature definitions.
- Logging is the direct fix for **training-serving skew**: every prediction logs the exact feature values used plus a prediction ID (so a retried write joins one row), and a delivery job joins that row with the label about an hour later.
- Start from a **baseline** (restaurant average prep time + route distance / city average courier speed) — a number to beat, and the first-day answer for a new city: if the baseline's MAE is 7 minutes, a model at 6.8 is probably not worth its extra jobs. Upgrade to **GBDT** (gradient boosted decision trees, each tree correcting the previous ones; can learn that a kitchen slows after 15 open orders but only when it rains; scores a row in under a millisecond on a few ordinary CPU servers). A **calibration layer** after the model turns predicted minutes into the promise window, and the buffer is a **product knob** the product team owns, changeable in minutes with no retraining.
- Serving: **online inference** is required because it needs the live kitchen queue and live route; the ETA service makes a single-batched online-store read and a routing call in parallel, and with every stage at full budget the request still finishes in about 36 ms, leaving room under 100 ms. Every service gets a **timeout** and a **fallback**: routing slow → cached zone-to-zone estimates after ~15 ms; stale features → trained-in defaults; model down → a heuristic table holding the baseline. Each fallback is less exact and shows a wider window, but checkout is never empty.
- Monitoring repeats the four layers (pipeline freshness in seconds; input drift; prediction drift in minutes; true MAE after about an hour), reported **by segment** because one global MAE hides city-level trouble. Retrain daily, plus an extra run when last hour's MAE crosses a limit, and gate every candidate: offline eval per segment, shadow, canary. The trap example: a new model cuts global MAE from 5.0 to 4.7 but raises Manhattan dinner-rush MAE from 6 to 9 — offline evaluation blocks it.
- Cost: customers reopen checkout, so about 1.5 million estimates a day; a few CPU servers handle ~70 requests per second at peak, so the GBDT costs little. The streaming pipeline (runs all day) and routing calls dominate — at an assumed $0.001 per call, routing is $1,500 a day, far more than the model servers. "Running the model is the cheap part."
