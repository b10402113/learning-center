# L1 overview

- Design fraud detection for a payment processor that approves card payments for many online stores. Fraud is a payment by someone who is not the real cardholder; each payment needs one decision, so this is a **judging** question. Start with numbers: about 10,000 transactions per second at peak, a fraud rate of **0.2%** (two in 1,000), and a ~**50 ms** scoring budget.
- What makes fraud different is the **adversary** — someone who studies the model to defeat it: fraudsters test small payments, and when one method is blocked they switch to another. That change in the data lowers accuracy (**drift**) and the adversary causes it on purpose, so last month's pattern may fail this month.
- Two errors with **asymmetric** costs: **missed fraud** (approved now, disputed weeks later, the bank reverses it — a **chargeback**) costs about **$80**; a **false decline** (stopping a good customer) costs about **$5**. The whole design trades one cost against the other. The **base rate** of 0.2% means approving everything is 99.8% accurate and catches nothing — that is **class imbalance**, so quoting accuracy reveals a missed point.
- Scoring language: the model gives each payment a score; the system **flags** it when the score passes a **threshold**. **Precision** = of the flagged payments, how many were fraud; **recall** = of the fraud that happened, how much was caught; a higher threshold usually raises precision and lowers recall. **PRAUC** (Precision Recall Area Under the Curve) sums both up over every threshold.
- Two thresholds and three actions beat one hard choice per unsure payment: below the low threshold **approve**; between the thresholds route to **human review** (an analyst decides); above the high threshold ask for **step-up authentication** (an extra check a real cardholder passes, like a code to their phone) — a thief with only a stolen card number usually fails it — and block when risk is very high. A wrong step-up costs a good customer a short delay, not the sale.
- The model returns only a score; a separate **decision policy** picks the action using thresholds set by **expected cost minimization**: approve costs P(fraud) × $80, decline costs P(good) × $5, review costs about $4 of analyst time. At a 5% chance, approving costs $4 = a review; at 20%, declining costs $4 (80% × $5), so the thresholds sit at **0.05 and 0.20**. This requires **calibrated** scores, where 0.10 really means 10%. The risk team revisits thresholds monthly and after every new model.
- Labels: **chargebacks** are the main one but arrive **2–8 weeks** late; faster signals like analyst decisions are **provisional labels**. A blocked payment never completes, so its label is **censored** — if the model wrongly blocks travelers shopping abroad, the next model never sees that mistake and repeats it. The cure is an **exploration slice**: about 1 in 1,000 payments the system would block, let through on purpose, capped at a small amount such as $50.
- Features: **payment** features (amount) in the request, **history** features (card age) from a nightly job, **velocity** features (activity count and speed on one card or device, e.g. payments in the last 10 minutes — a thief testing a card makes many small payments) kept fresh by a streaming job, and **graph** features (two cards sharing one device) that catch organized **fraud rings** whose accounts each look normal alone.
- Modeling: a **rules engine** (handwritten rules) handles clear cases, **logistic regression** (a weighted sum of features turned into a probability) scores the rest, then upgrade to **gradient-boosted trees** (each tree correcting the previous ones). Train on older weeks and test on newer weeks because serving always predicts an unseen period. Keep the rules engine: some payments must be blocked whatever the score (a card on the stolen card list) and a rule can stop a new attack within 15 minutes while retraining takes days or weeks — but stale rules pile up into **rule sprawl** and false declines, so give every rule an owner and an expiry date (e.g. 90 days).
- Serving the headphones payment at 9:14pm: the rules engine runs first (hard blocks are nearly free), no rule fires at ~2 ms, **feature assembly** reads the **online feature store** (a fast store of current values), and at ~30 ms the model returns 0.03 — below 0.05, so the policy approves inside the 50 ms budget. The log keeps score and features. If scoring fails, approve payments under a cap ($50) and send larger ones to step-up. Five weeks later the chargeback arrives and the training job joins that label to the logged features — "the system learned of that miss five weeks late."
- Early warning needs no labels: watch the **share of payments in each score band** (a range of scores) and **each rule's hit rate**, both split by **segment** (a group like one country) because an attack often hits one segment and the average hides it. If flagged payments drop by half in one day, treat it as a possible attack, not a win; if the false decline rate doubles overnight, check the **feature pipelines** (the jobs computing features) before the model, since a stalled streaming job can freeze velocity counts.
- Because labels lag about six weeks, test a new model in three stages: a **backtest** on payments from 8–16 weeks ago whose labels have arrived (using the exploration slice for payments the current model blocked), then **shadow scoring** on live payments, then a **canary** on ~1% of live traffic. If fraud grows tenfold but the analyst team does not, hold the review queue size fixed, raise the low threshold, and lower the high threshold.
- Cost is dominated by errors, not machines: of 1 million payments with 2,000 fraud, stopping 1,600 gives 80% recall; the 400 missed cost $32,000, false declines at most $2,500, reviews $4,000 — about **$38,500 per million payments**, versus **$160,000** for approving everything. Missed fraud stays the largest cost, so recall gains are worth the most. At ~500 payments per second per CPU core, 20 cores serve the peak, but the feature store often costs more (~200,000 reads per second at peak).
