# L1 overview

- Lesson 29 — Back of the Envelope Math for AI Systems — Five anchors (4 chars/token, ~$3/$12 per million tokens, small models ~10× cheaper, ~1 cent per chat turn, one GPU at ~$4/hour and ~2,500 output tokens/s) drive four calculations: a token bill (~$1.9M/year and routing to a small model), a GPU fleet (16 nodes rounded to 20, ~$525K/year), memory fit (13B fits one 80 GB GPU, 70B needs 2–4; KV cache caps concurrency), and a vector store (~30 GB at 10M chunks, ~3 TB at 1B so shard). Every calculation must end in a decision.
- Lesson 30 — Serving Optimizations — Three shelves tried in order: avoid the call (semantic cache, router/cascade, shorter prompts and outputs), tune the engine (continuous batching, paged attention, prefix caching), then change the model (quantization, distillation, speculative decoding). Each has a measured saving, a risk to quality, and one number to attach: caching near zero, routing ~5× cheaper, batching 3–30 users per GPU.
- Lesson 31 — Prompting, Fine-Tuning, or RAG — The three tools are not a ladder: prompting is the measured baseline, RAG supplies facts at request time, and fine-tuning (usually a LoRA adapter) changes behavior. Diagnose by failure kind — missing facts → RAG, wrong behavior → prompt then fine-tune, right but costly → a fine-tuned small model behind a router. Facts and permissions never live in the weights.

# L2 detail

- `#L1-L16` — Lesson 29 vocabulary: back-of-the-envelope estimation, node, precision, peak, headroom, chunk.
- `#L17-L48` — Part 1: what an estimate is for — the goal is the right order of magnitude and the right decision; it is not an audit, a substitute for a load test, or a price quote; the one habit is to state every assumption out loud so one line, not the whole answer, gets fixed.
- `#L49-L113` — Part 2: five anchors — ~4 characters or 0.75 words per token, ~$3/$12 per million tokens, small model ~10× cheaper, ~1 cent per chat turn, and one inference GPU at ~$4/hour sustaining ~2,500 output tokens/s; the first four price tokens, the fifth prices hours.
- `#L114-L189` — Part 3: calculation 1, the token bill — size a 4,500-word report (~6,500 input tokens with instructions, ~500 output), price it at ~$0.026, multiply by 200,000 documents/day to ~$1.9M/year, then conclude: test a small model, whose 80% routing at a tenth the price blends to ~28% and ~$530K/year.
- `#L190-L279` — Part 4: calculation 2, the GPU fleet — start from output tokens (100 rps × 400 = 40,000/s), divide by one node's 2,500 to get 16 nodes, round to ~20 for ~80% peak, price at ~$3/hour to ~$525K/year; the input-token trap undercounts the fleet because sequential decode sets real load; a node may be several GPUs.
- `#L280-L345` — Part 5: calculation 3, does the model fit — at 16 bits weights use 2 bytes per parameter on an 80 GB GPU: 13B ≈ 26 GB fits with ~54 GB left; 70B ≈ 140 GB needs 2–4 GPUs; 8-bit 70B ≈ 70 GB barely fits; the KV cache (~2 GB per active request for 13B, assumed) caps concurrency at ~27 requests.
- `#L346-L367` — Part 6: calculation 4, sizing the vector store — chunks × dimensions × bytes; 10M chunks × 768 dims × 4 bytes ≈ 30 GB fits one machine, while 1B chunks ≈ 3 TB needs sharding or quantization; add metadata, replicas, and the ANN index, and decide before the rest of retrieval design.
- `#L368-L395` — Part 7: using this in an interview — the 30-second answer; follow-ups on what to change first (model routing, then semantic and prefix caches), why 10 turns is not 10× one turn (55,000 not 10,000 tokens, near-quadratic), and why a good conclusion survives a 2× error in any single assumption.
- `#L396-L431` — Part 8: five mistakes — false precision, sizing for the average, sizing the fleet from input tokens, pricing every chat turn the same, and stopping at the number without a decision; key takeaways recap the anchors and the four decisions.
- `#L432-L451` — Lesson 30 vocabulary: semantic cache, router, cascade, continuous batching, paged attention, prefix caching, speculative decoding.
- `#L452-L537` — Part 1: where the savings come from — the larger support assistant costs ~$12,600/day; the three shelves in order (avoid calls, tune the engine, change the model) with the rule that avoiding a call beats accelerating one; each shelf saves something different, none is risk-free, and none replaces measurement.
- `#L538-L569` — Part 2: semantic caching — embed the question, search past questions, compare with a threshold, store new answers; the threshold is the trade-off, tuned on labeled pairs with wrong matches near zero first; exclude personalized answers, put an exact-match cache in front, and store the policy version.
- `#L570-L620` — Part 2: routing and cascades — a router decides before any model writes an answer (heuristics, a trained classifier, or self-assessment) while a cascade tries the cheap model first and moves failures up after a check; production commonly routes 70–80% to the cheap tier; both need evaluation watching the quality boundary.
- `#L621-L686` — Part 2: router vs cascade diagram and comparison — when the decision happens, how many answers are written, the main risk, and the best-fit case; evaluation with a golden set, sampled answers, and the move-up rate watches both.
- `#L687-L697` — Part 2: shorter prompts and outputs — trim unchanging instructions, summarize history, retrieve fewer better documents, cap output length; at 1M turns/day a 400-token trim saves ~$1,800/day; it applies to every request like a 100% cache.
- `#L698-L711` — Part 3: why decode leaves the GPU idle — each decode step reads all weights and is memory-bound, so at small batch most compute sits idle; batching turns one weight read into many tokens.
- `#L712-L793` — Part 3: continuous batching — naive batching holds every slot until the slowest request ends (a 600-token request wastes three slots for ~550 steps), while continuous batching refills free slots after every decode step; published throughput is roughly 5–10× higher, supported by vLLM and TensorRT-LLM.
- `#L794-L812` — Part 3: paged attention — store the KV cache in small fixed-size pages added on demand, with a block table so pages need not be contiguous; wasted memory drops to part of the last page, so more requests fit and pages can be shared, which makes prefix caching cheap.
- `#L813-L838` — Part 3: prefix caching — keep the KV cache for a shared prompt start (the ~550-token instructions), skipping part of prefill and cutting TTFT; the match must start at the first token, so order shared static content first and per-user content last; hosted APIs sell it as prompt caching at a discount.
- `#L839-L854` — Part 4: quantization — 16-bit to 8- or 4-bit halves or quarters weight memory (70B: 140 → 70 → 35 GB) and speeds memory-bound decode; quality drops little at 8 bits but more at 4, so gate on the golden set; it is the standard first self-hosting move.
- `#L855-L864` — Part 4: distillation — train a small student on the teacher's answers to real product requests, so it matches the teacher only on a narrow high-traffic task; it costs a training pipeline and evaluation set, and the teacher's terms must allow it.
- `#L865-L877` — Part 4: speculative decoding — a small draft model guesses the next few tokens, the large model checks them in one step, the engine keeps matches up to the first miss; commonly ~2× faster decode with no quality drop, at the cost of two models and wasted rejects, with gains shrinking under large batches.
- `#L878-L954` — Part 5: choosing and measuring — a nine-technique table of what each saves and what to measure; one example number per shelf (cache ≈ zero, routing ~5× cheaper after overhead, batching 3–30 users per GPU); a per-request saving is not a whole-bill saving because hard requests still pay full price.
- `#L955-L997` — Part 6: the 30-second answer and follow-ups on semantic-cache risk, caching personalized answers (cache the retrieved context instead), and why streaming helps despite no cost reduction; four mistakes (starting at the bottom shelf, caching personalized answers, routing without a quality check, quoting a speedup as fact).
- `#L998-L1015` — Lesson 31 vocabulary: base model, few-shot examples, grounded, LoRA, evaluation set (golden set); the support assistant at ~40,000 conversations a day.
- `#L1016-L1032` — Part 1: three tools for three problems — prompting changes what you tell the model, RAG changes what it can read, fine-tuning changes the model itself; the base model has never read the company's policy or learned its tone or handoff format.
- `#L1033-L1102` — Part 1 continued and Part 2: the fastest way to fail is treating them as rungs on one ladder, because they solve different problems (RAG = knowledge at request time, fine-tuning = behavior, prompting = a bit of both and always the start); prompting and RAG act online inside each request while fine-tuning acts offline.
- `#L1103-L1129` — Part 3: prompting is always where you start — instructions plus few-shot examples are cheap, reversible, and set the baseline; its ceiling is that instructions cost tokens every call (a 2,000-token prompt over 400,000 calls adds 800M input tokens/day) and cannot teach new facts.
- `#L1130-L1165` — Part 4: RAG supplies knowledge at request time — retrieve the relevant passages and answer from them (grounded); it wins on instant updates, per-user access control, and citations; it costs a retrieval subsystem, longer prompts, and quality capped by retrieval; it reduces but does not end hallucination, so add answer-only-from-context, cite-each-claim, and say-when-unknown instructions and check the citations.
- `#L1166-L1196` — Part 5: fine-tuning changes behavior in the weights — hundreds to thousands of example pairs for tone, format, or narrow skills; it learns patterns not facts, is not one-time, and cannot fix an unclear task; it can compress a behavior-only prompt; LoRA is the practical default since it freezes the base model and trains small swappable adapters.
- `#L1197-L1267` — Part 5 continued: evaluation comes before fine-tuning (evaluation set, scoring rules, and the prompted baseline, also testing skills the model already had); data comes from curated logs, corrected outputs, and filtered synthetic data, with the evaluation set kept separate and never trained on.
- `#L1268-L1279` — Part 5: why fine-tuning is the wrong tool for facts — four reasons (memorized unevenly, cannot be gated per user, updates need a new training run, no source to cite); "fine-tune on our wiki" is the most common wrong answer.
- `#L1280-L1359` — Part 6: diagnose first, then choose — start from a measured prompt baseline and sort each failure: missing facts → RAG, wrong behavior → improve the prompt then fine-tune, right but too costly → a fine-tuned small specialist behind a router; the cost branch is an economic decision, and a made-up policy detail is a knowledge problem, not a behavior one.
- `#L1360-L1384` — Part 6 continued: splitting a mixed plan — fine-tuning can absorb formatting and tone rules but not a glossary of company facts, so the better design fine-tunes for format and retrieves the facts.
- `#L1385-L1404` — Part 7: combining the tools — six measured steps from a prompted hosted model through RAG for policies, prompt fixes for format, a LoRA fine-tune routed for volume (still using RAG for facts), and a safe small-share release; each step answers one measured failure.
- `#L1405-L1417` — Part 8: beyond fine-tuning — continued pretraining on a large domain corpus only after RAG and fine-tuning have been tried and failed, and training from scratch only for a model lab, never for one product feature.
- `#L1418-L1462` — Part 9 and Part 10: the 30-second answer plus follow-ups (still hallucinating → RAG not more tuning; whole help center in the prompt; per-partner models) and three mistakes (fine-tuning with no evaluation set, putting permissions in the model, assuming RAG ends hallucination); the classic pairing is RAG plus a fine-tuned small model.
