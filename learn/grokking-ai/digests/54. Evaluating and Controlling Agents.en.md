# L1 overview

- A sandbox pass answers neither "can it talk to real customers?" nor "how much may it do alone?", because the sandbox covers only written cases; answering both takes measuring how the agent works and letting it earn freedom with evidence.
- The refund rule: $3 delivery fee refunded when an order is more than 20 minutes late, and past 30 minutes a 20% credit applies instead; two runs both end with one correct $3 refund, but 5 steps vs 14 steps with a second refund-tool call is a warning that output grading hides (an idempotency key blocks the second refund).
- The metric stack has four levels: task success rate (meets pre-set criteria; code checks the refund and step count, a rubric judge scores clarity, and a human anchor validates the judge), trajectory quality (steps to completion, wrong-tool rate, recovery rate — a leading indicator), safety metrics (correct refusals vs false-block rate, tracked separately, because "refuse every refund" and "approve every refund" each score perfectly on one alone, so the sandbox needs legitimate and adversarial scenarios), and online metrics (completion without takeover and cost per completed task).
- Trajectory quality moves first: a slow tool makes the agent retry, steps climb six→nine, success stays flat until tasks hit the 12-step budget and go to a person — watch only success and you learn last.
- Cost must be read with success: ~16,000 attempts a day at $0.30–$0.50 is $4,800–$8,000, and a human-handled ticket costs $4, so the agent must stay well under $4 per completed task; some agents give up cheaply, others succeed expensively.
- Levels one to three run in a sandbox harness with replica tools that fail like real ones, synthetic data that resets each run, seeded scenarios with known answers, and adversarial cases; trajectories run for real but no money moves, and the same production controls run there too — a sandbox score is evidence, not proof and often higher than first production numbers.
- A trace has four kinds of entries — reasoning steps, tool calls with inputs and results, per-step cost, and gate decisions (allow, block, or send to a person); the stated reasoning is for triage while tool results are the facts, and one trace serves incident forensics, aggregate dashboards (steps per task), and eval case harvesting — a spike in steps per task is the agent version of prediction drift, alertable per scenario category.
- The autonomy ladder earns freedom in stages per action tier: suggest (person does the work), approve (person approves the exact action), bounded auto (acts alone below a line like refunds under $10), and audited auto (acts alone with a sampled review); promotion follows a written rule (refunds under $10 reach stage 3 when sandbox success on refunds exceeds 95% and 30 days of stage-2 approvals show at least 98% agreement, checkable by anyone, working like a canary), irreversible actions always stay at stage 2, and demotion is automatic after a regression.
- Three standing controls never switch off: budget caps (12 steps, 50 cents) enforced by the runtime not the prompt, with a refund tool that counts refunds and rejects a second one whatever the model says; kill switches that stop one tier or the whole agent in seconds with no code release; and anomaly alarms per cohort (a group like one restaurant's conversations).
- The example incident: refunds under $10 sit at stage 3, a restaurant partner changes its order system so status is often "unknown", steps per task rise 6→9 over two weeks while success stays flat, the agent treats unknown as not delivered and that partner's refunds double in three days, the alarm fires, refunds drop to stage 2 automatically while credits and notes keep their stages, the team fixes the connection, failing traces become scenarios, and refunds must earn stage 3 again — the step count showed the drift two weeks before the alarm.
- Closing advice: if asked for one metric, refuse the premise and pair completion without takeover with cost per completed task.
