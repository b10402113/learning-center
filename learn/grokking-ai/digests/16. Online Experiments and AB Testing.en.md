# L1 overview

- AUC rising from 0.78 to 0.80 on last month's clicks does not justify a new restaurant ranker: offline evaluation only asks how well the model fits past clicks, while production asks whether the business gets better (more orders, on time, from returning customers).
- Offline misleads for three reasons: the label is a **proxy** (a click stands in for an order), the model changes the world it is measured in (customers could only click what the old ranker showed — a feedback loop), and serving is not scoring (offline data is clean and complete; online inputs go missing at 7pm on a busy Friday). So offline is a filter, not a verdict.
- Two gates in order: the **offline gate** (beat the incumbent on held-out data and lose in no segment), then **shadow mode** (score a copy of live requests, show nobody — catches latency and feature skew, but cannot show whether customers order more), then the **online gate**, an A/B test decided by the business metric.
- A/B mechanics: randomize by a **hash of the user ID** (not by request, or one customer sees both lists and neither group measures cleanly); write metrics down before the test (primary = order rate; guardrails like late delivery rate, with a written rule such as "don't ship if late rate rises more than 0.5 percentage points"; diagnostics explain but decide nothing); **power** the test (20% order rate vs a 20.2% worth-shipping lift may need several hundred thousand users per group); run at least a full week for day-of-week variation and for novelty effect to fade.
- Reading the test: check group sizes first (a 52/48 split in a 50-50 test signals an assignment or logging bug); do not **peak** — day 3 showed +3% and significant, day 14 showed +0.3% and not significant, and the 5% false-winner promise only holds if you judge once, so use a fixed horizon or a sequential test; keep a **holdback** group (about 2%) on the old ranker for weeks or months to measure durable effect and slow goals like 3-month repeat orders.
- Alternatives by situation: **interleaving** blends two rankers in one list with a coin flip for who picks first (needs far less traffic, compares both per customer, but measures preference not order rate) — good for filtering many candidates before an A/B test; **switchback** tests switch a whole city hour by hour so shared-courier marketplace interference does not inflate the measured difference; **multi-armed bandits** cut regret but produce hard-to-read results, so they suit short-lived choices like four banner headlines, not a permanent ranker launch. Small apps raise sensitivity instead: sharper metric (home-screen orders), longer run, bolder change.
