# L1 overview

- In a feed a slightly wrong score shows a slightly worse post; in ads the predicted click chance goes into an **auction** that picks the winner and its price, so every prediction converts straight into money — "ads the strictest design in this family."
- Auction basics with three ads: A bids $2, B bids $3, C bids $0.50, with **PCTR** (predicted click-through rate) of 2%, 1%, 4%. Ranking is by **bid × PCTR** = expected revenue per **impression**: A $0.04, B $0.03, C $0.02 — A wins even though B bid more. Under a **second-price auction** the winner pays the lowest bid that beats the runner-up's score (A needs a $1.50 bid to reach B's $0.03, so pays about $1.51 per click, not its own bid); with no runner-up a platform **reserve price** takes its place. Second price keeps bidding your true value optimal and bids stable.
- PCTR appears twice — it picks the winner and sets the price — so relative order is not enough. **AUC** checks the order (did clicked ads score higher than unclicked), **calibration** checks the numbers (about 2% of impressions scored at 2% end in a click), and **log loss** grows with both problems.
- Two miscalibration cases, neither changing the PCTR order (so AUC barely notices): A's true chance is 2% but the model says 1.6% → A still wins but its price becomes B's 3 cents / 1.6% ≈ $1.88 instead of $1.50, overcharging the advertiser; C's true chance is 4% but the model says 10% → C's score jumps to 5 cents and beats A, yet C is truly worth 2 cents versus A's 4 — users see a weaker ad and the platform earns less. "The damage appears only in the auction."
- Miscalibration hides in a **segment** (one slice of traffic, e.g. new advertisers or mobile users). The simplest check is the **calibration ratio** = sum of predicted PCTRs / real clicks (1.0 ideal): the platform may show 1.00 while new advertisers show 1.4 and mobile shows 0.8, the errors cancelling in the total — so draw a **calibration curve** per important segment, bucketing impressions by score and comparing predicted to real rate in each bucket.
- Data starts from logs: impression logs (every ad shown with its price), click events joined back to impressions, and advertiser-reported **conversions** (a valuable action after a click, e.g. a purchase). **PCVR** is the predicted conversion rate given a click. Clicks arrive in seconds, conversions often days later (click a shoe ad today, buy Saturday) — "the main data problem of ads," so a click from an hour ago is only a **provisionally negative** label until it flips, and a model trained on fresh data sees too many early negatives and predicts PCVR too low.
- Three treatments for the delay: **wait windows** (label a click negative only after a fixed window such as 7 days — far more correct labels but the newest data is a week old); **importance weighting of late positives** (train on fresh data now and add each late conversion back as a positive with weights based on how often conversions arrive late); **two-stage modeling** (one body, two heads: the PCTR head learns clicks from all impressions, the PCVR head learns conversions from clicked impressions only — 1 in 1,000 impressions converts vs 1 in 20 clicks — so only the PCVR head waits for slow labels).
- Models: the baseline PCTR is each ad's past click rate, blended with its category average for a new ad. A bigger model predicts a little better but is too slow for hundreds of ads per request, so use **distillation** — a large teacher trains offline and a small student copies it at auction speed. Raw scores are often too high or too low (one cause is **downsampling**: keeping only a share of non-clicked impressions to save compute), so ship a **calibration layer** after the score: **Platt scaling** (smooth S-shaped curve) or **isotonic regression** (step-shaped mapping that never goes down), one per model version.
- Serving path: **eligibility** first (~10 ms) — a hard filter on audience (**targeting**) and remaining budget, plus policy review failures, because these rules are contracts (an advertiser targeting Canada must not pay for a Mexican impression), cutting 5 million active ads to a few thousand; then **retrieval** (a cheap relevance model) keeps ~500; features are fetched, the student predicts, and calibration corrects — about 55 ms, inside the 50–100 ms target.
- **Budget pacing**: eligibility alone lets a strong ad with a thousand-dollar daily budget win almost every auction and spend it all by 9am, missing every evening user. The pacing service compares real spend with a planned curve and lowers the bid for fast spenders, raises it for slow ones. Two arrows leave serving besides the winning ad: the **event stream** (impressions, clicks, conversions, keeping counters and the model fresh) and the **pacing loop** (prices charged → campaign spend → budget state). If either arrow breaks, the next auctions run on old information.
- New campaigns start every minute, so the standard answer is **near-online learning**: the PCTR model updates every few minutes to every hour, and before each push the new version is tested on the latest held-out minutes — log loss must not get worse and the calibration ratio must stay near 1.0 per segment.
- **Cold start**: a brand-new campaign has no clicks, and if its guess is too low it never wins and never collects data. Start from **priors** (estimates from related data, e.g. the category average), then **capped exploration** (give the ad some impressions it would not win alone, with a per-day cap on what that costs).
- Capacity and failure: at 100,000 requests/s × 500 ads each = 50 million predictions/s; eligibility-first and the small student keep it affordable, and "the biggest cost is a wrong probability, not hardware." If the PCTR service degrades, the auction keeps running on warm fallbacks (last-known good model, then segment averages); if eligibility is down, **fail closed** and serve no paid ad — "a lost impression costs some revenue, but an ineligible impression breaks a contract."
