# L1 overview

- Capstone: design **People You May Know** for a professional network of ~900 million members — pick about 10 people out of 900 million for Anna's homepage. It is a recommendation system (candidate generation → ranker → rule layer, the same **funnel**), but "the hard part is what each stage must respect when the items are people."
- Two things make it harder than suggesting videos. The goal has **two sides**: a video has no opinion about who watches it, but a person does, so a suggestion is good only if Anna sends an **invite** (a request to connect) and the other member **accepts**. And a bad suggestion can be a **privacy incident** — if two patients of one clinic both uploaded the clinic's phone number, suggesting them to each other reveals something about their health. This is the **social cost asymmetry**: wrong suggestions cost far more when the item is a person, so privacy rules must hold even when other parts of the system fail.
- Don't optimize **acceptance rate** alone — it is a trap, because the system can raise it with easy reconnections (an old classmate who accepts out of politeness) that never send a message. Use three layers of metrics: the **primary** metric is accepted invites per member who sees the module (decides most tests); **guardrails** stop a launch if suggestions annoy people (the receiver's **ignore rate** and **report rate**, the viewer's **dismiss rate**); the **North Star** is the real goal, **network value** — messages or job referrals in the following weeks — which exposes the easy reconnections the primary metric still counts.
- Candidate sources, modeled as a **connection graph** (members are points, connections are **edges**; its shape is the **topology**, the strongest signal). Four sources: **triangle closing** (a.k.a. friends of friends — Anna and Chen share Ben and Eve, so suggest Chen to close the triangle; but a shared contact with 30,000 connections says little since almost everyone knows them); **imported contacts** (an uploaded address book with consent — highest precision, but the people in it never agreed, so never reveal that a number was uploaded and never suggest two members only because both uploaded the same number, which is the rule that stops the clinic suggestion); **affiliations** (shared company or school — works before a member has connections but weak alone); and **embeddings** (lists of numbers describing each member; similar members get similar numbers and it finds people the other sources miss). Together the four find about 500 candidates.
- Labels are the outcomes of past suggestions; an invite may be accepted days later, so a label waits a fixed **window** (e.g. 14 days). The simplest baseline ranks by number of mutual connections. The learned ranker predicts two chances — **p send** (chance Anna invites) and **p accept** (chance the candidate accepts) — and the score is the **product**. Anna is three times more likely to invite a well-known CEO, but the CEO rarely accepts strangers, so a former teammate scores 0.06 versus the executive's 0.015 and ranks first. Because the chances multiply, both must be **calibrated** to real rates.
- The **rule layer** applies three rules after ranking: **impression discounting** (lower a suggestion's score each time it is shown and ignored — after three ignores it stops appearing for good), **exposure caps** (limit how often any one member is suggested per day), and **source variety** (mix sources so the list is not all one company). A **privacy filter** — a step that removes members who must not be suggested — runs **before** the ranker, so an excluded member is gone before anyone ranks or stores a list.
- Triangle closing over 900 million members is too heavy to run while Anna waits, so the design **pre-computes**: a nightly batch job writes each active member's top 200 candidates into a **KV store** (key-value store, finding a stored value by key, here Anna's member ID), and a **streaming job** runs all day so when Anna dismisses Dana, Dana is removed within seconds. A brand-new member gets a starter list from contacts and affiliations. Anna's request then only reads one list, checks it, and shapes it — about **60 ms**, inside the 200 ms target.
- The privacy filter must run **at retrieval** — in the nightly job, before ranking, and on each request — not just at display time: if it ran only at display, excluded members would still sit in stored lists and logs and nobody could prove they never appeared. So it **logs every exclusion** and a nightly check scans every stored list, together proving to a legal reviewer that a member with suggestions turned off never appeared.
- Failure behavior is deliberately opposite: the nightly batch **fails soft** (if tonight's job fails, serve yesterday's lists, which are still useful), while the privacy filter **fails closed** (if it takes longer than its 30 ms timeout, hide the whole module) — an empty module costs a few invites for a few minutes, an unfiltered list could cause an irreversible privacy incident. Decide this policy ahead of time, not during an outage.
- The nightly job must be made tractable: a member with 500 connections each with 500 of their own has 250,000 two-step paths, so it uses three limits — start from only the member's **100 strongest connections** (250,000 → 50,000 paths), compute lists only for members active in the last **30 days**, and through a member with 30,000 connections follow only a **random sample** (e.g. 500). It is **idempotent** (running twice equals running once), stores each list under member ID and run date, and reads a fixed copy of the graph so a retried run never corrupts the lists.
- Because each accepted suggestion adds an edge, the system changes the graph it learns from — two traps. **Rich get richer**: well-connected members appear in many lists, gain connections, and appear even more, while new members stay hard to find (exposure caps and honest **exploration** — a small approved share of slots for under-connected members — help). **Segregation amplification**: triangle closing keeps suggesting people inside the same clusters (a school, a city), so the network can split into closed groups — watch the **cross-cluster rate** (share of new connections linking two clusters). In the A/B test, guardrail both senders and receivers.
- Cost sits in the pipeline, not serving: serving is about 1,700 KV reads per second, but the nightly graph job follows about 50,000 paths for each of 300 million members — roughly **15 trillion steps every night**. In the interview, name the social cost asymmetry before being asked and say the cost sits in the nightly graph pipeline, not in serving.
