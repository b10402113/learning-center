# L1 overview

- **Batch inference** computes predictions before anyone asks (a 2 a.m. job scores each customer's 500 likely dishes into a key-value store keyed by customer ID; the 7 p.m. lookup takes ~1 ms). **Online inference** computes while the user waits (checkout fetches features, runs the model, answers in ~10–100 ms). Choosing a mode also chooses when servers do the heavy work.
- Two things that are not batch: **batching** (grouping waiting requests into one model call — users still wait, so it is online) and a **cache** (stores an answer only after the first request computes it). The test for batch is timing: the answer exists before the request does.
- One deciding question: does the prediction depend on **request-time information**? If yes, it must run online (last night's job cannot use info that did not exist). If no, batch works when a slightly old answer is fine and the input list is known — dishes shelf in batch, ETA and any payment fraud score always online (the payment does not exist until it is made).
- Cost and freshness trade-offs: batch cost is predictable and can run **off-peak**; online cost grows with traffic and peaks at dinner. A batch answer is as old as the last run and scores users who never come — the main weakness. On 25 million customers × 500 candidates = 12.5 billion rows a night, 80% of rows may never be read. Three waste fixes: precompute only for customers active in the last seven days (5 million → 2.5 billion rows, one-fifth), shorten lists (20 shown; if 200 works as well as 500, rows drop by more than half), and use coarser **segments** (10,000 segments → 5 million rows, at the cost of personalization).
- Pick the **cadence** (how often the job runs) by testing the target metric — here the share of views that lead to an order — comparing lists 2 hours, 12 hours, and 3 days old; if the share stays flat, nightly or weekly is enough; running more often multiplies cost, so moving work online is often cheaper.
- The **precompute-then-re-rank hybrid** fixes staleness: at 2 a.m. store 500 candidates per active customer; at ~1 ms lookup, fetch live context (location, time of day, open restaurants, kitchen queues), and a light **re-ranker** scores the 500 and returns the top 20 after a simple closed-restaurant rule. Offline answers what the customer likes; online answers what makes sense now — near-online freshness at near-batch cost, versus a fully online shelf that would score millions per request.
- **Streaming inference** handles event-driven work with no waiting user (a restaurant uploads a menu photo; a check for blur or unsafe content). It is online inference with a **queue** in front of **scoring workers**; the queue absorbs bursts (a chain uploading 3,000 photos). A payment-holding fraud check stays online because a user waits.
- Degraded modes: batch **fails soft** (yesterday's predictions keep serving; never serve a half-written run, so write a new version and switch on completion; alert on row age after e.g. three days); online needs a planned fallback (previous model, cached estimate, simple average); hybrid falls back to its stored list; streaming alerts on the age of the oldest event and moves repeatedly failing events to a **dead letter queue**.
