# L1 overview

- **Offline evaluation** is the cheap first check — scoring a model on a held-back **test set** of recent past examples whose real outcomes are known. A **metric** is one number summarizing how good the predictions are, and it has one job: decide which candidates deserve an online experiment (an A-B test), never which one launches.
- Two facts decide the metric: the **problem family** (classification → yes/no like fraud; ranking → an ordered list like search and home screen; regression → minutes like delivery time) and the **base rate** (how common the positive is). At a 0.2% base rate, accuracy misleads: always saying "legitimate" scores 99.8% and catches nothing.
- Worked fraud day: 1 million payments, 2,000 fraud; the model flags 2,500 with 1,500 truly fraud → **precision** 60% (1,500/2,500), **recall** 75% (1,500/2,000), the 1,000 wrong flags are **false positives**, the 500 misses **false negatives**. The model really outputs a score; the **threshold** is the cutoff, and moving it trades precision against recall (0.9 → 90%/45%, 0.2 → 30%/90%) without changing the model — a business choice, so the risk team sets it (a missed fraud ~$80 vs. a blocked customer ~$5 favors a lower threshold).
- Because the threshold may change, one number must cover every threshold: the **precision-recall curve** and its **PR-AUC** (0 to 1). **ROC-AUC** plots recall against the **false positive rate**, which millions of easy good payments make look tiny (0.1% at a 0.5 threshold even though 40% of flags were mistakes), so ROC-AUC looks strong — **report PR-AUC when positives are rare** and always attach the base rate (60% precision is strong at 0.2%, barely better than guessing at 50%).
- Ranking is two stages. **Candidate generation/retrieval** picks a few hundred from a large catalog (500 restaurants out of 20,000); **recall at K** is the share of items the user really chose that land in the top K (3 of 4 → 75% at K=500), and order inside the K does not matter because stage two never sees anything outside it. Stage two sets the order: **NDCG** (**Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain**) grades each result 0–3 and discounts by position (slot 1 counts ~3.5× slot 10), normalized so 1 is ideal; **MRR** (**Mean Reciprocal Rank**) averages 1/position of the single right answer for **navigation searches**.
- Ranking labels come from **judged sets** (trained raters with guidelines: clean and consistent but slow and expensive) or **behavioral labels** (free and plentiful but biased — users can only click what was shown, and click the top slots more, **position bias**), so many teams use clicks for volume and a judged set to check they are not misleading.
- Regression: **MAE** is the average absolute miss; **RMSE** squares errors, averages, then roots — two models can share MAE 4 while one has RMSE 7.2, so pick RMSE when large misses hurt more. Report **P90 error** alongside the average (a P90 of 12 minutes means about 1 order in 10 misses by more) and error **per segment**, or one useless new city hides among nine good ones.
- **Calibration** means scores match real frequencies (of payments scored ~0.10, about 10% should be fraud). PR-AUC and ROC-AUC check only order — halving every score leaves both unchanged — so a model can rank well and be badly calibrated, which matters when the score is used as a probability (the fraud team reviews when score × amount exceeds $10). Fix with a small mapping fitted after training: **Platt scaling** (a smooth S-curve) or **isotonic regression** (a step shape that never goes down).
- Every number needs two **baselines**: the **naive baseline** (the simplest guess — yesterday's average misses by 6.2 minutes, so a 6.0-minute model gains just 12 seconds) asks whether the problem needs a model at all; the **incumbent** (the model in production today) asks whether the switch is worth it. Compare both on the same test set and segments; a gain in only one segment is not ready.
- The offline check only sends models forward. When offline improves but the A-B test is flat, the metric is a **proxy** — a measurable stand-in for the real goal (clicks standing in for orders, until a new ranking model raises NDCG on clicks while orders do not move); fix by grading results by orders. A strong report names the metric and base rate, the baselines, the results for important groups, then lets the online experiment decide.
