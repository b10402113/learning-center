# L1 overview

- Classic ML design questions repeat, and their designs share most parts, so the job is to recognize the question and adapt a known design — not to invent one. A weak answer starts with a model name; a strong answer starts with the **scale and the time limit**, because those two set how much work the system can spend per item.
- Find the question's **shape**, which depends on what arrives with each request. **Ranking** chooses a few items out of many (one home screen request gets ~20 items from thousands or millions); **judging** decides about one item (a payment, a post). The shape tells you which known design to start from.
- Ranking uses a **funnel** — a sequence of stages, each handling fewer items so it can spend more work per item: stage 1 **candidate generation** (a.k.a. retrieval) cheaply cuts millions to a few hundred; stage 2 the **ranker** orders those; stage 3 the **rule layer** adds business rules (variety, freshness) outside the model so a policy change needs no retrain.
- Each stage has its own metric, and the failures are not equal: retrieval is judged on **recall** (share of good items kept), ranking on **precision at the top**. A good item dropped in retrieval is gone forever; one ranked 40th can be fixed by a better ranker. So a retrieval miss is final, a low rank is not.
- Judging shape: a payment may need a decision in about 50 ms — cheap checks catch clear cases (a card on a block list), then a model scores risk, then an **action policy** applies **thresholds** set by the cost of each mistake. Actions can be richer than yes/no: approve, block, extra verification, hold for human review. Harmful content stretches this into a chain where each later step sees fewer items.
- Worked video-platform example at 50 million videos, ~20 on screen, ~200 ms: three cheap retrieval sources in parallel (ANN index over embeddings, co-occurrence with recently watched videos, trending/new) merge to ~500 candidates by ~35 ms; the ranker scores all 500 in one batched call with richer features and keeps 50 by ~80 ms; the rule layer stops one channel filling the screen, leaving ~20 by ~85 ms — the ranker saw 0.001% of the catalog. Impressions are logged because without that log nobody can train on what the user actually saw; labels carry **position bias** (top items get more clicks partly because of where they appear).
- Each of the six designs adds exactly one new requirement to the ranking shape, and that is where interview time goes: recommendation adds cold start and position bias; feed ranking adds many goals at once (one multitask model plus a weighted formula of positives minus negatives); search ranking adds a typed query (query understanding, lexical + semantic retrieval); ad click prediction adds a price (bid × predicted click chance, so **calibration** matters); fraud detection adds an **adversary** and rare positives (99.8% accuracy catches nothing at a 0.2% fraud rate, and labels arrive as chargebacks weeks later); harmful content adds scale plus harm in two directions with no simple dollar value, so policy teams set thresholds. The capstone (People You May Know) adds a two-sided goal plus privacy that must hold even when parts fail.
- Six recurring mistakes: designing from scratch (job search is search ranking with a second side), running one rich model over every item, judging every stage by one number, treating clicks as the truth (name position bias before being asked), making every decision yes/no, and quoting accuracy on rare events (quote precision and recall at your thresholds instead).
