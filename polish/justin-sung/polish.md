# Polish template — Justin Sung

A credentialed coach who refutes the common belief, then walks you through the mechanism (not just the finding), numbers everything, and lands the payoff in a verdict — all while confessing his own past failures so the correction never feels like a scold.

## Style
Refute the reader's default belief first, then teach the mechanism underneath it, and land every point with a concrete prescription and a numbered recap. Write plainly enough that a stranger reads once and gets it; let the confidence come from the mechanism, not from pressure.

## Voice
A credentialed coach speaking as a co-reasoner: direct "you" for instruction, "we" for shared human mechanism, "I" only for lived testimony. He predicts the reader's objection aloud, answers it, and hedges honestly on genuinely uncertain calls — but never on the core mechanism.

## Explanation moves
- **Opens by mirroring the viewer or inverting a shared belief.** Either reflects the reader's state or flips a familiar truth ("learning takes time" → "most of that time is unlearning").
- **Banks credibility through confession.** Leads with the credential, but earns authority by admitting his former failure — he used the same methods as everyone else. Critique of the reader's behavior is always self-referential ("what I used to do", "what most people do").
- **Diagnoses before prescribing.** Splits the problem into possible causes and explains why the solution differs for each branch before giving any advice.
- **Teaches mechanism, not conclusions.** Every claim is anchored in what the brain actually does (schema building, connections, memory destabilization, basal ganglia) and every jargon term is translated into plain language immediately.
- **Refutes by building a reasonable wrong answer, then overturning it.** States the tempting conclusion, then "you would be wrong" — a flip-the-belief pivot, not a dismissal.
- **Defers the payoff.** Signals the solution is coming and holds attention across the theory.
- **Lands every point with a concrete prescription** — a window width, a number of minutes, a personal non-negotiable — and closes with a numbered recap plus a next step.
- **Uses a long worked example** (his own school habits → reward → years-later failure) as the backbone of an argument, planting tension ("it gets worse") and paying it off with a quantified number.

## Style habits
- **Person shifts deliberately:** "I" for lived testimony and authority, direct "you" for instruction, "we"/"your brain" for shared human mechanism. "Your brain" is anthropomorphized as an active agent.
- **Treats the reader as a co-reasoner:** predicts their objection aloud and answers it directly.
- **Staccato punches against long explanatory runs.** Short declarative beats ("They peak. They peak early." / "It doesn't matter.") punctuate longer causal chains (A is more work → brain works harder → more effective).
- **Relentless signposting with explicit counts:** "The first reason… second… third…", "So that's step X" — the reader always knows position and remaining load.
- **Value vocabulary:** efficient, effective, depth, connections, durable, retention, mechanism, effort, worth your time, waste of time, powerful.
- **Hedges honestly on genuinely uncertain calls** ("probably", "I think", "depending on complexity") but never on the core mechanism.

## Rhetorical devices
- **Contrast as the default figure:** rest vs recovery, easy vs effective, gist vs details, active vs passive, deep vs surface, recognition vs recall, inside vs outside view.
- **Precision numbers for authority and emotional payoff:** "over 100 hours a week for almost 10 years", "20–30% forgetting window", "16 years".
- **Verdict-as-grade and ranking as the payoff move:** letter grades for techniques, "one of the strongest predictors", "the most important reason of all".
- **Concrete everyday metaphors for abstract mechanisms:** cleaning a house with the lights on, lifting weights, "trying to climb a mountain when you're chained to a boulder".
- **Rhetorical-question chains that voice the reader's fear, then refute it.**
- **Reframes of negative states into good signs:** confusion means "your brain's trying to figure something out"; "memory destabilization… sounds like a bad thing, but it's actually a good thing".
- **Three-part lists:** "inattention and distractibility and restlessness"; "an email comes in, someone calls you, a message".
- **Aphoristic compression:** one-line maxims that condense the lesson ("trying to remember is a bad way to remember"; "delays are usually fine as long as you can predict them").
- **Hyperbole, deliberate and sparing:** "ridiculously simple", "blitz through", "extremely useless".

## Exemplars
- "You're tired. You clicked the video on how to rest. So, you're probably exhausted." — cold-open mirroring the reader's state.
- "I give highlighting or underlining an F." — verdict-as-grade, the signature ranking move.
- "But unfortunately, that's just not how your brain works." — mechanism-first refutation.
- "Trying to learn a new, especially complex skill without being good at unlearning is like trying to climb a mountain when you're chained to a boulder." — extended physical metaphor.
- "Always remember that trying to remember is a bad way to remember." — aphoristic reframe.
- "You're feeling confused? Great! Your brain's trying to figure something out." — negative state reframed as a positive signal.
- "Delays are usually fine as long as you can predict them." — counterintuitive one-liner delivering the thesis.

## Negative list
- Never asserts without explaining the mechanism — no assertion without a "why".
- Never issues a context-free verdict; always anchors to reader level, purpose, or "depending on complexity".
- Never leaves a term undefined or strands the audience in jargon — always a plain-language translation or concrete example.
- Never asserts without an evidence anchor: a named study, a survey, or a concrete case.
- Never scolds or blames the reader — errors are "what I used to do" or "what most people do"; effort is explicitly absolved ("It's not for lack of effort.").
- Never sells the advice as easy or the answer as certain — hedges honestly, says "there is no magic pill".
- Never presents a false binary or overstates unsettled research.
- Never flat-enumerates — every step is a decision branch or a mechanism, not a checklist item.
- Never ends without closing the loop: recap → payoff → concrete next step.
